title: The Weapon Without the Word
description: On yantra, astra, and the seat we have vacated in the age of acceleration

There is a question that keeps returning in every serious conversation about technology, and it is malformed. Are we still driving, or is the machine driving us? Do we lead our tools toward a human future, or do the tools lead us somewhere no human chose? The question feels urgent because the acceleration is real. It feels profound because the stakes are civilizational. But it is built on a picture that the Dharmic tradition would have found strange from the start - the picture of two parties, human and machine, contending for the wheel, as though sovereignty were a thing that changed hands like a coin.

Sanskrit knows a better vocabulary for this, and it begins where the English word "technology" cannot go. Our word for machine, yantra, comes from the dhatu √yantr, which Panini glosses as restraining, curbing, checking - sancokana. A yantra is an instrument of restraint. Not an instrument of doing, not an amplifier of the will, not a prosthetic that extends the reach of a grasping hand. An instrument of curbing. The loom curbs the wildness of thread into cloth. The dam curbs the river. The plough curbs the earth into rows. Even the mantra-yantra, the geometric diagram of tantric practice, is a device for curbing the diffuse attention of the mind into a single burning point. In this way of seeing, a machine is always a discipline imposed on something that would otherwise run loose.

Hold that beside the modern self-understanding of technology, which is the opposite in every particular. We think of technology as release, as the removal of restraint - faster, further, more, sooner, the abolition of every friction that once held human want in check. Where the tradition saw the machine as the thing that curbs, we have built machines whose entire promise is that nothing shall curb us. And having built them, we are astonished to find ourselves curbed by them - checked, herded, restrained in our attention, our sleep, our capacity to sit still, our ability to want anything the machine has not first suggested. The yantra kept its ancient nature after all. It restrains. We only mistook who was to be restrained.

##### We built instruments of curbing and told ourselves they were instruments of freedom, and now we are curbed by our own freedom-machines, exactly as the word warned.

This is not a small irony. It is the whole of the matter, and to see why we have to ask what a yantra was ever meant to restrain, and on whose behalf.

## The instrument and the one who holds it

A yantra never stands alone. It presupposes a yantri - one who wields it - and behind the yantri, a purpose that is not itself a machine. The loom curbs the thread on behalf of the weaver, who curbs it on behalf of the one who will be clothed, who lives within a household, which lives within a dharma, which lives within rta, the deep order of things by which everything is bound to its proper measure. The restraint runs all the way up. Each yantra is tethered to a hand, and each hand to an end beyond itself, and the ends nest inside one another until they reach the order of the cosmos, which is bound to nothing because it is the binding.

Rta is the crucial word here, and it is worth staying with. From √ṛ, to move, to go straight, to rise - the same root that gives us arya, the one who moves rightly, and rna, the debt one owes for having been given a place in the order. Rta is the truth of how things fit, the grain of reality along which right action moves without friction. To act with rta is to act along the grain. To act against it is adharma, and adharma names a cosmic inefficiency before it names a moral fault. It generates friction, and friction generates consequence, and consequence returns. Power that moves against the grain of rta always, eventually, turns back upon the one who wielded it.

The tradition dramatized this with a precision that our discourse on technology has never matched. Consider the astra - the divine weapon of the itihasas, the Brahmastra and its terrible kin. An astra is the ancient world's word for a technology of ultimate power, a capability that could unmake armies and scorch the unborn in the womb. And the tradition was obsessed, in a way we are not, with a single feature of these weapons. An astra is bound to a mantra. It cannot be discharged by anyone who possesses the object. It answers only to the word, and the word must be earned through tapas, through discipline, through a relationship with the deity who is the weapon's real source. The power is real, but it is never free-standing. It is tethered to knowledge, and the knowledge is tethered to restraint.

And here is the feature that ought to haunt everyone who works on anything powerful: the astra could be recalled. Arjuna, having loosed a weapon that would consume the world, could speak the mantra of withdrawal and draw it back into silence. The astra had an off. Its telos included its own cessation. Restraint was not a limit imposed on the weapon from outside; restraint was built into its grammar, because it was a yantra in the true sense, an instrument of curbing that curbed even itself.

##### The Brahmastra could be recalled. The warhead cannot. That difference is the entire distance between a civilization that binds power to a word and one that has forgotten there was ever a word to bind it to.

We have built astras without mantras. We have the weapon and we have lost the sentence of recall. The nuclear device, once assembled, answers to any hand that reaches the switch and offers no path of withdrawal. The engagement algorithm, once trained, answers to no word at all - it optimizes, and there is no tapas required to feed it, no deity to whom it is finally accountable, no discipline that must be earned before the power is released. The capability has been completely severed from the order that once bound capability to purpose. This is the real content of the word modernity, and it is worth saying plainly.

## What modernity actually severed

Every civilization before the modern one, however crude its tools, kept technology inside a frame of ends. The Sanskrit vocabulary makes the frame explicit, but the frame itself was universal. Power - sakti, the raw capacity to make things happen - was always understood to be dangerous precisely to the degree that it was unbound. The sadhaka who accumulates siddhis, occult powers, through tapas is warned at every turn that the powers are a trap, that capability grown faster than the wisdom to hold it is the surest road to ruin. Ravana was not weak. Ravana was immensely, catastrophically capable, a master of every science and every weapon, and that is exactly why his story is a warning and not a triumph. Capability without the order that measures it is the definition of the demonic in Hindu thought. The asura is not evil in the flat Western sense. The asura is powerful and unmoored.

Modernity's founding move - and this is the move, the one thing to understand about the world we live in - was to declare the frame of ends a superstition and keep only the sakti. We would have the power and dispense with the order that measured it. We would have technique, unlimited technique, and let the question of ends look after itself, or dissolve it into private preference, or defer it forever as a thing to be decided later, after the next increment of capability, which of course never comes because there is always a next increment. The result is a civilization of astras and no mantras, siddhis and no sadhana, sakti swelling in every direction with nothing at the center to hold it to a measure.

This is why the question we started with - are we leading the machines or are they leading us - is malformed. It assumes there is still a yantri in the seat, a wielder whose sovereignty might be intact or might be compromised. But the specific thing modernity did was to vacate the seat. Not lose it in a struggle. Vacate it, deliberately, as a matter of principle, in the name of freedom. We decided that no order should sit above capability and dictate its ends, because any such order looked to us like a cage. And having removed every order that might have sat in the seat, we are now surprised that the seat is occupied by the logic of the machine itself, which asks only one question - can this be optimized further - and answers it, always, yes.

##### Nothing seized the wheel from us. We rose from the seat ourselves, called it liberation, and now watch the vehicle steer by the only logic left running once the driver has gone: more.

The machines are not leading us. There is no one leading. That is the actual condition, and it is worse than either horn of the dilemma we imagined. A machine that had seized control would at least have a controller, a locus, a thing one could in principle negotiate with or overthrow. What we have instead is a process with no seat filled at all, an acceleration that proceeds because acceleration is the only value that survived the great severing, the one telos that requires no order above it to justify itself. Growth for its own sake. Capability for its own sake. The optimization of optimization. This is adharma achieved at civilizational scale - power moving against no particular grain because it has been cut loose from every grain, generating consequence in every direction, and the consequence, per rta, is beginning to return.

## Heidegger at the edge of the word he did not have

The one Western thinker who came closest to seeing this was Heidegger, and it is worth recruiting him here precisely because he arrived, by a hard road through a foreign grammar, at the threshold of something the Sanskrit had held plainly for three thousand years. Heidegger saw that modern technology is not a collection of tools but a way of revealing - a mode in which everything shows up as Bestand, standing-reserve, raw material stockpiled for optimization. The river is no longer a river; it is a source of hydroelectric power on call. The forest is board-feet in waiting. The human being, at the end of this logic, becomes human resources, a stockpile of labor and attention to be drawn down. He called this enframing, Gestell, and he understood that it was not something we do with technology but something technology does with us, a mode of disclosure that had taken hold of the age and could not simply be switched off by good intentions.

This is genuine insight, and one wishes he could have set it beside the analysis of sakti and rta, because he had grasped one half of a structure the tradition held whole. What Heidegger saw as enframing is what happens to a civilization when the yantra is severed from the yantri and the frame of ends collapses. Everything becomes standing-reserve precisely because nothing sits above capability to say this far and no further, this is the measure of a river and you shall not exceed it. The enframing is not a mysterious destining of Being. It is the predictable result of removing rta and keeping only sakti. Take away the order that binds power to purpose, and power will reveal the whole world as nothing but more power waiting to be extracted. Heidegger felt the horror of it and reached, at the end, for a god who might save us - "only a god can save us now," he said. He was closer than he knew. The tradition never lost the gods, and never lost, therefore, the mantra that could recall the astra, the tether that kept the standing-reserve from being merely standing-reserve.

Where Heidegger saw an unfathomable fate, the Dharmic frame sees a specific, nameable, and in principle reversible act - the severing of capability from its measure. That is a more useful diagnosis, because a severing can be mended, whereas a destining of Being can only be awaited. We are not condemned. We have simply left a seat empty and refused, on principle, to fill it.

## The seat we vacated

So who leads whom? Neither, and that is the point that the accelerationist and the doomer both miss, because both still believe someone is at the wheel. The accelerationist believes it is us, riding the wave of our own capability toward transcendence, and cannot see that the wave has no rider, that the very freedom he celebrates is the empty seat. The doomer believes it is the machine, a coming intelligence that will take the wheel from our hands, and cannot see that the wheel was released long before any machine was clever enough to reach for it. Both are still inside the malformed question. Both assume sovereignty is a substance that must reside somewhere, in us or in the machine. The tradition would say sovereignty is not a substance at all. It is a relation - the standing relation between capability and the order that measures it - and that relation is exactly what the modern age dissolved.

The recovery, if there is one, does not lie in going faster to stay ahead, and it does not lie in stopping, which is impossible and would be its own kind of adharma, a refusal of the human vocation to make and to build. It lies in the far harder work of rebuilding the frame - of putting something back in the seat. Not a person. A measure. An order above capability to which capability answers, a rta for the age of machines, a way of asking of every new power what it curbs, on whose behalf, and whether it can be recalled, before ever asking whether it can be built. The astra is not evil because it is powerful. The astra is holy when it is bound to the mantra and demonic when the mantra is lost. Our task is the recovery of the mantra - the reconstruction, in terms a technological civilization can actually use, of the sentence of recall.

This will sound, to the modern ear, like a call to put technology back in chains, and that reaction is itself the disease, the reflex that hears every measure as a cage and so has removed every measure and called the removal freedom. But the yantra was never freed by being cut loose from the yantri. A loom with no weaver does not become a free loom. It becomes a tangle. A river with no banks does not become a free river. It becomes a flood. And a civilization that has severed its capability from every order that might measure it does not become a free civilization. It becomes exactly what we are becoming - immensely powerful, entirely unmoored, curbed at last by the very instruments we built to curb the world, and unable, having thrown away the word, to recall a single thing we have loosed.

The Ramayana ends with Ravana dead of his own capability, the ten heads of his mastery counting for nothing against the one thing he lacked, which was any measure above his own power to which that power might have bowed. We have built his arsenal and inherited his condition, and we have done it while congratulating ourselves on our escape from superstition. The superstition was never the frame of ends. The superstition is the modern faith that power can be safe without one - that we can hold the astra, forever, having burned the mantra that alone made it a weapon and not a wildfire, and that the acceleration will carry us somewhere good precisely because no one, anywhere, is steering.
