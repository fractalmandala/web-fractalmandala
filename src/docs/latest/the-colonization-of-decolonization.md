## The Bronze Comes Home

Over the past decade, a procession of Chola bronzes has been making its way home. The National Gallery of Australia returned the Sripuranthan Nataraja after the Kapoor smuggling network unravelled. The Manhattan District Attorney's office, of all institutions, has become one of the great repatriators of Indian sacred art, seizing and returning hundreds of idols that passed through the antiquities trade. There are ceremonies. A minister receives the deity. Photographs are taken. Everyone agrees that something wrong has been put right.

Look closer at what returns. In the museum catalogue, the Nataraja was "bronze figure of Shiva as Lord of the Dance, Chola dynasty, 11th century." An object. A masterpiece, certainly - the curators were never stingy with aesthetic praise. But an object: provenance, patina, exhibition history. What was taken from the temple in Sripuranthan was none of these things. It was a deity in whom generations had invested prana through consecration and daily worship, an utsava murti that processed through the village streets, a presence around whom a community's calendar turned. The theft converted a deity into an artwork. The repatriation returned the artwork. The conversion itself - the frame that turned presence into property - stayed in the museum, and stays in us.

This is decolonization as currently practiced: the return of objects with the frames intact. And it is the small, visible instance of a much larger operation, one that has gone almost entirely unremarked. The word "decolonization" has itself been colonized.

## The Capture of the Word

The word once named something total. For Fanon, decolonization was the complete replacement of one order by another, a remaking of the colonized down to the level of the person. Ngugi wa Thiong'o, who died only last year, spent his life insisting that the battlefield was the mind and the weapon was language - that a people who tell their own story in the conqueror's tongue, through the conqueror's categories, remain conquered no matter what flag flies over the parliament.

What happened to this word in the decades since is instructive. It was taken up by the Western academy, processed through the machinery of critical theory, and re-issued as a genre. "Decoloniality" now names a discourse with its own canon - Quijano's coloniality of power, Mignolo's border thinking - its own journals, its own conference circuit, its own career track. It is conducted in English and French, in the theoretical dialect of Paris circa 1975, in institutions the formerly colonized mostly cannot afford to attend. Its liturgical form is the land acknowledgment: a statement, read aloud before proceedings begin, that the ground beneath the seminar belongs to someone else, followed by the seminar.

I do not doubt the sincerity of many people inside this discourse. Sincerity is what makes it work. But notice its structural signature: the colonized appear in it, once again, as objects. Their dispossession is theorized, their trauma catalogued, their artifacts processed for return. What never appears is their knowledge - as knowledge. The decolonial theorist can tell you, in exhaustive detail, what was taken: land, labor, loot, the forty-five trillion dollars Utsa Patnaik has computed as the drain from India between 1765 and 1938. These are categories the West possesses - property, surplus, extraction - and so the theft can be named in them. Boaventura de Sousa Santos coined "epistemicide" for the deeper killing, and the coinage has circulated widely, which tells you the phenomenon has been noticed. But noticing that knowledges were destroyed is one thing. Being able to state what those knowledges claimed, in their own terms, and taking the claims seriously as claims - that is another thing entirely, and the discourse cannot do it.

Ask the decolonial seminar what a pramana is. Ask what itihasa is as a genre - what kind of truth-claim a civilization makes when it calls something "thus indeed it was." Ask what the Veda understands itself to be, what shruti means as a category of knowledge rather than as an ethnographic curiosity. Silence - or worse, a reflexive suspicion that the question itself smuggles in nationalism. You cannot return what you have no word for.

##### A decolonization that can inventory every stolen object while remaining unable to state a single claim of the stolen knowledge system has not decolonized anything. It has re-described the loot in the accountancy of the looter.

## What Was Actually Taken

To see why this matters for India specifically, recall what colonization actually did to us. The wealth extraction was real and staggering, but it was the shallower of the two operations. The deeper one was a systematic reclassification of everything we knew into categories in which it could only appear as a defective version of something European.

Macaulay's Minute of 1835 is the famous document, with its shelf of a good European library outweighing the whole native literature of India and Arabia. But the Minute was only the budget line. The real work was taxonomic. Itihasa - the civilization's memory of itself, transmitted with its own critical apparatus - was reclassified as "mythology," a word that in English means beautiful falsehood. Darshana, which means seeing, and which names disciplines for the transformation of the knower, was reclassified as "Indian philosophy" and shelved as a regional variant of a Greek activity. The Veda became a "text," to be dated, stratified, and mined for evidence of tribal migrations, its rishis demoted to bards. Varna, a fourfold vocational-spiritual principle, was fused with jati, run through the race science of the day - Risley taking nasal measurements for the 1901 census - and issued back to us as "the caste system," a phrase in which most Indians now sincerely believe their ancestors lived. And the whole integrated fabric of Veda, Agama, Purana, temple, and practice was cut to Abrahamic pattern and issued a name: Hinduism, a "religion" now embarrassed to be missing its founder, its single book, its creed.

Each reclassification was a small conquest, more durable than any garrison. And here the fractal logic that governs all civilizational life applies with full force. What an implanted memory is to a person, an implanted history is to a civilization. A person carrying implanted memories does not experience himself as deceived; he experiences himself as remembering. He acts against his own interest with a clear conscience, because the falsification sits beneath the level at which conscience operates. S.N. Balagangadhara gave this condition its precise name: colonial consciousness - the state in which the colonized can describe himself and his culture only in the descriptions of the colonizer, and then, crucially, experiences his own tradition through those descriptions. Ashis Nandy mapped the same territory as the intimate enemy. The English-educated Indian - and I write as one, with empathy for the condition rather than contempt, having lived inside it - learns his tradition as the anthropology of himself. He encounters the Gita first as a set text about which certain critical things are said, the temple as heritage, the puja his grandmother performs as custom. The knowing was replaced, and the replacement was made to feel like knowledge.

This is what was actually taken. Not the bronze - the bronze was merely moved. What was taken was the frame in which the bronze was a deity.

## The Living House

Ngugi saw all of this for Africa, and his prescription - write in Gikuyu, dream in your own tongue - remains the most honest sentence in the entire literature. But India's case differs from the African one in a respect so decisive that it changes what decolonization can mean. For much of the colonized world, the destruction approached completeness: languages extinguished, transmission lines severed, the knowledge systems surviving as fragments in the colonizer's archives. Decolonization there must mean reconstruction - rebuilding from pieces catalogued by the very people who broke the original.

India's tradition is alive. Bruised, mislabelled, administered by hostile bureaucracies - but alive. Sanskrit is still composed and still argued in. The Agamas are still practiced; the consecration rites that made the Sripuranthan Nataraja a deity rather than a bronze are performed today, this week, in functioning temples. The parampara - the teacher-to-student transmission that is the civilization's actual storage medium - never stopped running. The pramanas, the means of valid knowledge that the darshanas spent two millennia refining, remain a working inheritance, available to anyone willing to be trained. India is the one major civilization for which decolonization can mean restoration - the reoccupation of a living house - rather than the archaeology of a grave.

This is also why the Indian case is so fiercely contested, in a way the return of Benin bronzes is not. Restoring a dead tradition threatens no one; it is heritage, and the West loves heritage. Restoring a living one - conceding that the Veda might be a pramana, that itihasa might be memory, that the darshanas might know things about consciousness that the philosophy department does not - would require the Western knowledge system to admit a peer. Everything in its institutional structure is built to prevent that admission. The South Asia studies department exists precisely to ensure that India is studied rather than listened to; the Indian elite still travels abroad to be certified competent in its own civilization. And at home, the colonial frame persists in the most literal institutional form imaginable: the secular Indian state administers Hindu temples through its endowments departments, appointing bureaucrats over deities, an arrangement no church or mosque is asked to tolerate. The house is living, and the occupying furniture is still in it.

## The Pramana Test

So let a single test be proposed for any project that calls itself decolonization: does it return the means of knowing, or only the objects known?

By this test, most of what travels under the name fails immediately. Renaming a building fails. A land acknowledgment fails. Even repatriation, alone, fails - the bronze comes home to a nation of people trained to receive it as art. What passes the test looks different and harder. It means reading itihasa as a civilization's memory of itself, subject to its own critical and commentarial apparatus - which exists, and is rigorous, and is older than the Western criticism that ignores it - before the default assumption of fiction is granted. It means teaching darshana as what it is: a set of disciplines with truth-claims, methods, and a training of the knower, to be engaged and argued with, as one argues with Aristotle rather than excavating him. It means approaching the Ramayana with Valmiki's questions before Foucault's. It means, institutionally, freeing the temples, and rebuilding the guru-shishya transmission lines with the same seriousness the modern state applies to engineering colleges. None of this requires rejecting Western knowledge; the tradition has never met an argument it refused to have. It requires only that the encounter happen between two knowledge systems, rather than between one knowledge system and its specimen.

The objection arrives on schedule: is this not nativism, the mirror-image chauvinism of the wounded? The answer is that the emic starting point is simply the standard courtesy every other tradition already receives. No one demands the Talmud be taught primarily as failed Bronze Age history, or opens a seminar on Aquinas by establishing that God does not exist. The tradition knows itself; begin there, and argue afterward. That is all. And the beneficiary of that courtesy would be everyone, because the questions the West's own frontier disciplines are now fumbling toward - what consciousness is, what a mind is, what technology is for - sit fully articulated in the darshanas, waiting in the blind spot.

The bronzes will keep coming home; the Manhattan DA has a backlog. Good. Let them come. But do not mistake the procession of returning objects for the return of anything that matters, so long as the receiving civilization has been trained to catalogue rather than to worship, and so long as "decolonization" itself remains a Western seminar - the kind that opens by acknowledging stolen land from a written statement on an iPhone, and proceeds directly to teaching the Gita as an ideological apparatus of Brahminical patriarchy. That is the colonizer's latest self-portrait, hung in the gallery where the Nataraja used to stand.

The deity was returned as art. The remaining task, the real one, is to become again the kind of people who can receive him as a deity.
