---
title: The Problem That Wasn't There
description: How Western philosophy built a trap for itself, and why Indic phenomenology never fell in
category: consciousness
---

There is something strange about the way Western philosophy of mind has organized itself around the "hard problem of consciousness." Coined by David Chalmers in the mid-1990s, the problem is this: even a complete neuroscientific account of the brain — every synapse, every firing pattern, every chemical gradient — seems to leave out the felt quality of experience. You can explain why a creature processes red-wavelength light and behaves accordingly without ever explaining what it is like to see red. That irreducible what-it-is-likeness, which philosophers call qualia, appears to float free of any physical account. This gap between objective description and subjective experience is what Chalmers calls "hard" — not empirically hard, but hard in principle, resistant to the reductive methods that have worked everywhere else.

The hard problem is real, in the sense that the gap it points to is genuinely there. But the way it has been framed — as a puzzle internal to a physicalist ontology, a local difficulty to be solved by better neuroscience or clever metaphysics — is not the only way to see it. There is another reading: the hard problem is not a problem that consciousness poses to physics, but a problem that a particular intellectual tradition poses to itself. It is a symptom of a starting point, not a brute fact about the world. And recognizing that changes what solutions are even thinkable.

---

The specific tradition that generates the hard problem is the one running from Descartes through Locke, Hume, and Kant, and into the analytic philosophy of mind that dominates Anglophone universities. Its central move, made explicit by Descartes and never quite undone by his successors, is to split reality into two: the extended material world, available to third-person measurement, and the thinking subject, available only in the first person. Descartes thought he could hold both substances together through God. When that theological glue came unstuck in the Enlightenment, philosophy was left with two irreconcilable residues — matter and mind — and a permanent crisis about how they relate.

The scientific revolution deepened the problem by making the material side seem more and more complete. Newton's mechanics, then chemistry, then evolutionary biology, then molecular biology — each wave of science closed off another space where mind might have been hiding. By the twentieth century, the dominant intellectual culture had committed to physicalism: there is only matter, and mind is somehow what matter does when organized in a certain way. This is the framework within which the hard problem lands with such force. If everything real is physical, then consciousness should be explicable physically. But it conspicuously isn't, which produces either elaborate philosophical maneuvers to explain it away (eliminativism, illusionism) or equally elaborate maneuvers to smuggle it back in (panpsychism, property dualism). The whole argument is conducted inside the Cartesian room.

What makes this strange is that the starting assumption — that matter is the primary and irreducible substance, and mind is derivative — is not derived from observation. No one has ever observed matter giving rise to consciousness. No one has ever been outside their own experience long enough to verify that the physical world as described by physics is more fundamental than the experience of it. The assumption of matter-primacy is a methodological posture that has been extraordinarily productive for technology and certain domains of science, but it has no special claim on metaphysical truth. The fact that it generates intractable puzzles about consciousness is at least some evidence against it.

---

The Samkhya darshana, among the oldest systematic philosophies on record, begins from the opposite end. Its foundational distinction is between purusha — pure witnessing consciousness — and prakriti — the dynamic, evolving material principle from which everything from gross matter to subtle mind is derived. Consciousness, in Samkhya, is not produced. It does not emerge. It is the given. What the world explains is not how consciousness arises from matter but how matter comes to be illuminated by consciousness — how the operations of prakriti, which are otherwise inert and mechanical, become experience when they come into the orbit of purusha.

The framework sounds dualist, and technically it is, but the dualism works in the opposite direction from Descartes. In Samkhya, it is matter that is the mysterious term, not consciousness. Consciousness is the one thing that needs no explanation — it is self-luminous, svaprakasha, the condition of any explanation at all. What requires explaining is why this pure awareness seems to be bound up with a body and a mind, why it seems to have experiences rather than merely being experience. The entire Samkhya project, and the Yoga school that inherits it, is directed at understanding that entanglement and working backward to the pure witnessing that precedes it.

Advaita Vedanta takes this further. For Shankara, the apparent duality between consciousness and matter is itself an appearance within consciousness. Pure awareness — Brahman, or in its individual guise, Atman — is the single reality. What we call the external world is not a separate substance but an appearance, much as a dream landscape appears in and to the dreaming mind. This is not idealism in Berkeley's sense, where matter is reduced to collections of ideas in human minds. It is closer to the claim that the categories of subject and object, mind and matter, arise within awareness and are not prior to it. The hard problem simply does not arise in this framework, because there is no explanatory gap to bridge. There is only awareness appearing in various forms, some of which we call material and some of which we call mental.

I am not arguing that Advaita Vedanta is correct as a metaphysical claim, or that Western philosophers should simply adopt it. What I am arguing is that it represents a coherent and internally consistent philosophical framework that does not generate the hard problem — and that this fact is philosophically significant. A framework that does not generate the hard problem is not less sophisticated than one that does. It may be more sophisticated. The ability to see through a bad question is rarer than the ability to answer it.

---

The standard Western response to this line of argument is to accuse it of mysterianism or obscurantism — of explaining consciousness by making it mysterious in a different way. But this response is too quick. The Indic traditions are not saying that consciousness is beyond understanding; they are saying that consciousness is the condition of understanding, and therefore cannot be treated as an object of explanation in the same way that material things can. This is not a retreat from rational inquiry; it is a claim about the logical structure of rational inquiry. Any explanation proceeds from consciousness. Any experiment is conducted by a conscious observer. Any theory is entertained in a conscious mind. To explain consciousness as an output of physical processes is to explain the precondition of explanation by the things it makes possible. There is a genuine logical point here.

Phenomenology, the Western philosophical tradition that takes experience seriously as a starting point, arrives at something similar from inside the European tradition itself. Husserl's reduction, his methodological bracketing of the natural attitude in order to examine the pure structure of consciousness, is an attempt to do what Indic epistemology does structurally: to take experience as the primary datum rather than an afterthought to be explained away. Heidegger's being-in-the-world, Merleau-Ponty's embodied perception, Levinas's ethics of the face — all of these are attempts to describe the texture of lived experience without forcing it into the objective-description model that generates the hard problem. They have not solved the hard problem in Chalmers's sense, but they have made plausible the view that the hard problem is an artifact of a particular descriptive strategy rather than a brute fact about reality.

There is something telling about the fact that Western phenomenology, when it goes deepest, tends toward positions that resemble Indic philosophy more than they resemble standard analytic physicalism. This is not a coincidence. It is what happens when serious attention is paid to experience itself rather than to theoretical models of it.

---

None of this settles the metaphysical question. Consciousness remains genuinely difficult. The relationship between awareness and the physical processes of the brain is genuinely puzzling, even granting that the hard problem as formulated by Chalmers is the wrong way to put it. What would a better framing look like?

Something like this: rather than asking how physical processes give rise to experience, ask how the categories we use to describe physical processes are derived from experience in the first place. The concepts of space, time, causation, and matter are not directly perceived — they are abstractions from the flow of experience, refined over centuries of scientific work. They are extraordinarily useful abstractions. But they are derived from experience, not prior to it. This means that there is no paradox in experience's not being explicable in terms of them; it would be more paradoxical if it were.

This reframing is not obviously true — there are smart people who would resist it at every step — but it has the advantage of not generating the hard problem. The experience of red is not mysterious against this background; it is simply what experience looks like when it is organized in a particular way. The gap between objective description and subjective feel is a gap between two different modes of attention, not a gap in the fabric of reality.

What the Indic traditions offer, practically speaking, is not just a philosophical framework but a set of methods for investigating consciousness directly — through yoga, meditation, and the systematic cultivation of attention. These methods have been refined over millennia. Their results are not merely anecdotal. They point consistently toward something that the philosophical framework predicts: that consciousness has a depth and structure that is not visible from the third-person perspective, and that sustained first-person investigation reveals dimensions of experience that conceptual analysis alone cannot reach. The hard problem was generated by a tradition that tried to understand mind purely through its outward traces. The Indic traditions suggest that the mind can also be investigated from within — and that this investigation is not less rigorous, merely differently organized.

---

The persistence of the hard problem in Western philosophy of mind is partly a sociological fact. Academic philosophy has its own gravitational fields; problems that have generated a century of literature are sustained by the weight of that literature, independent of whether they are well-formed. Chalmers's formulation crystallized a set of intuitions that were already present in the Cartesian inheritance, and gave them a precise enough shape to be argued over productively, which is no small thing. The hard problem has sharpened thinking about consciousness in useful ways. But there is a risk that its apparent intractability gets naturalized — that philosophers begin to treat the gap between objective description and subjective experience as a brute feature of reality, something to be accommodated rather than dissolved.

The Indic philosophical traditions suggest a different ambition. Not to accommodate the gap but to question the assumptions that produce it. Not to find a special metaphysical glue to connect matter and mind, but to notice that the categories of matter and mind are themselves products of a particular mode of attention, and that shifting that attention reveals a different landscape — one in which consciousness is not the puzzling exception to a material world but the unquestionable ground from which any world appears.

Whether that shift is possible within a philosophy that still carries the Cartesian inheritance is an open question. Western phenomenology has made serious attempts. Neoplatonic and idealist currents in European philosophy point in the same direction. But the Indic traditions have the advantage of three millennia of sustained engagement with these questions, a vocabulary developed specifically to describe the structure of consciousness from within, and a lineage of practitioners whose first-person investigation has been tested and refined across generations.

The hard problem is not hard because consciousness is mysterious. It is hard because the framework doing the asking was built to exclude it. The first step toward a better philosophy of mind may be to notice that, and to ask what a philosophy of mind built around consciousness as the primary datum — rather than as the awkward residue of a materialist ontology — would actually look like.

The Indic traditions have been asking that question for a long time. The answers they have arrived at are not easy or simple — but they do not generate the hard problem. That alone should give Western philosophy of mind pause.
