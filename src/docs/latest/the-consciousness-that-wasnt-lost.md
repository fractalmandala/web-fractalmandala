---
title: The Consciousness That Wasn't Lost
description: The hard problem is a symptom of materialism, not a discovery about mind.
category: consciousness
---

When David Chalmers published "Facing Up to the Problem of Consciousness" in 1995, he drew a line that philosophy of mind has been unable to cross ever since. On one side of that line: the "easy problems" - explaining attention, perception, memory, the integration of information, the coordination of behavior. These are difficult science, but solvable in principle by the existing methods of neuroscience and cognitive theory. On the other side: the hard problem. Why does any of it feel like anything at all? Why is there something it is like to see red, to hear a minor chord resolve, to remember a face that is gone? The neural correlates of color perception can be measured, mapped, and modeled. The redness - the qualia, the raw phenomenal character - cannot. This gap between objective description and subjective experience is the hard problem, and three decades after Chalmers named it, it sits in the center of philosophy of mind like a stone nobody can lift or carry away.

The standard response from the materialist camp is to insist that the problem will dissolve once neuroscience matures - that qualia, like vital spirits before them, will turn out to have been a confusion. Daniel Dennett has spent a career making exactly this argument, and making it with such rhetorical force that many physicalists have simply adopted his confidence as a substitute for the explanation. His position - that there is no hard problem because consciousness itself, as a phenomenon distinct from information processing, does not really exist - is called illusionism. The name is diagnostic. When your philosophy of mind requires consciousness to be an illusion, something has gone wrong at the foundations.

What has gone wrong is the starting point.

The materialist frame, which Western philosophy of mind has inherited from Descartes and then from the scientific revolution, begins with matter as the primary datum of reality and asks how consciousness can be derived from or explained by it. This is the move that generates the hard problem. If matter is prior, and if matter is fundamentally non-experiential - defined, in the Cartesian settlement, by extension, by measurability, by the absence of any interiority - then the emergence of felt experience from it will always be mysterious. The explanatory gap Chalmers identified is built into the ontology itself. Closing it would require showing that felt experience can be fully explained in terms that, by definition, exclude felt experience. This is not a difficulty to be solved by more research. It is a contradiction.

The Sankhya school of Indian philosophy - one of the six astika darsanas, worked out in elaborate systematic detail by Ishvarakrishna around the third century CE but drawing on traditions far older - begins from the opposite direction. It begins with cit: consciousness, awareness, the bare fact of experience. Sankhya is rigorously dualist - prakriti, primal materiality and the substrate of all physical process, is real. But purusha, the witnessing consciousness that illuminates prakriti without being identical to it, is equally and irreducibly real, and has ontological priority. Purusha is the condition under which matter is cognized at all.

The consequences of this starting point are profound. If consciousness is primary, there is no hard problem. There is nothing puzzling about the fact that experience exists, because experience is the baseline. What requires explanation is not why matter produces experience, but how consciousness comes to be apparently entangled with a particular body, in a particular world, perceiving red and hearing chords. This is a different question - and it is a tractable one. Sankhya answers it through the theory of guna-interaction, through the operations of buddhi and ahamkara, through the mechanics of how prakriti evolves its multiple levels of manifestation. The answer may not satisfy a physicalist, but it does not generate an explanatory gap that collapses into incoherence. The map is consistent with its own territory.

Vedanta goes further. Where Sankhya maintains a real distinction between purusha and prakriti, Advaita Vedanta - as elaborated by Shankaracharya and his tradition - holds that the distinction is itself an appearance within consciousness. Brahman, the ground of all reality, is described in the Upanishads as sat-cit-ananda: being, consciousness, and bliss as an undivided unity. Consciousness is what reality is. The world of apparent plurality - of objects, bodies, minds, and their interactions - arises within brahman as a manifestation, the way a dream arises within the dreamer without being separate from the dreaming mind.

##### The hard problem of consciousness is precisely the problem that arises when a civilization begins with the one thing consciousness cannot be: a category of explanation that excludes it.

The Upanishadic tradition worked out the phenomenology of this with a precision that Western philosophy of mind has only recently begun to approximate, and then only partially. The Mandukya Upanishad's analysis of consciousness through its four states - jagrat (waking), svapna (dreaming), sushupti (deep sleep), and turiya (the fourth, the pure witnessing awareness underlying the other three) - anticipates much of what twentieth-century phenomenology struggled to articulate. Edmund Husserl spent decades trying to describe the structure of pure consciousness stripped of its intentional content - what he called the transcendental ego, the bare subject that remains when all objects of awareness are bracketed away. The Mandukya had named turiya as exactly this structure, and had located it as the most fundamental mode of consciousness, the one that is always present, never sleeping, never dreaming, not lost in waking perception. The difference is that where Husserl's phenomenological reduction was a methodological tool for investigating consciousness, the Upanishadic tradition took turiya to be ontologically basic - the ground from which the other states arise and into which they subside.

This is a claim about the structure of reality. And it is testable in its own terms - by the first-person methods of sustained introspection that the Dharmic tradition has developed over millennia, though not by the instruments of neuroscience, which measure physical correlates and nothing else. The entire structure of yoga and meditation, as laid out in Patanjali's Yoga Sutras, is an empirical program for investigating the nature of consciousness directly. The investigative instrument is the mind itself, refined and stabilized through sustained practice to the point where it can examine its own nature without the distortions of conceptual overlay and habitual reactivity. The data generated by this investigation are not publishable in the Journal of Neuroscience. They are accessible to anyone willing to undertake the program. That most Western philosophers of mind have not undertaken it, and consider first-person investigation methodologically inadmissible, is a self-imposed limitation that has nothing to do with rigor and everything to do with ideology.

The ideology in question is the one that Thomas Nagel, in a rare moment of philosophical honesty, called "the materialist dogma." In his 2012 book *Mind and Cosmos*, Nagel - not a Vedantin, not a mystic, a thoroughly secular analytic philosopher - argued that the neo-Darwinian materialist synthesis cannot account for consciousness, that the existence of mind is a fundamental fact about the universe that any adequate theory of reality must explain from the start rather than hope to derive at the end. The book was received with extraordinary, organized, tribal hostility. Nagel had questioned the dogma, and the community organized to contain the damage. But the arguments have not been answered. They have simply been ignored, or met with reassertions that materialism must eventually work because the alternatives are unacceptable.

The alternatives are unacceptable because they require Western philosophy to question its founding ontological commitment. If consciousness cannot be derived from matter, then either matter has some form of experiential character at every level of organization - the position called panpsychism, which has gained considerable ground among analytic philosophers in recent years - or consciousness is ontologically prior to matter, which is something very close to what the Upanishads say. Both options require revising the Cartesian-Newtonian picture of a universe of inert, meaningless matter governed by physical law, with consciousness as a late and puzzling addition. That picture has been so successful at generating technology and science that it is now treated as self-evidently correct. Its success in those domains does not entail its completeness. Newton's mechanics were extraordinarily successful and demonstrably incomplete.

Panpsychism, the currently fashionable escape route for analytic philosophers who have given up on illusionism, introduces its own problems. If every particle of matter has some micro-experiential quality, how do these micro-experiences combine to produce the unified, rich, perspectival experience of a conscious human being? This is the combination problem, and it is genuinely difficult. The panpsychist inherits the hard problem in a new form. The Vedantic resolution is more radical and, paradoxically, simpler: there is no combination problem because consciousness was never divided in the first place. What appears as individual experience - the jiva's particular perspective on the world - is brahman appearing as if limited, the way a pot limits the sky it encloses without the sky inside the pot being different in nature from the sky outside. The limitation is real at the empirical level. The division is not real at the ontological level.

None of this means that neuroscience is asking the wrong questions. The neural correlates of consciousness are genuinely interesting. Understanding how specific patterns of brain activity correspond to specific experiences is useful knowledge - for medicine, for treating disorders of consciousness, for building better brain-computer interfaces. What it cannot do, given the framework within which it operates, is explain why any particular neural pattern is accompanied by experience at all. Neuroscience maps the correlates. It does not and cannot touch the explanation.

The deepest irony is that the most sophisticated attempt to address this within contemporary analytic philosophy - Integrated Information Theory, Giulio Tononi's proposal that consciousness is identical to a specific kind of information integration he calls phi - arrives at something strikingly convergent with aspects of Vedanta, though without knowing it. IIT holds that consciousness is a fundamental feature of any system that has the right kind of causal structure, and that it can in principle be present in systems very different from biological brains. Tononi takes consciousness seriously as a datum rather than explaining it away. He is moving, haltingly and without the conceptual vocabulary to articulate where he is going, toward the insight that consciousness has to be built into the ontology at the ground level. He is fumbling toward what the Mandukya said with precision.

One wishes he had been pointed toward the cidakasha - the space of consciousness, the luminous expanse of awareness that Tantric and Vedantic traditions take as the primary metaphor for the ground of experience - before he spent years developing phi. The detour through information theory is not wrong. It simply covers enormous ground to arrive at a destination that was marked on an older map.

The hard problem, then, is a gift we accepted from a tradition in crisis. Chalmers was right that there is a genuine mystery. He was right that the mystery is being covered over by philosophers who prefer to deny the problem than solve it. What he did not do - could not do, given his formation - was step outside the materialist ontology to ask whether the mystery is an artifact of the starting point. When you begin with matter and ask how consciousness arises, you get the hard problem. When you begin with consciousness and ask how matter arises within it, you get the Upanishads. The second question is not easier. It is different in kind, and it generates a philosophy of mind that does not require consciousness to be an illusion in order to maintain its coherence.

The consciousness was never lost. We just stopped looking where it was.
