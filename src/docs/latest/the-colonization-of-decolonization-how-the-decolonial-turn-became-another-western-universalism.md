---
title: The Colonization of Decolonization
description: How the decolonial turn became another Western universalism
category: decolonization
---

There is a paradox lodged at the center of the decolonial movement as it has flourished in Western universities over the past two decades. The project that bills itself as the most rigorous challenge to Western intellectual hegemony has been conducted almost entirely within the categories, institutional frameworks, and rhetorical conventions of Western academia. Its founding texts are written in European languages, published by American presses, taught in syllabi assembled by faculty who have climbed the ladders of Western credentialing, and evaluated by the standards of Western peer review. The decolonial turn has largely remained a turn within Western thought — a family quarrel, a critique from inside the house. This would be unremarkable if the movement's ambitions were modest. But they are not. Decoloniality, as theorized by Walter Mignolo, Aníbal Quijano, María Lugones, and their many descendants, claims to offer an escape from what Mignolo calls "the colonial matrix of power" — a systematic reorganization of the world's knowledges, not merely a reform of Western philosophy. The question worth pressing is whether any such escape is possible when the escape route runs so conspicuously through the colonial metropole.

The irony is not accidental, and tracing it requires some precision about what decoloniality actually argues. Quijano's foundational concept of the "coloniality of power" distinguishes between colonialism, the political-economic system of territorial domination, and coloniality, the deeper epistemic-cultural ordering that outlasts formal colonial rule. The argument is that even after the flags were lowered and the governors departed, the fundamental classification of peoples, knowledges, and forms of life introduced by European expansion persists. The world continues to be sorted by European-derived categories of race, civilization, modernity, progress, and development. Non-Western peoples remain legible to themselves and to institutions primarily through frameworks that were designed by and for European self-understanding. This is a genuinely illuminating diagnosis. The question is not whether the disease is real — it is — but whether the prescribed treatment reproduces the pathology.

Consider how the decolonial tradition handles non-Western intellectual heritage. Mignolo, following Quijano, speaks of "border thinking" and "epistemic disobedience" — cognitive postures of resistance performed at the boundaries of Western modernity. The framing is essentially reactive. Non-Western thought enters the scene not as a self-sufficient tradition with its own internal problems, methods, and ambitions, but as a response to colonialism, as a critical counter-position. Indigenous knowledge becomes interesting to the decolonial theorist precisely when it serves as a negation of Western knowledge. This is a subtle but severe reduction. The Nyāya school's rigorous epistemology of pramāṇa — valid sources of knowledge — was developed over roughly two millennia to answer questions that arose from within the Sanskrit intellectual world: questions about inference, perception, the reliability of testimony, the nature of universals. These questions were not primarily responses to Western philosophy. They had their own genealogy, their own internal disputes, their own criteria of success. The decolonial framework, by positioning non-Western thought as counter-modernity, inadvertently centers modernity as the reference point. The West remains the sun around which all other intellectual traditions orbit, even in opposition.

This is not a minor technical objection. It touches the deep structure of how decolonial theory handles alterity. Levinas wrote about the danger of reducing the Other to a totality — of assimilating what is genuinely foreign to familiar categories. The decolonial tradition, aware of this danger in the abstract, enacts it at the level of method. When Walter Mignolo describes the Zapatista movement or the World Social Forum as instances of "border thinking," he is reading them through a theoretical vocabulary assembled in European and Latin American graduate seminars. When Linda Tuhiwai Smith writes about "decolonizing methodologies," the concept of methodology itself — a systematic, reflexive account of research practice — is a legacy of the same tradition she is interrogating. The decolonial move is often to add a prefix: de-colonize, un-make, re-center. But prefixes are added to words that remain standing. The underlying conceptual architecture goes largely undisturbed.

None of this would matter if Western concepts were genuinely universal — if the Enlightenment had indeed discovered the neutral language of human reason rather than one regional tradition's self-description. The foundational wager of modern Western thought was precisely this: that its categories, by virtue of their rationality, were applicable everywhere and to everyone. The decolonial critique rightly identifies this universalism as the sleight of hand that allowed European expansion to present itself as the spread of civilization rather than the imposition of a particular form of life. But if that critique is correct, it applies reflexively. The decolonial framework cannot exempt itself from the charge it levels at liberalism: that local categories are being universalized. Decoloniality in practice tends to generate a new universalism — the universalism of the oppressed, the universalism of anti-colonial resistance — that is just as totalizing as the Enlightenment universalisms it replaces. Everyone, in every corner of the world, is now to be understood primarily as a subject of colonial power and a potential agent of decolonial resistance. The Andean peasant, the Dalit lawyer, the Aboriginal artist, and the Bedouin shepherd are herded into a single categorical identity — the colonized — that is itself a product of Western social theory.

The comparison with what a genuinely emic intellectual recovery looks like makes this clearer. Take the reemergence of serious Sanskrit philosophy in contemporary Indian scholarship — the work of Jonardon Ganeri, Bimal Krishna Matilal, or more recently Vishwa Adluri and Joydeep Bagchee's reconstruction of the hermeneutics of the Mahābhārata. What distinguishes this work from the decolonial turn is that it enters non-Western intellectual traditions on those traditions' own terms. Matilal's reconstruction of Navya-Nyāya logic, for instance, does not begin by positioning Nyāya as a response to Western analytical philosophy, even though he was himself trained in Oxford and wrote for Western philosophical audiences. He reconstructed the internal arguments, the technical vocabulary, the problems the tradition was actually trying to solve. The result is not a negation of Western philosophy but an enlargement of it — a demonstration that the logical work done in Gangeśa's Tattvacintāmaṇi is commensurable with, and in some respects more sophisticated than, contemporaneous European treatments of inference and predication. The standard for success is philosophical rigor, not political resistance.

This distinction — between recovery and resistance — points toward what a serious decolonization of knowledge would actually require. It would require sustained scholarly work in the intellectual traditions that colonialism suppressed or distorted: not because they are useful weapons against Western hegemony, but because they are worth knowing, because they contain genuine insights into problems that matter, because they represent accumulated human thought that has been made invisible by an accident of power. The Yoga Sūtras of Patañjali, the Vijñānavāda of Vasubandhu, the phenomenology of Abhinavagupta's Pratyabhijñā school — these are not exotic alternatives to Western philosophy useful for scoring points in graduate seminars. They are bodies of thought that made real progress on real problems: the nature of attention, the structure of consciousness, the relationship between perception and inference, the possibility of liberation. Their neglect represents an impoverishment of human thought, not just a political injustice.

This matters because the political framing tends to subordinate truth to power. The question "whose knowledge?" is not the most important question one can ask of an intellectual tradition. The more important question is: is it true? Does it clarify? Does it illuminate the actual structure of things? Decolonial theory, shaped by its inheritance from Foucauldian and Saidian analysis, tends to treat all knowledge claims as expressions of power-relations — to ask not whether something is true but who benefits from its being regarded as true. Applied consistently, this methodology dissolves itself. If the claim that "all knowledge is political" is itself a knowledge claim, it must itself be political, and we are left with nothing but power. But the Indian epistemological traditions offer a different starting point: the systematic investigation of pramāṇa, of what counts as valid cognition, conducted as a philosophical rather than a political inquiry. Recovering that tradition in its depth would be genuinely decolonizing in a way that writing yet another paper about "epistemic injustice" for a Western philosophy journal is not.

There is also the question of who the audience for decolonial theory has been, and what that reveals. The genre presupposes a reader who is already steeped in Western critical theory — in Gramsci's hegemony, in Spivak's subaltern, in Derrida's deconstruction. These references are not supplementary decoration; they are the conceptual infrastructure of the argument. Mignolo explicitly positions his work as a departure from, and an extension of, postcolonial theory, which is itself a departure from, and extension of, poststructuralism. The intellectual genealogy runs through Derrida, Foucault, and Lacan in Paris, not through Śaṃkara, Nāgārjuna, or Ibn Rushd in their native contexts. The decolonial text is written for a reader who already knows what a "subject position" is and why "epistemic violence" is philosophically loaded. This is not a criticism of those individual thinkers, who are often doing serious work. It is an observation about the sociology of the enterprise: decoloniality as an academic genre is principally a conversation among Western-trained scholars about non-Western peoples, conducted in Western institutional spaces and evaluated by Western professional standards. The people whose traditions are being discussed have largely not been architects of the framework.

Compare this with the way in which Swami Vivekananda presented Vedānta at the Parliament of World Religions in 1893, or with the way B.R. Ambedkar reconstructed Buddhist social philosophy in the 1950s. Neither was performing decolonial resistance for a Western academic audience. Vivekananda was making a substantive claim about the universality of Advaita — a claim he had arrived at through immersion in the tradition itself. Ambedkar was doing political philosophy with tools partly borrowed from Western liberalism and partly recovered from Pali Buddhism, and the synthesis was genuinely original rather than derivative. Both figures were in dialogue with Western thought, but neither was primarily reactive to it. The frame was theirs.

What decolonization of knowledge would genuinely look like is something the decolonial academy has so far found difficult to enact rather than to theorize. It would require institutional restructuring: seminaries and universities in India, West Africa, and the Arab world capable of training scholars in their own intellectual traditions to the same level of rigor and depth as Oxford trains a classicist in Greek. It would require the reversal of prestige gradients that still make a publication in an American journal more valuable on the academic job market than original scholarship in Sanskrit or Arabic. It would require the painstaking recovery of textual traditions that were disrupted, and the patient reconstruction of interpretive communities within which those traditions make sense. None of this is the work of writing theory papers. It is the work of philologists, philosophers, and institutional reformers operating over generations.

The decolonial turn has had real value. It has named something real about how knowledge hierarchies operate, forced Western philosophy to acknowledge its parochialism, and opened some institutional space for non-Western perspectives within the academy. But its central failure is the failure of any critique that remains too comfortable with the object it is critiquing. By conducting the critique of Western intellectual hegemony from within Western intellectual institutions, using Western critical theory as its primary toolkit, and writing for an audience that is principally Western-trained, decolonial theory has reproduced at the level of method exactly what it diagnoses at the level of content: the subsumption of non-Western thought into a universal framework whose universality is assumed rather than demonstrated. The colonization of decolonization is not a paradox to be celebrated as transgressive irony. It is a problem to be solved — and solving it requires something harder than theory. It requires turning toward the traditions themselves, with enough patience and humility to let them speak in their own voices, about their own questions, on their own terms.
