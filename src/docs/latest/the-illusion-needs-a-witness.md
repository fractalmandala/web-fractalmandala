description: Western philosophy of mind has argued itself into denying the one datum it began with. A millennium-old Indian debate already contains the diagnosis, and the way out.
category: consciousness
---

Something strange has happened in the Western study of mind. A tradition that began with the one datum its founder declared indubitable - I think, therefore I am - has, four centuries later, produced a serious and growing school of philosophers who deny that experience exists. They do not deny that brains process information, that organisms discriminate stimuli, that people report inner states. They deny that there is something it is like to taste coffee or to grieve. Daniel Dennett spent five decades preparing this ground; Keith Frankish gave the mature position its name: illusionism. Phenomenal consciousness, the felt quality of experience, is an introspective illusion. We seem to have qualia, and the seeming is the whole of the fact.

The temptation for anyone raised in a Dharmic frame is to laugh. Resist it. Illusionism deserves something better than ridicule, because illusionism is honest. It is the third-person method of Western science arriving, with complete integrity, at the only two destinations that method permits. Follow the logic and the fork becomes visible. Galileo founded modern science by an act of exclusion: the qualities that could be mathematized - extension, motion, quantity - were assigned to the world, and the qualities that could not - color, sound, taste, feeling - were exiled into the mind, which was then handed to the theologians. Philip Goff has told this story well: physics does not struggle to explain consciousness; physics was constructed by removing consciousness from its domain, so that everything remaining would submit to equations. The method that resulted is magnificent, and it has one structural property that concerns us here. It admits only objects. Whatever can be measured, pointed at, varied, and publicly checked can enter; whatever cannot, cannot.

Consciousness cannot. Whatever else the traditions disagree on, they converge here: awareness is that by which objects appear, and it does not itself appear as one more object alongside them. The eye does not occur in its own visual field. So when the object-method turns to consciousness, it faces a forced choice. It can declare the thing permanently mysterious, which is David Chalmers' hard problem - the honest admission that no arrangement of objective facts will ever entail a subjective fact. Or it can declare the thing nonexistent, which is Frankish's illusionism - the honest admission that if the method is complete, then whatever the method cannot capture was never there. Mystery or denial. Both positions are rigorous. Both are artifacts. Neither tells us anything about consciousness; both tell us everything about the instrument that produced them. A metal detector sweeping a field reports either an anomaly it cannot resolve or a clean, empty scan. It will never report wood.

##### The hard problem and illusionism are the two failure states of a single method: a science built by excluding the subject, asked at last to account for the subject it excluded.

## The Regress

There is a classical objection to illusionism, and every neophyte finds it within minutes: an illusion needs a witness. If phenomenal consciousness is an illusion, the illusion itself is a way things seem, and a seeming is precisely an instance of the phenomenon being denied. The magician's trick requires an audience that experiences the deception. Deny the audience and there is no trick; there is only a stage mechanism operating in an empty theater, and no fact of the matter about whether it fooled anyone.

Frankish has a reply, and it is worth stating in its strongest form because the entire contemporary debate now balances on it. He distinguishes functional seeming from phenomenal seeming. When the illusionist says consciousness "seems" phenomenal, he means only that the system enters states that dispose it to judge, report, and react as if phenomenality were present. The seeming is a pattern of dispositions, a configuration of causes and effects, and dispositions require no inner light. The theater can be empty and the reviews still get written.

Notice what this reply does. It answers the regress by converting every apparent experience into a report, a judgment, a disposition - into one more object, one more publicly specifiable state. Which is to say: it answers the objection by reapplying the method whose adequacy is the very thing in question. The debate has become, as its own participants now concede, meta-philosophical: a dispute about what kinds of premises are admissible. The illusionist admits only third-person premises and finds, unsurprisingly, no consciousness. His opponent begins from the bare fact of experiencing - Morozov's recent reformulation of the Moorean argument trades on nothing more than the felt reality of pain - and finds the illusionist's conclusion less credible than any premise that could support it. Each side calls the other's starting point question-begging. Stalemate, on the current maps.

But the maps are incomplete. This exact debate has been fought before, at length, with better instruments, and it reached a resolution.

## A Debate Already Fought

For roughly a thousand years, the darshanas of India argued about precisely this question: how is a cognition known? When I see a pot, what makes the seeing itself something I am aware of, rather than a blind mechanical registration?

The Nyaya school gave the answer that contemporary philosophy would call a higher-order theory. A cognition is an event, an object like any other, and it is known the way objects are known: by a further cognition directed at it. They called this anuvyavasaya, the after-cognition. First the seeing of the pot; then, if attention turns inward, a second perception whose object is the first. David Rosenthal's higher-order thought theory, currently one of the four or five live contenders in consciousness science, reproduces this structure without knowing it has a Sanskrit ancestry: a mental state is conscious when a higher-order state represents it. Kumarila Bhatta's Mimamsa refined the position - we infer our cognitions from the "cognizedness" they leave on their objects - but kept the essential commitment: awareness of awareness is a second act.

Against this stood Prabhakara, and behind him the Advaita of Shankara, with the doctrine that resolves the field: svayamprakasha, self-luminosity. A cognition reveals its object and itself in a single occurrence. It does not wait for a second cognition to confer awareness upon it, and the argument for this was the one the Naiyayikas could never finally answer: the regress. If the first cognition needs a second to be known, the second needs a third, and awareness never manages to occur at all - anavastha, the fault of non-termination. The lamp analogy carried the point. A lamp illuminates the objects in a room and requires no second lamp to be seen; illumination is what it is. Consciousness is svayamprakasha in exactly this sense: its occurring and its being manifest are one fact, without an inner spectator watching a screen, without a second state reflecting on a first.

Advaita then pressed the analysis one step further, and this step is the one contemporary philosophy has not taken. In the Drg-Drshya-Viveka, the discrimination of seer from seen, the tradition established that whatever can be made an object of awareness belongs, by that very fact, to the seen - including thoughts, emotions, the sense of self, the entire mind. The seer is never among the seen. Consciousness is that to which all appearing appears, the sakshi, the witness; and a witness, by its structure, cannot turn up as an exhibit in its own courtroom. This dissolves both prongs of the Western fork at once. The hard problem asked how objective processes generate subjectivity, and the answer is that the question is malformed: subjectivity is never a generated object, so no object-story could either produce or exclude it. Illusionism claimed the light is an illusion, and the answer is that illusion is a precise technical concept with preconditions. The tradition analyzed it exhaustively: the rope mistaken for a snake at dusk. Every illusion requires a real substrate on which the false form is projected, and a real awareness to which it is projected. Adhyasa, superimposition, presupposes a witness the way a shadow presupposes light. One can be wrong about any content of consciousness. One cannot be wrong about the fact of consciousness, because being wrong is itself an event in it.

##### The self-luminosity debate is the higher-order-theory debate, run for a thousand years with the regress objection pressed to its terminus - and the terminus is that consciousness is never an object, which means the object-method's failure to find it was a foregone conclusion, not a finding.

Frankish's functional-seeming reply, seen from this vantage, has a familiar shape. It is the Nyaya move - awareness reduced to a further state, the state to an object, the object to a disposition - performed without the opponent who spent centuries showing where it leads. A seeming that seems to no one is a report; and a report was exactly the sort of thing the theory of consciousness was supposed to explain. Illusionism does not explain the datum. It deletes the datum and keeps the paperwork.

## The Missing Instrument

Why did the West end here? The uncharitable answer is metaphysical prejudice, but the accurate answer is methodological, and it contains the more useful lesson. The West does not lack arguments about the first person. It lacks an instrument for it.

There was a moment when Western psychology tried. Wundt in Leipzig and Titchener at Cornell built laboratories of introspection, training observers to report the fine grain of experience. The project collapsed within a generation: the laboratories could not agree, Titchener's observers found sensory elements that Wurzburg's observers swore were absent, and there existed no procedure for adjudication. Behaviorism drew the conclusion that defined a century: the first person is unscientific, henceforth only behavior counts. It was the correct verdict on untrained introspection and a catastrophic verdict on the first person as such, roughly equivalent to abandoning astronomy because the first telescopes had chromatic aberration.

India drew the other conclusion, and built the telescope. The entire architecture of yoga - in the Patanjala sense, and in the Vedantic disciplines of shravana, manana, nididhyasana, and in the Buddhist cartographies of shamatha and vipashyana - is the systematic refinement of attention into an instrument capable of stable, repeatable, intersubjectively teachable observation of the mind by the mind. This is pramana logic applied inward. Perception counts as valid knowledge only when the instrument is unimpaired; the mind is the instrument of inner perception; an ordinary mind, flickering at the mercy of every vritti, is an impaired instrument. The tradition's response was calibration - decades of it, with lineages functioning as the peer review. The maps that resulted agree with each other across schools and centuries to a degree the Leipzig and Cornell laboratories never approached in their few decades: the layered analyses of the sheaths, the stages of absorption, the taxonomy of mental factors, the state-descriptions of turiya. Where observation is trained, first-person reports converge. That convergence is what a scientific instrument looks like from the inside.

The West has begun, in its peripheral vision, to notice. Francisco Varela's neurophenomenology proposed in 1996 that trained first-person method be married to neuroscience as an equal partner, and he reached for Buddhist contemplative discipline to supply the training - reached, in other words, for the Indic instrument, since his own tradition had none to offer. The mainstream, meanwhile, runs adversarial collaborations: the Cogitate consortium's seven-laboratory test of integrated information theory against global workspace theory, published in Nature in 2025, delivered blows to both. This was excellent science and its result was inevitable, because both theories are object-theories through and through - one locates consciousness in a posterior cortical hot zone, the other in fronto-parietal broadcast, and both treat the light itself as a thing to be found among the lit. The experiment was a metal-detector duel over a field of wood.

What holds for the person holds for the civilization; the fractal runs in both directions. An individual taught that his interiority is unreliable, or illusory, outsources it - to experts, to instruments, to whatever authority will tell him what he feels. A civilization that spends four centuries ruling the first person out of its knowledge system ends the same way: magnificent on objects, bankrupt on the subject, and finally denying the subject exists so the bankruptcy will not show on the books.

## What the Blind Spot Costs

The register to strike here is clarifying, and the loss to name is everyone's. Nothing in this argument requires Western science to fail; it requires Western science to notice what its method was built to exclude, and to stop mistaking the exclusion for a discovery. The cost of not noticing is now practical. Ethics boards are being asked whether animals suffer and whether machine systems might; an illusionism that cannot ground the badness of a burned child's pain - since the pain, on the theory, misrepresents a phenomenality that was never there - is in no position to answer. Psychiatry medicates conditions whose entire reality is first-personal while its parent discipline debates whether the first person exists. And a science of consciousness that runs decade-long international collaborations without once asking whether the observing instrument itself might be trainable is leaving its largest methodological upgrade sitting in the public domain, documented across two and a half millennia of texts, free of charge.

The svayamprakasha debate ended. The self-luminosity position won on argument, and the traditions that held it then did what the West has not yet imagined doing: they made the conclusion operational, building disciplines by which the witness-character of consciousness could be verified in the only laboratory where it ever shows up. That laboratory is open. The alternative is on display in the journals: a research program solemnly concluding that no one has ever experienced anything, in papers written by authors who merely seem to exist, for readers who merely seem to read them, reporting a seeming that seems to no one - and calling this, of all the words available, an illusion. Our tradition kept a better word for the whole affair. It called the witness that cannot be denied the atman, and it noticed that every attempt at the denial is one more thing the witness watches.
