Joseph Drew joseph.drew@iscsc.org
For many years, I have been an active member of the International Society for the Comparative Study of Civilizations and Editor-in-Chief of its journal, the Comparative Civilizations Review. Since the 1960s, I have also been a university administrator and a professor, globe-trotting to learn about societies near and far, their rises and falls. Yet even though I lived and worked in Prague, as president of a university, not far from the Polish border, I had never set foot in that country, notwithstanding that my paternal grandfather came from there.
Two years ago, however, the annual meeting of the ISCSC was set to be held at the War Studies University, on the east bank of Warsaw. That forced my hand. Now I had to travel there and investigate up close. There are “memory trees” at civil war sites and in national parks in America. I saw them in Poland, sometimes planted to cover up evil and sometimes put in the ground to serve as living urns. Perhaps also, I have become like a memory tree for those murdered at Treblinka, relatives I have never met.
Here is a story that might seem unbelievable. Our late Senior Editor, Carolyn Carpentieri Potter, asked me to print it here, so I am doing so now.
## Faraway Roots
- As my grandmother told the story, it must have been in the warm weather, in the summer of 1894, when word quickly spread through Mława of a momentous event that surely would bring great attention to the town. This might even equal the time long ago that Napoleon slept overnight in the old building down on Szkolna Street. So what if it had turned out in fact to have been only one of his generals? That was nonetheless still an important highlight for the town to rave about. Could other places nearby claim such a distinction?
What amazing news? It was announced by those in authority – and the news spread through town like a lightning bolt – that a member of the royal Romanoff family, a nephew, it was said, was soon going to come to Mława for an official visit. What an honor for the town. After all, Mława was a border town, thirteen kilometers away from East Prussia, one of Germany’s most important states. Naturally, it was important for the Russian nobility to tour the site, to inspect the frontier. (Who, exactly, the distinguished visitor really was I cannot swear, but this is the way I remember the story being told.)
Even though most people in town wished that Poland would be allowed once again to become its own country, sovereign, it could be argued that there were some advantages for Mława now to be part of Russia. Hardly remembered anymore was the major battle Polish soldiers had waged in town against Russian forces three decades earlier, on February 20, 1864, during the so-called January Uprising.
Now, all saw that Russia was a great expanse, a mighty empire, a vast territory covering a substantial percentage of the Earth’s terra firma. And today, a member of the Czar’s family was coming here, from the imperial capital to this small border town.
Town leaders and illuminati gathered quickly to consider how to celebrate this momentous day, how to honor the distinguished royal visitor. There was one suggestion with which all these local luminaries must have concurred right away: we will hold a grand parade, right through the center of town, featuring the great visitor from Moscow.
Soon, people were running hither and yon. Everyone was busy, in anticipation. They were putting up colorful bunting along the downtown streets, marking the route of the grand parade. They were repainting their store fronts, planting flowers, and polishing the lampposts. All would be at the ready, in order, everything would show Mława at its finest, on the wonderful day. Nothing else was talked about, even in the Jewish precincts.
This was going to be the most impressive day in the 19th century for the small city, one which occupied, truth be told, a minor spot on the planet, even if it served as an important railway junction town. Hitherto ignored by most, Mława might become talked about from one end of Europe to the other, suddenly famous, because of this stupendous moment in history. Everyone was agog.
Soon enough, the momentous day dawned. The mayor and council members went to the train station early to await the distinguished one’s arrival.
The reception at the station reminded all that the coming of the railroad, the Vistula Line, fifteen years earlier, had meant that Mława was no longer simply a border town. The opening of the railroad and this new location on the junction of the Polish and German rail lines meant that travelers could travel south, to Warsaw and beyond, or, if they wished, north and west via the German train system. The world had opened for them.
It also drove magnificent economic development in the town. Industrial plants had been built in Mława. Now thriving were a farming tools factory, a vinegar factory, three brickyards, several tanneries, soap-works, breweries, mills, five oil factories, and three soap factories.
The railway also facilitated local trade in livestock, grain, army supplies, and – importantly for my family – trade in wood. Even the Jewish population surged, now freed from the most onerous and restrictive real estate laws. It was approaching in this decade’s count of 4,845, constituting 41 percent of the total population.
Now this momentous day was going to cap off all that progress.
Then it was, this grand morning, that the royal car approached, stopped – and there he was. Off the train stepped the nephew of the Czar, a Grand Duke of the realm, a Velikiy Knyaz, resplendent in all his raiment. First, the local dignitaries saluted. Next, they stood at attention, as Mława’s official orchestra strained to produce the best possible performance of the Russian Empire’s national anthem – God Save the Czar! Then, the Grand Duke worked the rope line, learning each man’s name as he shook hands.
High up in the sky, the sun shone magnificently, with no cloud to mar the occasion. Even the heavens themselves acknowledged the significance of this major event.
From the train station, the Big Man and his welcoming delegation drove together to town, where a delightful breakfast was served to all at a restaurant on the north side of the city’s town hall, next to the church, where today an ice cream parlor occupies center stage. The tables were set in a royal manner to reflect the majesty of the Grand Duke, and all attendees sat down for the meal in their finest clothes.
When the tea and cake were being served, after most of the participants had finished their breakfast meat and potato meals, the mayor arose, praised the distinguished visitor, and rattled off what distinctions of the town he could summon to mind. Everyone agreed that it was a masterful presentation.
- At 9:30, the entourage left the restaurant and went three blocks south, where today the main Mława gas station sits wedged in between several modest stores. The designated Grand Marshal of the Parade then announced the order of the parade: first, the town band again, and it would be featuring patriotic Russian and Polish music.
The entire procession was to halt, as the drum major would signal, precisely at 10:30, in front of the town square which surrounds the ratusz (city administrative building) and the magnificent wooden and stone Mława church, Kościół Rzymsko-Katolicki – which locals called “the cathedral.” Those imposing edifices, with grass lawns and signs stuck in the ground, were surrounded on four sides by lined streets all called “Stary Rynek.” This constituted the central square of the town.
Following the band, there were to be huge horse-drawn carriages, atop which town fathers were perched, able to wave to the crowd. Then, prominent local officers of the military would follow, marching martially and dressed perfectly for the part. Next, at the apex point of the entire parade, the Grand Duke himself would ride on a huge white horse. He was resplendent in his white and gold royal uniform.
The goal: to reach the city square and the newly erected bandstand for speeches at 10:30. At 10:15, upon a signal from the mayor, the band began to play – Russian imperial music, which stirred the soul of any patriot – and then the parade was launched.
Crowds had lined the streets since dawn between the point of departure and the town square, admittedly only a few blocks, but people had come from all over. They had flocked in, especially from the suburbs, but also from the farmlands in all directions, to gawk at the historic sight unfolding in downtown Mława.
Cheers quickly arose from the crowds as the parade proceeded as planned down the Warszawska Street route. The crowd of celebrants was mostly all Gentiles, for Jews tended to stay away from such major organized activities in the town. They had reason to fear unpleasantness developing, especially when people celebrated too much, loading themselves up with liquor. Only the Jewish members of the council were to be seen that day, and they were on stage, with the official party.
Right on schedule, at 10:30, the parade turned the corner into the main square. All were ready for the parade to culminate at the planned climax.
All were to halt, the band to play the national anthem, and the local dignitaries would address the listeners, crowded around the podium, which now featured the famous double-headed eagle of Russia, followed by his eminence, the Grand Duke.
Then the disaster struck.
Someone in the crowd – but where? – shot a stone at the huge white horse atop which the Grand Duke sat, waving to all in the crowd. Frightened, the stallion reared up, vertical now, his shriek and loud whinny deafening, his hooves flashing violence in the bright sunlight, roaring over the martial music that the band was playing, ready to lead into the national anthem. Now, instead, terror and fear ran through the crowd, and it was like the devil himself directing this crowd.
The great horse had reared up at such a tremendous angle and so suddenly that the Grand Duke was dislodged from his saddle. He could not hold on. He lost the reins. Off the rear of the horse slid the Grand Duke, falling at tremendous speed.
The Grand Duke, up until then so magnificent, the powerful symbol in person of the great might of the empire, had fallen to the hard, paved ground of the Stary Rynek with a discernible thud. His martial crown, his swords, his buckles were flying in complete disorder, clattering and then crashing to the ground. People, men and women, raced with alacrity to surround the victim, to view what had taken place.
The local doctor quickly ran toward the body of the Grand Duke, his medical satchel in hand, to examine his unforeseen patient.
“Dead!” pronounced the doctor two minutes later, his arms raised in pain and shock.
In that very moment, the Russian Grand Duke had been martyred – and Mława’s glory, its momentous day on the world stage, was now Mława’s shame. In a flash, this transition, this horrible flip from wonderful to terrible, this awful tragedy played out on the faces of all the onlookers. What a nightmare! What a disaster! The poor Grand Duke. Dead – right here, in front of us, right here in Mława. Right here, on this border between the powerful but once warring foes, the Russian and the German empires, which hardly trusted one another.
It took only a split second for the reaction to set in. Sorrow turned to denial – no, it could not have happened, not here in our town! – and but just a little bit less swiftly, that denial turned into seething anger. Such anger grew that the townspeople manifested it all at once. Social forces rule individual feelings on such occasions. The erstwhile celebrators were instantly preparing to make a transition, to become a vengeful mob.
Who was the culprit? Who had disgraced the town? Where was the malefactor? From what direction had that stone been thrown? How did someone not see it?
People were running around madly, in all directions, shocked by what they had seen, exchanging views with one another. The city leaders, already at the bandstand, were also in disarray. Paper and bunting were strewn everywhere, on the streets, flapping in the breeze. No one knew what to do. No one offered any solutions. The band had stopped playing and just stood still, defeated.
There lay the body of a once-powerful symbol of all-encompassing Russia, the world’s biggest empire. Oh, how the mighty have fallen. But panic was now in the air.
## Jewish “Guilt” Manufactured
This being the segregated city of Mława, all with foresight immediately suspected what would come next. Was Mława in the Polish precincts of the Russian Empire in 1894, or was this a small Southern town in Jim Crow America? Similar processes were at work.
A mob, lynchers, deranged, would coalesce, determined to wreak vengeance on the wrongdoers. And who was the chief wrongdoer? Obviously, one of the Jews. They despise and manipulate us. They sell liquor to our vulnerable peasants. They collect taxes for the hated Russian and Polish authorities. And they killed Christ. Obviously, them.
So, anticipating what was soon to come, it was only minutes after the Grand Duke had been killed in the city square that a dozen or so of the Jewish leaders of Mława joined together, rushing as a single body to the house of the owner of the lumberyard not far from the marketplace and town square, right there on Butcher’s Lane, in the dilapidated Jewish ghetto lane – even if it was a so-called Mixed Street.
“Haim, Haim,” they called out to the owner of the lumberyard. “Haim, Haim, let us come in. Quickly.” So, Haim Dratva, who, like others in his community, had not been at the big goings-on downtown, opened the door to this distinguished assemblage of his fellow Jews.
“How can I help you?” Mr. Dratva asked in consternation.
Never had such leaders ever come to his place of work, and certainly not together, as a group. After all, he was not particularly active in Jewish affairs. He had not held any office in the community. He was merely a businessman, successful, working hard to support his wife and family.
Obviously, he was honored by the presence of these distinguished men, but simultaneously, he was anxious to learn the reason for this sudden gathering of so many respected leaders of the community right here at his lumberyard, all seeming concerned.
“Haim, do you know what just happened at the town square? Have you heard?” “No. I apologize. I was not there. I had to move some new lumber into the back part of the building. So, what is it?”
“Haim, in the parade just now, the Grand Duke was killed. Someone shot a stone at his horse, he was thrown off the stallion, and the doctor five minutes ago pronounced him dead.”
“Oh, that is terrible. I am sorry to hear the news. They say he was the most important man to come to this town in decades.”
“Yes, yes, Haim. But there is more.” “What, Reb Klingier, what more is there to this disaster? The poor town and its reputation, damaged again!”
“Yes. Haim, but there is still more. We are afraid – if history is our guide – that a lynch mob is getting ready to be formed among the peasants, those who have been drinking all morning for this occasion, and the people who hate perpetually. They say it looks like it is just happening now, with those peasants starting to show their wrath right after the tragic accident. Men are angry. They are talking amongst each other without restraint. They say they must look to find out who shot the deadly stone – and punish him.”
“Haim, we don’t know who did it, but it’s surely going to be a Jew who is accused.” “But if they don’t know who did it, why is it going to be one of us?”
“Haim, you have never lost your optimism, which is your nature. Everyone knows it. You have always seen the bright and cheery side of things. But Haim, stop and think for a minute. Do you know where we live? Do you know how much our ancestors, your ancestors!, have suffered in this country? Right here in Mława?”
“So, can’t you imagine what is going to happen next?” “Ok, you are right, rabbi. But why have you gentlemen come to me? I have been here in the lumberyard all morning, working away.”
“Yes, and no one would accuse you, Haim. But everyone in Mława knows that your twelve-year-old, what is his name, Sol, Shlomo in Hebrew, called Karol Salomon by the Poles, is a troublemaker. He always seems out of control. He is a regular pest to everyone.
We are not blaming you and your wife, but last month he got in an actual fight with those hoodlums over there near Priests’ Lane, the ones who are always agitating and instigating conflict with our community, and Sol was yelling for the Jewish boys to join him, to attack the Gentiles back. Not only that, last week, he was fighting with the other boys in the cheder, arguing that his interpretation of some religious point was correct. And a fistfight broke out, Haim. And we know that Gentiles passing by overheard it, they saw it.
“Look. He is the most outspoken and aggressive young person in our community. He is in decent shape, too. At twelve, he is already known to the authorities as a troublemaker. Such a young boy – and so feisty already. He should be studying the Holy Scriptures, not arguing, and fighting all the time. Besides, even if he is an angel, the Gentiles are murmuring, gossiping, they are aroused, and they are going to be out for blood. His blood.”
“Haim, the fact is that no one knows who actually shot that stone, we don’t know, probably some kid from the farms beyond town, all in the huge crowd, but they have to find a Jew who is guilty, and we all think it is likely going to be Sol that the mob will settle on. And very soon.”
“Do something, quick, Haim. Save that boy.” The Quick Route Out
So, in this moment, Haim Dratva heard and understood what the community leaders were saying, and he thanked them for coming so quickly, warning them. What to do?
Mr. Dratva was not poor -- middle class, in a small Polish town way, you might say. So, he went to his cash register and took out all the money he had. It was mostly in rubles, the legal tender of the region since 1842. Mr. Dratva also had some coins there that were denominated in zloty, which had been in use and were legal until a year previous. But few knew that yet in Mława.
He ran upstairs, where the family lived, stuffing the money into his pocket. He gathered them all together and hurriedly explained to the wife and children (it was a big family, with ten children) what had happened downtown at the parade that was being held in honor of the Grand Duke of Russia. They knew about the parade, of course, but none of them had been out of the house that morning.
“Now, Sol, the leaders of the Jewish community have just come to me. The Gentiles are looking for a scapegoat, and they think, son, that it is going to be you. I know it was not you because you were here in the house all morning. But you are going to be accused.
Quick, then! Grab your clothes. Mother, pack him a suitcase – we have that brown and red travel case in the closet there. It is not too heavy. Move quickly, Solomon; there is no time to waste.”
The twelve-year-old was stunned. He had heard what his father, Haim, said. Naturally, he would follow what his father told him and obey. There was no time to cogitate, to meditate on what this meant, to think about what the future held for him. After all, how deeply can a child of twelve consider the future, anyway?
“Sol, you are lucky, in a way. It is almost time for the train to arrive at the new station. Grab your things and let us go.”
So, in mid-summer, 1894, the lumberyard owner Haim Dratva took his young son to the train station, being careful to move through the backstreets of Mława in such a way that nothing would seem irregular to the townspeople, so that the boy would be safe. Within ten minutes, they were at the new station; the opening of this Vistula Line fourteen years ago had changed everything, made Haim’s business possible and profitable, and exposed Mława to a new, far greater world. Mława had become a junction town since it was where the Vistula Line system connected to the Prussian railway network. The ability to go north or south to main locations in both Russian Poland and Germany reduced the once-storied isolation of Mława.
The twice-a-day mainline train arrived no more than fifteen minutes later. It was the so-called international express.
“Sol, boychik, here is money. Put it in your pocket. Now, I know that you are only a child, but you must act like a grown-up. Remember: We have relatives – a good family, your aunt and uncle, the Bishoffs – in New York City, in America. Go to them. Your mother and I love you. Think of us as you travel, son. I am sure we will not be apart for long.”
So, Sol went onto the train, bound north and west, and he sat by himself, waving to his father outside. For safety, his father had written on a sign in Cyrillic and Latin letters the phrase “To America.”
The train pulled out of the station. It was but a few minutes later when the train conductor, dressed in the fancy linen of the Vistula Line, came on his way through the car: “Tickets, Tickets!” Sol sat in his chair, mulling over what had just happened to him. He was therefore startled when the man, this conductor, came to his seat.
“Ticket, please, young boy,” said the conductor. Sol looked perplexed. He did not understand the system. “Huh?” “Ticket, please. You must have a ticket to ride this train. It looks like this.” He showed a sample ticket. “I do not have one. My father did not give me one.” “Well, that is no good. But tell me, son, do you have any money?” “Yes.” “Give it to me, then,” said the conductor. “Ok, here,” said Sol. He took out the whole wad of bills his father had given him just a half hour earlier, all his treasury.
“That’ll do,” smiled the conductor, as he took all the money from the child, far more than what was required for the ticket. He then inserted a punched ticket for the final stop on the journey of this and a connecting train, Berlin, on the metal bar above the boy’s head, and went on to the next passenger.
So it was that Sol, aged twelve, a cheder boy and suddenly a now publicly identified Jewish troublemaker from Mława, a border town that had been the universe to him, but was unknown to others, sat on his seat, looking out the window. The express hurtled north, going faster than he had ever experienced. The other people in the car seemed pleasant enough, although Sol could not understand the language they were speaking. He was broke, but he did not know that. He was alone, but he did not realize that yet, either. He took out the sandwich his mother had packed for him and feasted on it. What an exciting adventure, the boy thought.
Time flew by that day, as did the scenery outside. The land was flatter than he knew. Increasingly, as new passengers got on the train, he did not understand what the others were saying. They were talking in German.
He knew Yiddish, of course, and some Polish, but this sounded strangely off to his ears. This German, however, was like a misspoken version of Yiddish.
By seven in the early evening, as the sun was setting, there was an announcement, and he could understand it. The train was coming to the end of its route. The train entered a station, a huge building with a roof on top. Sol had never seen this type of terminal. He could not get over it. But he could read the sign: Berlin.
Yet where was he? Sol and the other Jewish boys in the cheder had never yet been introduced to the specifics of geography. And then he was told to get off the train: everyone off, the announcement said, this time in that wrongly–pronounced–to–his– ears bad Yiddish. So, he descended from the train car, carrying the one piece of luggage he had.
What a cacophony, this racket. What a perplexing terminal. What a confusing place. Then, Sol saw something that looked familiar. Over there, not too far away, was a group of men, all dressed in black tuxedos. They were Orthodox Jews, and that to Sol was a very welcome sight. He walked to the men. He tugged on the tails of the suit of one of the men to get their attention. They turned around, all five or six of them, and saw the boy. They were speaking Yiddish.
So, Sol, the twelve-year-old from Mława – a place the men surely had never heard of – said who he was. He explained that he had been in danger from a drunken mob of antisemites and that his father gave him money, but that the conductor had taken it all, and he said that he was supposed to go to a place called New York, where he had relatives named Bishoff.
By this point, he was able to disgorge from his mind those points – but not much more. The Orthodox men were touched by the boy’s story, plus his now flowing tears of confusion and sadness, and they resolved on the spot to help him, right there in the Berlin train station.
They walked Sol to the ticket counter. There, they pooled their resources and bought two tickets for him. One would take him by the train to the seaport, and the other, via very cheap steerage, booked him on the boat to faraway New York City. They bought him some food to eat and even gave him some German money for whatever might come.
Thanks to the generosity of these religious men and the good fortune of Sol, he was able to ride the train to the seacoast and, within just a few days, board the huge transatlantic vessel, in the steerage compartment at the ship’s bottom, for New York.
When the boat reached New York, he was directed to the immigration officials at Ellis Island, which had on January 1, 1892, replaced the “Barge Office” in the Battery, which itself had only recently replaced Castle Garden as the reception spot for processing migrants. This twelve-year-old had made the long journey from Mława across the great ocean solo, all by himself.
How was he able to connect with his uncle and aunt, the Bishoffs? Did he have their address? Had his parents written to them? Were the Orthodox men in Berlin so kind as to send a telegram to the Bishoffs with the news and the anticipated arrival date of the boat? The answer must remain speculative. What is known for sure is that the next official New York census showed that a young boy, now named Charles Solomon Drew, was living with the Bishoffs, on storied Park Avenue, a great address in that giant metropolis.
He would never have been found living in the tall apartment building on Park Avenue had it not been for that stone that someone shot at a horse in the caste-ridden, segregated, border town of Mława back during the summer of 1894.
And if one were scarcely able to believe that such events could have happened, that a young kid could have traveled across Europe and the Atlantic alone, what would be harder to believe was that this youngster, this twelve-year-old “troublemaker,” from a poor town thousands of miles away was now ensconced on famed Park Avenue, New York, in the land of possibilities that was America.
Nor would any rational person have believed what unbearable sorrows awaited the rest of his family of origin, back in what was soon-to-be, for a while, free Poland once again.
## Silk and Memory: Upwardly Mobile in New York
In New York, and in a land of equality, at least for most immigrants of that time, the young boy thrived. A teacher in his high school wisely changed his name from Shlomo Dratva to the closest near-equivalent, Charles Solomon Drew. He Americanized as fast as his name was changed. He learned excellent English, and he learned American society’s values and its mores.
Here, there was freedom of opportunity, and a young man with his wits about him could prosper in New York during the first decade of the Twentieth Century. His first step in business matters was to survey the men’s clothing field, emerging now in New York’s famous midtown, the Garment District along Seventh Avenue. He walked that neighborhood, absorbing the social climate and the latest gossip.
The niche he thought might be good to pursue was in silk clothing. He sensed a coming public taste for fine clothing, especially among the well-to-do.
Silk is a filament that a silkworm produces for its cocoon. By domesticating the silkworm moth, the early Chinese had learned as far back as 4000 BCE to unravel the cocoons and then weave the strands together to make cloth. It worked exceedingly well. Such sericulture led to the making of luxury writing paper, a medium for the exchange of wealth, and, especially, luxurious, comfortable, and beautiful clothing.
Following the opening of the trans-Asian Silk Road in 114 BCE, once distant civilizations were connected. They did not clash regarding silk. Knowledge of – and demand for – silk skyrocketed worldwide. Its use declined for a while when, during the Industrial Revolution, Europeans discovered how to use cotton, a competing material for good clothes, which cost much less to produce on a large scale.
However, in America, silk production, which had begun around the time of the War of Independence, took off by the middle of the nineteenth century. The reason: mechanical innovations led to a lowering of the cost to manufacture silk. Agriculture gave way to industrial production by the turn of the century. Not surprisingly, as of 1910, the United States held the lead position worldwide in the making of silk.
This was the Gilded Age. Fortunes could be, and were, made by those endowed with a driving and enterprising spirit, and young Charles Drew was one such entrepreneur. He seized on the possibilities of manufacturing luxury silk goods for men's clothing, such as, particularly, silk shirts. So, he borrowed seed capital from the Bishoff family and utilized the savings he had accrued from working small manual jobs, which he had taken beginning as a teenager.
With this nest egg, he opened his very own small factory, more like a sweatshop than a plant, to which he awarded the impressive name: Yorktown Mills. Soon, his silk shirts, given by the French, and rich-sounding label of Fleur-de-Lis, became all the rage. Across the country, men were demanding these Fleur-de-Lis shirts. The former unruly child from Mława, taking advantage of the trends, was soon on his way to building a solid financial foundation in New York City. In fact, he would become, at least twice in his adult life, a rich man.
He would also lose some of his money twice, as well, by misreading the trends, possibly by having not-so-trustworthy partners, some said.
As this rising industrialist shaped his young life, the next step after starting the company and seeing it on solid footing was to marry. His eyes lit on a pretty young worker in his factory, Rose Sternberg, kindly, charming and smart, and within a few years there were five Drews in the family: Charles, Rose, and three children, all given names that, at least on the surface, did not reveal the Mława origin of the family patriarch. The kids were Bernice, Norman, and Helene. Charles and Rose did not come from New York’s high society, but they absorbed thoroughly their worldviews and lifestyles.
The family lived in Washington Heights, on Audubon Avenue, and their now relative success was on public display. Horses had been displaced by automobiles as of the 1920s in New York, and when the Drews drove along the Manhattan streets in their new luxurious automobile, a limousine, crowds followed them in a kind of parade of hero worship.
As compensation for the lack of acceptance back in Mława, this was a parade of wealth that the patriarch Charles appreciated. His son, Norman, detested it and, in fact, rejected such ostentation from his childhood to the end of his life. It was only natural that the second generation would react this way.
But Norman, who became an optometrist, spurned the trappings of commercial success, feeling his entire life that the poor were more honest than the rich, as they worked at real jobs for a living, and that the simple life was the pure life. Equality came easier to someone who had never experienced the horrors of segregation in Poland.
As the Roaring Twenties rolled onward, there were servants in the home, Rose and Charles had many welcome friends, business was booming, orders were coming in steadily at the factory, and life was grand. Inwardly, there were signs that the road ahead might get rocky; by the end of the 1920’s the stock market crashed, and the Great Depression landed as a dreadful burden on most Americans. But the Drew factory kept producing its coveted silk shirts. The rich kept buying them.
In those days, the wealthier, far more assimilated, mostly German or Sephardic Jews of New York constituted the so-called Yehudim, or “Our Crowd.” They were descendants of those who had arrived centuries earlier, or at least by the 1850s. They now belonged to the Reform temples uptown.
By contrast were the increasing numbers of new refugees from Eastern European oppression, the Yidden. They began to flood America, fleeing persecution in droves after the notorious Kishinev pogrom of 1881, and subsequent outrages in Russia, which had made it almost impossible to survive in most of Eastern Europe. These new Jewish immigrants huddled together, downtown on the Lower East Side, crowded in slums, and they were Orthodox.
The Uptowners and the Downtowners occupied two different worlds, culturally, linguistically, educationally, economically, and religiously. In the eyes of many nonJewish New Yorkers – patricians and others who called themselves “real Americans,” it became imperative to end immigration. The driven masses of the Lower East Side were never going to be able to assimilate, said those stumping for restriction.
On the other hand, it was especially charity, a Jewish variant of noblesse oblige, that united the two Jewish communities in New York; antisemitism, when it appeared, also did so.
How did Charles and Rose, certainly members of the Yidden but, by 1911, far wealthier than most of their fellow recent refugees from bigotry and persecution, manage this problem? Simple. The Drews belonged to two synagogues, one Orthodox and one Reform. They rarely attended either, however.
One success followed another. The family was photographed by the leading society photographer, Bachrach of New York, its prestige since reviewed and analyzed by the Smithsonian Magazine, and there the Drew children were memorialized – young aristocrats of a certain sort, finely attired, acceptable to society.
Popular magazines asked to shoot photos of the royal-looking family, an indication of what was possible in America. The children went to excellent Jewish summer camps and grew up refined, with solid American values.
Charles chose a partner to manage the factory; he preferred to travel America, selling his shirts to fine haberdasheries, including, as his wife Rose liked to point out, meeting Harry Truman in his store long before the Missourian went on to fame and national politics. Perhaps Charles, based on his childhood drama, felt the need to stay on the move. But he wasn’t the most cautious of drivers, though, for the family stories usually ended with him saying: “And what size shirt do you wear, officer?”
The late Prof. Digby Baltzell of Philadelphia was widely hailed as the leading academic expert on the American Upper Class, which flourished during the late Nineteenth through the end of the Twentieth Century.
A brilliant, best-selling scholar who popularized the term “WASP,” for white AngloSaxon Protestants, he authored books such as The Protestant Establishment: Aristocracy and Caste in America (1964, 1987); Philadelphia Gentlemen: The Making of a National Upper Class (1958); Puritan Boston and Quaker Philadelphia (1996, 2nd ed.); and The Search for Community in Modern America (1968).
His works chronicled the rise and explained the fall of America’s “upper upper” elite class, the Brahmin aristocracy, as a leadership group for society. These books and arguments are taught in university classes on social stratification to this day.
Prof. Baltzell had earned some fame early in his career by comparing those found in Who’s Who with those named in the Social Register, the national black and orange books that since the early years of the Twentieth Century have claimed to list high society in major metropolitan areas of the United States.
One day in class, as he was discussing the sociological meaning and impact of the Social Register, he happened to mention that Jews had long been excluded from the realms of
the establishment, including being listed in the Social Register. That was not possible. Further, he said, wealthy, nonestablishment Americans, including Jews, could not buy their way in, either.
In the next class, I brought in a copy of the 1927 Social Register for St. Louis. There, on the top of Page 39, was listed Mr. and Mrs. Charles Drew of Audubon Avenue, New York. When we broke up the apartment of our grandmother, Rose Drew, I picked up the book that no one else wanted. And now it had illuminated a fascinating datum.
Prof. Baltzell was amazed and said that while he had heard about such a thing, he had never actually seen it before.
What it told me was that a boy who, at age twelve, had to run away from a lynch mob in his home in a backward region of Poland, because of antisemitism and segregation, had lived the American dream. This was physical proof of the benefits of American social mobility, no matter how high a bribe he had paid for that listing!
## Horror Comes to Mława
Meanwhile, Charles Drew did not forget his Mława hometown, the place of his birth, and he was able to make the journey back there once, in 1920. It must have been a visit with mixed results.
Since the busy manufacturer was a limited commodity, he could not stay for long. He would have been happy to see the progress in Mława, though, because his hometown was also doing well in 1920. But it is likely that in part, he had also visited the town to encourage his siblings to join him in New York, to pass through the golden door to acceptance and success in the New World. But in that effort, he was not successful.
The year before his brief return home had seen the emergence of a new independent Poland, free from foreign domination, and a thriving Mława. In Mława, the number of Jews rose to 6,301; in early 1939, it reached about 6,500, one-third or more of the overall population. Most Jewish males were employed as craftsmen, small traders, or businesspeople; one indicator of improving economic times was that there were now two flourmills and one sawmill owned by Jews. The community was actively continuing the practice of helping those in need.
A bilingual (i.e., Polish and Hebrew) co-ed Jewish gymnasium, supported by municipal funds, had been built; it soon received official accreditation. In addition, there were several other schools for the Jews, including, for a while, a college where the Talmud was studied. New libraries were founded, there was a Hebrew printing house, and periodicals in Yiddish were published during the 1920s. Social and political life flourished, and competing Jewish movements formed.
It was after Charles returned to New York, during the next decade, however, that ominous clouds began to appear. Did they reflect the Great Depression or political troubles in Europe? Did they portend threats to the recent instances of social progress?
In Mława, an increasing number of ugly anti-Semitic eruptions broke out. It signaled a sudden reversion to bigotry not seen for a century.
Starting in 1937, guards were positioned at Jewish stores to prevent Christian customers from entering, and some shops owned by Jews were closed, to be replaced by new shops targeting Christian shoppers instead. Jews in the city felt increasing ostracism. The situation in Mława was not unusual; it reflected what was occurring throughout the length of the newly independent Poland, where even pogroms were becoming increasingly common.
In August of 1939, Polish army units, including some with Jewish soldiers, were moved into Mława to guard the border town. Germany was only 13 km. away. The Poles were concerned.
The Nazi regime in next-door East Prussia, Germany, had grown steadily more hostile; its aggressiveness and militarization alarmed many residents, some of whom were moving from town, richer Jews, for example, relocating to Warsaw. A number headed east for safety, to the border with the Soviet Union, not far away.
So it was that despite the Polish army’s presence along the border, the Germans attacked Poland early on September 1, 1939, commencing World War II. After bombing Mława that very morning, they occupied it two days later. But the center of the city had already burned to the ground; no water was left to extinguish the fires.
On October 8, Mława was incorporated into the Reich; Reich territories were required by new German law to have no Jews resident. So, local Jews were quickly rounded up by the new German rulers, placed on trucks, to be expelled to the Soviet Union, but at the last minute, the convoy was halted, and the people were allowed to return to their homes.
How do we know what happened in the town once the Nazis arrived? In Israel, a yizkor (memory) book on Mława was published in 1984.
It is one of the great many of this genre. Aging survivors have typically written these drafts, to preserve for posterity remembrances of a once-thriving, former civilization and its settlements.
The relevant book here was entitled Mlawa Ha-Yehudit; Koroteha, Hitpathuta, Kilayona --- Di Yidishe Mlawe; Geshikte, Nyfshtand, Unkum. In English translation, it is Jewish Mława; Its History, Development, Destruction (Mława, Poland).
If you want to read it online, go to https://www.jewishgen.org/yizkor/mlawa/Mlawa.html.
Other sources about Mława during this period include an excellent writeup in the Virtual Shtetl at https://sztetl.org.pl/en/towns/m/584-mlawa/99-history/137690-history-of-community. See, also, The Encyclopedia Judaica, Volume 12, Pages 186-187, with a nice bibliography.
## From Terror and Repression to Public Execution
Assaults on the Jews immediately began, right after the invasion. Men were attacked on the streets, especially those religious men with beards. They were tortured, beaten with batons, and sent to hard labor. Soon, all Mława’s Jews were forced to wear clothing patches, with white ones mandatory on top of yellow ones. The label “Jude” was to be sewn on their clothes, front and back.
Within the month of September 1939, all Jews were banned from walking on the sidewalks or visiting the parks. Jews were forced to pay a fine. The synagogue and two Jewish houses of study were set ablaze on purpose by the Nazis, the German soldiers forming a ring around the buildings to prevent the Jews from trying to put out the fires.
Then, a ghetto was created in December 1940 along Warszawska Street; the few houses there, already crowded, were soon packed with other people, families that were forced out of nearby towns and thrown into Mława.
Gradually, most Jews were reduced to starvation. Many of the able-bodied were also made into forced laborers. A slave labor camp was set up in the town. Then, the first deportation of Jews took place; three hundred were sent away.
Gentile residents of this town, as in many others, were aware of developments, and many did their best to protect and save their Jewish friends and neighbors. Home Army leaders, such as Dr. Joseph Witwicki, Dr. Michał Łojewski, and Dr. Adrian Laskowski, harbored Jews in their homes, at notable risk to their own lives.
Others from the National Armed Forces, gendarmes and security guards, and women who were Home Army spies, in the ghetto and outside it, all helped their Jewish fellows. In addition, at least a dozen families supported the Jews with food; it was provided secretly by Stanislawa Zajcowa, a grocery store owner, and Maria Szubertowa and her employees. Christian religious leaders helped to save the lives of Jewish children by issuing them baptismal certificates.
One special hero was the pre-war head of the Bank of Mława, Artur Pienkiewicz, who helped the Jews and was sent to a concentration camp, to be killed, as punishment. The worst came as a lull ended early in 1942. A prelude to the final roundups and destruction was the public hangings of the Mławan Jewry. Then came the total deportation and murder of the community by the Nazis, arranged in stages.
On January 23, the Germans arrested 25 Jews, accused them of being smugglers, and sent them to their deaths in concentration camps. That night, Eliezer Perlmutter, the much-respected leader of the Judenrat (the Jewish Council, created by the Germans), a hero who had kept matters from getting out of hand, was arrested and murdered by the authorities, right there in the municipal court. This marked the beginning of the end.
Public executions started on April 18. Four young men were lined up and then hanged downtown, before a crowd of witnesses, by the Gestapo, ostensibly for treason, smuggling. The very next day, the theoretically powerful head of the Jewish police, David Davidson, along with his subordinates and several members of the Judenrat, were all deported to Auschwitz, destined to perish.
## All Sent Away to Be Killed
On June 4, the final horror began. The Nazis forced all the residents of the ghetto to the town center.
They then led in a line of thirteen handcuffed Jews, including eight police officers, two prominent women, and three other men, all with rope nooses tied around their necks, to be hanged.
The audience was ordered to remain silent and watch. Then, to heighten the agony, several of the Jews were forced to remove the boxes from below the victims’ feet, thus executing them. Cries, screams, and protests broke out in the town square. Their order breached, the Germans fired into the crowd of forced onlookers, killing several.
The Germans later announced that “on June 4th, during the execution, the Jews presented an arrogant and aggressive behavior.” So, in response, the Nazis were to execute 50 more Jews of Mława. They arrested one hundred Jews without work permits, tortured them, and forced them to dig a wide trench.
Then they divided the victim group in half, sending the older ones temporarily to prison. On June 17, however, they paraded into the city square, both the youngsters and the older ones.
A group of the first five young Jews were lined up, denied masks, and shot on the spot, to be followed by the next five. When the Nazis had finished with all the fifty murders, public assassinations really, they ordered the fifty older Jews to bury the youngsters in the ditch which the teens had been forced to dig before they were shot. And the elders were instructed to bury the youngsters under the earth, whether they were dead or still alive. Schnell! Schnell! Hurry! Hurry!
On October 13, seven more young people under the age of twenty were murdered. Then, in late October and early November, all the Jews who had been working outside in labor camps or private farms were returned to the ghetto. Everything stopped suddenly. German police officers began patrolling the borders of the crowded ghetto nonstop.
All wondered: Why? Soon, the answer became evident.
The Judenrat, such as it now was, was forced to draw up a list of 2,000 sick and elderly. On November 10, these individuals were sent to the departure point. Included in the deportees were the rabbi and the elderly and sick from nearby towns. They were not allowed to bring any luggage or carry-ons. All were sent to their deaths in Treblinka.
Then, the end of Mława’s Jewry came in four waves. On November 13 and November 17, 1942, there were two additional deportations, this time to Auschwitz.
To increase the frightening theatricality, the candidates for deportation were now forced to run fast at the flour mill; those who did not run as quickly as the Germans wanted were murdered on the spot or put up against the mill wall and shot. The Germans then stripped the Jews and robbed them of their money before sending away those who had not already been killed.
After that, only several hundred Jews were still alive in town. They were deported – part of a collection of up to 7,000, including people not originally from Mława – in two separate convoys to die in Treblinka.
The last group was sent to their deaths on December 10, 1942.
By the end of 1942, the final, terminal year for the Jews of Mława, all had been murdered, most at Auschwitz or Treblinka. The ghetto was liquidated. Hundreds of years of history ended.
## Supernova Out of the Shoah
We know that the life of aging stars in space culminates in a supernova explosion, a gigantic burst of light. For Mławan Jewry, that happened two years later, in 1944. It brandished forever, I would argue, the pride of the community, enabling the Jewish community to die with dignity.
That star was a Jewish young man, Czeslaw Mordowicz, the son of a Mławan grain merchant and an actor. Unbelievably, incredibly, he joined in Auschwitz Birkenau with three other prisoners, Slovakian Jews, to pull off what the Jerusalem Post has called “the greatest escape in human history.”
The chances of success for the four plotters were minuscule, if not less than that. The Germans were fanatic in their attempt to squelch any information from reaching the Allies about what constituted the greatest camp for organized murder in world history. As a result, they built rows of barriers, including almost endless deadly impediments, huge guard towers, and electric fences, all designed to render a prisoner’s escape from Auschwitz-Birkenau impossible.
The four men knew the dangers, of course; they had even witnessed prisoners being hanged in public for making the attempt. But these four possessed exceptional and thorough knowledge, plus they were intelligent, brave, cautious, and strong. These qualities informed their meticulously planned scheme, and they knew how to keep their mouths shut.
There were two goals that compelled them to take the risk in the spring of 1944. First, they needed to inform the outside world of the details of what Auschwitz really had become – a place to murder off all the remaining Jews of Europe. Second, they wanted to prevent the commencement of what was to be an Eichmann-led huge and sudden systematic action to murder the last remaining major Jewish European population, the Hungarians, a process that the four knew conclusively was ready to unfold.
So first, after hiding for several days underground, overnight on April 7, Rudolf Vrba, only nineteen at the time, and Fred Wetzler, barely twenty-five, escaped in a complex maneuver taking several days to get beyond the outside perimeter. Second, on May 27, followed the other two in their group: Mordowicz, then aged twenty-three, and Arnost Rosin, the oldest of the four, at 30.
A miracle! All four made it out alive, three Slovakian Jews and one Mławan Jew. This was a first for the Jews of Europe.
Keeping their wits about them, they defied rivers, mountains, dogs, starvation, and Nazi informers. All four arrived alive in Slovakia, starving, ill-clad, stumbling over the border into this officially independent, but actually Nazi-collaborationist country. They were able to make their way quickly to the leadership of the remaining Slovakian Jews.
There, all four joined in spilling out the facts of Auschwitz, drawing maps, recounting the methods of murder, and listing actual numbers of prisoners, including the elimination of the Jews by their lands of origin. They dictated their report to Oscar Krasniansky of the Slovakian Jewish Council. Vrba and Wetzler took the lead as they laid the groundwork for the report.
Then, the newly arriving Mordowicz and Rosin followed up, confirming what the first two had reported and adding that, already, between May 15 and May 27, a total of 100,000 Hungarian Jews had arrived at Auschwitz, with most gassed as soon as they had set foot there.
Their completed report, known as The Auschwitz Protocol, was typed up, translated, and snuck to the Hungarian Jewish leadership and to the West by a variety of stratagems. By good fortune, it worked. The report would wind up on the desks of Winston Churchill and Franklin Roosevelt. Attempts had been made to squelch it by antisemites who denied or refused to believe it, but the mass media got hold of a copy, and the major facts made Page One throughout the Allied world. The world had been alerted, and the Hungarians had, too. Best of all, an estimated 100,000 lives, Hungarian Jews, were saved because of the acts of these four courageous Jews and their report.
Yet, very sadly, terror was not yet over for Czeslaw Mordowicz. Three months after his escape, in August of 1944, a band of Slovak patriots tried to revolt.
It did not work, of course, but it did lead to a full-scale Nazi military invasion of Slovakia. People were rounded up. Unfortunately, young Mordowicz was among those arrested by the Germans.
He was sent to Auschwitz again.
En route, he later recounted, he warned the others in the car that they were being taken to their deaths. It would be best to try to jump out right away, he said. But the resulting noise of the passengers only attracted the attention of the guards, who beat him. Cleverly, in the railroad car Mordowicz chewed off the tattoo from his arm, the one with the prisoner number inscribed.
Amazingly, it worked. Upon arrival at the deadly siding in Auschwitz, the SS failed to recognize him.
Fellow inmates at Auschwitz, however, did, and one of them tattooed a flower right over where the original number was. His life was saved, and he was sent to another camp, and then liberated with everyone else.
The story of this brave cohort was recorded for posterity. Czeslaw Mordowicz gave testimony in Yiddish at Yad Vashem, in Israel, in 1965, found at https://collections.yadvashem.org/en/documents/3556474 and in a long set of interviews to the United States Holocaust Memorial Museum in 1995 and 1996, in English https://collections.ushmm.org/search/catalog/irn504847.
A true, brave, and brilliant hero, a son of Jewish Mława, he died a free man, a hero, in Toronto, Canada, in 2001. But the world of the Jews in Poland had disappeared, perished.
After liberation, it was found that some forty local Jews had survived the concentration camps. Counting in those who had fled successfully to the Soviet Union, probably no more than 150 Mława Jews were still alive anywhere.
The first Holocaust survivors returned to Mława in late 1945, about sixty at most. Mordowicz did not do so, knowing, he said, that his entire family had been murdered. But these 60 were confronted now with a new threat: violent anti-Semitic Polish gangs were wandering around the city, threatening everyone with murder, and it became so dangerously bad that two Jewish officers of the Soviet forces, stationed there in order to protect the townspeople, provided trucks to escort the returned Jews to safety, out of town.
A sad final note about these kind and thoughtful men: For their action, the two Red officers were labeled Zionists by their Soviet bosses and removed from the army. One was deprived of his pension, which had been scheduled to vest in two months, and the Antisemites that hated them saw to it that he was left to die impoverished and miserable.
In town, a very few of the population stayed despite the danger. In August of 1946, after burying what corpses they could find, the last contingent of the Jews of Mława left the town. After several centuries of existence, Jewish Mława had been extinguished.
## Will The Circle Be Unbroken?
When I learned in the Spring that the 2023 Annual Conference of the International Society for the Comparative Study of Civilizations was scheduled to be held at the War Studies University in Warsaw during September, I knew I had to go.
As an active member and as an officer of the body, I was expected to attend the sessions and speak, naturally, at the university during those sessions.
But no one from the Drew family had ever been to Poland since Charles Drew had paid his brief visit in 1920. None of us had ever seen the town from where the family rose, the place where that stone was shot a century and a half ago. Instinctively, I knew I had to take this opportunity to see this site, even though I had resisted doing so in the early 2000s, when I was a university president living not far away, in Prague.
Now, however, I felt that I could not go to Poland and avoid seeing Mława. There was a drive to learn, but more than that: to close a yawning gap.
Why?
From my parents, I inherited a love of classical music and sacred music. And, from my six years at YMCA Camp Chingachgook on Lake George, then at Columbia where I sang in the St. Paul’s Chapel Choir (Episcopal), and finally as a result of years of participation in the world of Civil Rights and the African American community, I have loved Protestant hymns and spirituals, both of the white and African American varieties. In fact, much of the basis for the character of hymn-lovers, I would argue, arises from the words, the libretto, with the music implanting it in the brain.
Singing in the choir at Columbia exposed me to values I have carried all my life since. Once to Every Man and Nation Comes the Moment to Decide. Rise, Crowned with Light, Imperial Salem Rise. Come, Holy Spirit Heavenly Dove. Activity in the Civil Rights Movement exposed me to Lift Every Voice and Sing. Oh, Freedom. We Shall Overcome. Adding this to my Israeli hymns, such as Hatikvah, Mi Shebeirach, and the Prayer for the State of Israel – and you have my soul.
So, what went through my head as I thought about this upcoming trip to Warsaw? It was the old hymn: Will the Circle Be Unbroken?
Will the Circle Be Unbroken, By and by, Lord, by and by? Will the circle be unbroken? There's a better home a-waiting. In the sky, Lord, in the sky.
The hymn parallels comparative civilizational studies. Many famous writers, from Ibn Khaldun to Spengler to Toynbee, have seen history as cyclical, with rise and fall. Had my family’s circle been broken when Charles left Mława, or when the Jewish people of the town, including his family members, were deported to death camps, and murdered? Could the circle be completed, made unbroken now?
Could the unsettling past now be re-entered, rise to a new level of conclusion, rise above, transcending and incorporating all, and then be ended, not left hanging?
Could a civilization and a family history be peacefully put to rest?
How would I feel walking on the ground of the Old World, including land that had been trodden over, in blood, by my recent relatives? What would reconciliation mean? Could it be that we arrive at a coexistence with the motto: Never to Forgive, Never to Forget?
My father had volunteered to fight in the Navy during World War II to get back at the Nazis, and I had volunteered for Israel in 1967, to preserve what had been achieved there. But those acts did not negate the fact that there was unfinished work for us in Poland. A broken circle had to be completed, an issue put to rest.
So it was that on the morning of Tuesday, September 12, 2023, my good and devoted wife Shakun, my Columbia Journalism School friend Carolyn Carpentieri Potter, and I met Agnieszka Couderq in Warsaw. She was there, with her car, to drive us that day to Mława and on the next day to Treblinka and to her cottage on the River Bug.
The trip to both places had been kindly arranged by her thoughtful stepfather, Prof. Andrew Targowski. My friend Dr. Targowski is a heroic Polish American, from a distinguished family of Polish leaders and patriots. He and his family personally suffered from both the Nazis and the Soviets; he says he was executed two times by totalitarianism.
First, his father, a diplomat, was hanged at a Nazi munitions site for sabotage after a fellow prisoner informed the Germans.
Then, his house in Warsaw was set on fire as the Germans fought the Russians near the end of the war; he survived because his nanny made him run outside the burning house and, bullets flying everywhere, she threw him down and covered his body with hers. She died, but he survived. Finally, the Polish secret police came after him in Michigan and tried to murder him at high speed on the highway.
Now the former president of the ISCSC, he recently retired as a professor at Western Michigan University and lives in Los Angeles.
His stepdaughter, Ms. Couderq, whose grandfather died in Buchenwald, grew up in Michigan. So, in addition to Polish and English, she also reads Chinese (she took a master’s in the subject), and she has recently written a fascinating history of Michał Bohm, 1612-1659, a Catholic priest from Poland who represented the Pope in the late Ming / early Qing China.
Bohm may have helped convert the emperor’s family to Christianity, traveled back to Europe via India, and then returned again to China. Her book is now being serialized for television.
She kindly agreed to guide us, in her car, through history and through what is now the north central part of Poland.
Once we cleared the traffic of Warsaw, we took Highway E-77 north. What a beautiful, modern highway, at the level of our interstates. Two hours later, signs appeared, indicating that the exits for Mława were approaching. More than one exit as well. Amazing!
## NATO and the EU in Mława: A Make-Over
As we approached Mława by car, there stood a row of huge rotating electricitygenerating horizontal-axis wind turbines. This was a jolt, as I was expecting to see something out of Fiddler on the Roof, not a sign of modernity, not progress such as one expects to come across only in an advanced industrialized country.
Was the trip also going to reveal a different Mława from what I had anticipated?
Mława today has about 30,000 residents. We drove in and stopped at a gas station. I realized that the gas station was located about where that parade had set out for the town square in 1894, propelling my grandfather on his journey to America. It was also just below where the Jewish community had lived under segregation before that. And it was, as well, close to the tiny area into which the Nazis had shoved thousands of Jews before killing them all in 1942.
We parked on the main square and gazed in admiration at the cathedral. Next to it was the town’s administrative building, with lawns on which were placed signs that told of the history of Mława.
To absorb some of the town’s mores, we walked around, the weather being comfortable, weaving in and out among cars which occasionally stopped for the pedestrians to pass, and which sometimes indicated in so many ways that we were rudely jaywalking outside the proper crosswalks. The buildings were modern, as were the streets and cars. The people were well dressed, middle-class-looking, and peaceable.
None of this squared with my anticipations. There were no signs of the Shoah, the Holocaust, nor any obvious reflections of the vanished Jewish world. We went into an ice cream parlor and pastry shop, straight across Stary Rynek from the town administrative center.
But in my mind’s eye, I was seeing River City, from The Music Man, not the segregated Mława of 1894. Having grown up in Upstate New York with the same population, I felt at home.
The patrons of the ice cream parlor cum bakery were mostly teenagers, spending time chatting in abandon after carefully choosing from the panoply of exotic flavors and fancy cupcakes on offer. It reminded me of Squire’s in South Glens Falls or DeLong’s Dairy in Glens Falls in 1952.
Could I absorb and accept this? Were these pleasant Mławans the descendants of those drunken racist peasants that for decades, for centuries, had harried the Jews, driven them in and out of the place, accused and murdered them for Desecrating the Host, made their lives so restricted and so unpleasant, segregated them, kept them in misery?
I had studied up on the history of Mława, a royal town looted by the Teutonic Knights during the sixteenth century and later burned by Swedish troops, throughout fortified in bigotry. Was this really that place?
In the 1700s, the 1800s, and part of the 1900s, it was the same geography, right here! But it was two totally separate places. There was the gentile half of Mława, and the Jewish half, two civilizations, on top of each other but a world away from each other, lived in the same geography but on a different level of consciousness.
As to Jewish Mława, we were in a place that still exists geographically but no longer socially. It is not a site you can find on any contemporary anthropological map of the region’s significant cultures. You can go there, I realized, but you cannot unearth it. You can see its indicia, its reflections, but not it. Not anymore. These days, it is a question of misplaced concreteness, a logical fallacy in which the manifestation of something is mistaken for the thing itself.
For centuries, a strange and powerful European caste system enveloped the Jewish society, enclosing, shaping, containing, and denigrating it, and them.
Now, it was simply a lovely setting of friendly ice cream parlor patrons that I saw sitting there. I could not square the differences, and I concluded they could not be squared. Social evolution has occurred. Change in its rawest form presented itself. Polish society had improved, and I was only a visitor here, a tourist.
I was not home.
Thomas Wolfe was right. You can’t go home again, not if it is Poland and not if you are a Jew. But that was not the fault of these charming young teenagers, chatting above their ice cream.
In Israel, the very first time I landed there, I felt immediately at home, welcomed, familiar. I did not speak the language, but it felt like home to me. It was a place to which I belonged. Here, in Mława, despite centuries of history for my family, I was only an outsider, looking in at a very pleasant sight, but as a visitor, nonetheless, NATO and the European Union be praised.
After the stop at the ice cream and pastry shop, we walked over to the Archives. The building was impressive, just a stone’s throw from the main town square, on the other side of Warszawska Boulevard. I had written to the archivist about my intention to visit Mława on this day, and see the archives, but no answer had been forthcoming. Wrongly, I assumed that they were not interested. On the contrary, we found out, they were – it is just that they did not speak English.
At the Archives, Ms. Lampkowska and Mr. Karwowski greeted us warmly; they were more than happy to help us. Ms. Lampkowska brought out a book of Mławan records through which she had searched.
Thousands of Jews had lived in this place over the centuries, and the Dratva family must have numbered in some minor way, at least, among them. Yet, the only indication she could find was of the recorded death of a woman whom I concluded, from what my brother had written, was our grandfather’s grandmother. Well, that was not much, but it was something; we took pictures of the open page of the book and of the archivists.
They were all genuinely nice to us. They showed me a letter, prominently displayed in a glass case in the main library, of David Ben Gurion, who came originally from Płonsk, close to here. That was great. He was always a hero of mine, from childhood, the real founder and builder of Israel, no enemy to the Arabs but simply the creator of a once in two thousand years Jewish state. Long live the builders of social justice!
We saw where the Nazi ghetto had been. It was now simply a small park. You could not imagine how thousands had once been packed into this space.
We left the small city -- a nice, pleasant setting along a contemporary major superhighway, passing those huge windmills again.
The train still runs through Mława, making a stop, always ready to carry passengers to distant locations. You can see it.
But you cannot see or hear the social past. What about the screams of the people of Mława being forced to watch Nazi hangings of their friends and neighbors, the murder and starvation of these decent people who were guilty of no crime, the forced transportation to their gruesome deaths: These cannot be seen but cannot be forgotten. Never to forgive, never to forget.
## The Place They Were Murdered
The next day, Agnieszka dutifully showed up again with her vehicle, as she had promised, and we took off this time for the northeast, not the northwest. We took another highway, another interstate, the road E67, toward Białystok. Destination: the death camp of Treblinka.
One turns south at Ostrow Mazowiecka and then passes through Malkinia Gorna, crossing the River Bug. This river was important as a dividing line when the Soviets and the Nazis agreed to slice up Poland in 1939. It is not so big, really, as history would seem to have made it. The town is important, too, as the last settlement where people traveling to their deaths at Treblinka would have passed.
Soon, you see a sign from your car window: Muzeum Treblinka Niemiecki Nazistowski -Treblinka Museum. German Nazi Camp of Extermination and Labor Camp. Then, you turn off the main road.
My advice is not to do it. Do not go there.
To me, it is the real, the true not fictional — and the lowest bolgia, in Dante’s Circles of Hell, but actually on Earth, not in a novel. “Abandon all hope, ye who enter here.” You cannot forget Treblinka. You will always remember those executed there, and the inhuman racism that motivated it.
The place is set amidst a forest of trees. If you did not know better, you would think you were somewhere in the Adirondacks. To those from where I grew up, it looks like the scenery on the road south of town, on the way to Fenimore. Tall, straight, thick forests of trees, shooting up to the sky, a pleasant sight for the eyes.
Then you realize that these beautiful trees are pallbearers. Pallbearers. What in the United States are often called “Memory Trees.” The Nazis planted them in 1944, on purpose, to cover up their evil. Their shame, their disgrace.
After all, it was here and at the other murder and concentration camps all across the Bloodlands and the killing fields, in the ravines of Ukraine, in forests of Vilna, and all around Europe, in this archipelago of sadism and death, that the Germans and their allies descended to the lowest point in the history of civilized people, the Death Valley of Homo sapiens who have walked this planet for over 100,000 years.
More than in Mława, you must strain when looking at this sight to get past the pleasant present and to realize what went on here. You must close your eyes, if you have read what went on here, to hear the screams piercing the air, to see the whips lashing and the dogs biting, walking flesh, and to feel the horror those innocent victims felt at their approaching doom in gas chambers.
This place was designated by the post-war Polish government in 1964 as a National Monument of Jewish Martyrdom. We must be careful to blame the Nazis, and only them, for this depravity; they are the ones who were in the end responsible, even if others collaborated.
The murder site, Treblinka was originally constructed in 1941 as part of Operation Reinhard, the Nazi plan to assassinate around two million Jews. Treblinka was meant, along with Belzec and Sobibor, to be a factory dedicated to killing people.
Treblinka II, the murder site, was operated from July 1942 to September 1943 and then plowed over to cover up, disguise what had been done. So today, not much remains. A small museum lays out the map of the former extermination camp (named Treblinka II), the former forced labor camp nearby (Treblinka I), and The Way of Death, which led there.
After hours, often days, of trying to survive in a packed, constricted, airless cattle car on a train carrying about 6,000 people each, roughly 800,000 to 925,000 doomed Jewish men, women and children (and several thousand Roma), none of whom were given death certificates or burial plots, were forced to jump off the rail cars.
Immediately after getting off the train, people went to the gas chambers. There was no tattooing, no huts with wooden bunks, no louses, not even any harsh labor. From the start, three gas chambers were in operation, with dimensions of four-by-four meters and a capacity of 300 to 500 people an hour. In September 1942, a further ten gas chambers were added, with a much greater capacity. They allowed between 1,000 and 2,000 people to be put to death in an hour. https://www.holocaust.cz/en/history/concentration-camps-and-ghettos/treblinka-3/
In fact, up to 15,000 people per day could be murdered, the head of the camp later testified.
Our visit on that Wednesday began with a stop at a railroad siding near the town of Treblinka, about four kilometers away from where the camp was. It is not the real railroad siding but a guess as to where it was.
On a pillar is inscribed a poem, The Little Station Treblinka, written by Wladyslaw Szlengel, a poet later killed during the Warsaw Ghetto uprising. Part of it reads:
There is no cashier Not a baggage man Not for a million will you get A return ticket...
On debarkation from the trains, there was chaos . Sadistic guards sometimes grabbed toddlers by their feet and smashed their heads against a wall. Those who objected or resisted in any way were beaten to death right there. People’s heads were cut off, or their eyes gouged out, their noses slashed off.
Leaving the siding, we parked at the museum entrance. At the front of the drive, a pile of stones, with an Israeli flag, had been planted by recent visitors. I walked the Way of Death. And then came the “Tube,” where the people, all stark naked, were driven, five across, into the gas chambers.
Guards with whips hurried them on, the dogs sometimes biting off parts of their bodies, the victims heading unknowingly toward doom and not the “showers” inscribed above the gas chamber doors.
When the memorial to the victims was built, it was a symbolic cemetery of 17,000 stones, centering on a main monument. On many of these stones are written the cities and countries from which the victims came. I went on alone, standing amidst this vast stone memorial, treading atop the soil that was a holy relic.
There I talked in my mind – communicated – to the hundreds of thousands of dead, to those especially those of my Mława family who, I imagined, had been executed right on this spot, whose ashes were below me now. If not for that thrown stone in 1894, I could have been trampling on top of my own ashes.
I told them that we remember them, that they had not died in vain and that we had a Jewish country today, a flag, an army, a navy, and an air force to defend our people.
In accord with the sign painted in various languages right next to the big monument, I affirmed “Never Again.”
Also, I silently recited the words of Hatikvah. “Our hope is not yet lost, the hope two thousand years old.” For fate and justice had swiftly contrived to create a living memorial for these victims, after but five years, one that still survives: Israel.
Later, I was told that most of the people, Gentiles, who live in that region have never been to the site of Treblinka. They do not consider themselves responsible, they do not want to think about the horrors that played out there, and they are not interested in paying homage to these martyrs. It is not an easy conclusion, but in the end, a fair person must ask: who can blame them? After all, two million non-Jewish Poles died at the hands of the Nazis, too.
The Jewish Pale of Settlement, the archipelago of the Jews, in the Bloodlands, was murdered off. The vanished, ejected, are never to be seen again, disappeared. The witness trees and the giant, silent rocks at Treblinka, though, speak for them, taking their place as their representatives: they say that these people once were present and walked this Earth.
Concomitantly, we can look with equanimity upon the young people of Poland today, in their ice cream parlor; after all, they are innocent, not responsible for the Holocaust nor for the unpleasant events that may have happened there in the past.
But, at the same time, what is also true is that we are not Polish. We are Jewish Americans. We are not from Mława. I am from Glens Falls, which Look Magazine declared “Hometown, USA” in 1944. It was nice to visit your town, Mławans, to honor my antecedents at the archives, to walk where my ancestors did, but the United States is “the land where my fathers died” – and my mothers, too. And in fact, even on one side, a great-grandparent is buried up in Maine.
Here, at Mława and Treblinka, I felt that the circle of the Drew family’s sojourn had now been completed, the circle made unbroken by this visit.
Farewell, killing fields of the east.
## From Civilization’s Failure
This, then, is a story of how over 130 years ago, a stone thrown by someone unknown in a small city close to the Prussian border in what is now Poland led a young boy to journey across land and sea to America. Renamed Charles Solomon Drew, this highspirited youngster was able to adjust quickly and assimilate, climb from the poverty of his oppressive birthplace up a ladder of social mobility to American success, and bequeath to his family bountiful opportunities and American lives.
Had that boy not been driven from Mława all those years ago, his body would have wound up with his fellow townsmen, ashes under foot at Treblinka or Auschwitz, a victim like so many others of Nazi perfidy. Instead, the Drew family has followed a wonderful trajectory, only possible in America. In our case, the arc of history has bent toward liberty, justice.
## Final Reflections on this Story from Andrew Targowski and Joseph Drew: Forgetting European Ghosts in the 21st Century?
Looking back from the vantage point of today, it is hard not to see eerie resonances between the petty absolutism of small-town authority in Mława of the late 19th century and the currents coursing through modern Europe. The ghosts of abusive power, the arrogance of office, and the suppression of truth in favor of convenient fictions—these did not vanish with the fall of empires.
They echo today in new, and worsening, forms.
What is especially troubling is the resurgence of ultraright political movementsparties with unmistakable fascist overtones—gaining traction across Europe. In Germany, Poland, Slovakia, and even in France, we witness the rise of voices promising purity, control, and national rebirth, but also offering division, fear, and historical amnesia.
These developments suggest that the lessons of Mława — or of Treblinka — are fading from collective memory.
To recall the story of Mława, and the extinction of its Jewish society, is to be reminded that freedom and justice are fragile, and that memory—especially painful memory—is not a burden but a defense. It may be that we are all, in some sense, becoming “memory trees,” living monuments tasked with recalling the truths others may seek to bury.
What will Europe, Western Civilization, remember—and what will it choose to forget?
