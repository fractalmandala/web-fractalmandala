Medieval German legends regarding the gold of the Rhine have both a mythological source, as the gold of the Nibelung dwarf Andvari, and a historical Burgundian one, as a hoard possessed by the Burgundian princes. Here we point to more ancient sources of these legends, ones that are to be found in the cosmological myths and rituals of the Āryans.
## The Gold
The gold of the Rhine appears in several mediaeval German texts, and it is famously presented in the operatic masterpiece of Richard Wagner, Der Ring des Nibelungen (1876). The first association of the gold and the ring shaped therefrom, in the Eddas, is with the Aesir (Old Norse for the principal gods) Odin.
In the Poetic Edda, ‘Reginsmál’, we find Odin mentioned in relation to a theft of the gold of the Rhine from the Nibelung dwarfs. Odin, Hönir and Loki had once lodged with Hreithmar and received his hospitality. However, later they come upon Otter, a son of Hreithmar’s in the form of an otter, and the wayward Loki kills and flays the otter. Thereafter, Odin and Loki are forced by their host Hreithmar to fill the skin of the otter with gold as recompense.
Loki returns to the original guardian of the gold, the Nibelung dwarf Andvari/Alberich, who gives him all the gold except a ring, which however Loki finally extracts from him. It is important to note here that the net that Loki uses to gather the gold is obtained by him from Rán, the wife of the sea god Aegir.1 When the gods proceed to fill the otter’s skin with gold and set it on its feet, they find that there remains a whisker that needs to be covered, and this is achieved by inserting the ring into the skin. But Loki warns the gods:
The gold is given, and great the price Thou hast my head to save; But fortune thy sons shall find not there, The bane of ye both it is.2

In fact, tragedy ensues quickly thereafter. Fáfnir, a brother of Otter’s, decides to obtain his share of the gold given to his father Hreithmar by Odin and, when Hreithmar refuses to do so, he kills his father and appropriates all the gold. To securely guard this gold, Fáfnir assumes the form of a dragon. Regin, the third son of Hreithmar, who has brought up the young Sigurd/Siegfried, encourages the latter to kill the dragon Fáfnir, which Siegfried does, though he finally kills Regin, as well.
The gold of the Rhine then reappears along with Siegfried within a Merovingian/Burgundian historical context. Siegfried’s story is recounted in the Poetic Edda — in poems such as ‘Fra Dautha Sinfjotla’, ‘Fafnismol’ and ‘Sigrdrifumol’ — as well as in the 13th century Nibelungenlied. From these texts we gather that the Burgundians, led by Gunther, are themselves considered as Nibelungs, though the Andvari family of the Odin story were considered as dwarfs.
Siegfried wins for Gunther the princess Brünnhild of Iceland and Gunther rewards him for this by granting his own sister Kriemhild as Siegfried’s bride. Siegfried, overjoyed, declares that he will give Kriemhild all the gold that he had obtained from the dragon. But Kriemhild taunts Brünnhild by saying that it was Siegfried who, in disguise, consummated her marriage. Brünnhild complains to her husband Gunther of this deception and the latter decides to avenge his bride and obtain the ‘Nibelung hoard’ that Siegfried had presented to Kriemhild. This he accomplishes by having Siegfried killed by the Burgundian vassal Hagen. But the Nibelung hoard itself is sunk into the Rhine to prevent further misfortunes.
While the Nibelungenlied continues the legend of the gold of the Rhine in a historical context, it is the original references to this phenomenon in the Poetic Edda involving the god Wotan/Odin that we must focus on to determine the significance of the gold hidden in the river.
This significance will emerge more clearly when we study the cosmological bases of the most ancient Indo-European mythologies. For this reason, I may briefly repeat here the reconstruction of ancient Indo-European cosmogony that I presented some years ago in my study Ātman: A Reconstruction of the Solar Cosmology of the IndoEuropeans:3
At the end of the first cosmic age, the supreme Soul (Ātman/Shiva), desirous of creation, acquires through the fervor (Agni) of his desire an ideal macroanthropomorphic form as the Purusha (Man).

From the nostrils of this macro-anthropos emerges the life-breath of the deity, Vāyu,4 in the form of a boar5 which recovers the Earth sunk at the bottom of the cosmic ocean during the flood that brought the first cosmic age to a close. The boar/Vāyu then impregnates and spreads Earth, producing, as a result, extended Earth (Prithvi/Gaia) and its “cover” primal Heaven, Brahman (Dyaus/Ouranos/source of the later Āditya) in a closely united complex.
However, the temporal concomitant of the rapidly moving Wind-form of the supreme deity, Shiva/Kāla/Chronos, separates the united Heaven and Earth by castrating the Purusha, since the phallus is the instrument of desire as well as of life. The semen that falls from the castrated phallus of Heaven impregnates the Purusha bound to Earth with a Cosmic Egg, called in Sanskrit Hiranyagarbha (the Golden Womb), from which then emerges the manifest cosmos constituted of Earth, in the form of a lotus, crowned with a Heaven of divine Light and Consciousness (Brahman/Helios). The ruler of this primal cosmos is Chronos.
This manifest Purusha, or macro-anthropos, crowned with effulgent Light (Brahman/Ouranos) is, however, shattered by the persistent stormy aspect (or ‘son’) of Chronos  Zeus / Indic Ganesha  and forced to descend into the nether regions of the “lotus” Earth. Zeus/Ganesha, however, preserves the castrated phallus of the ideal Man (containing the life of our yet unmanifested universe) by swallowing it.6

We see, therefore, that there are two distinct stages in the evolution of our cosmos, the first marked by the formation of the ideal macro-anthropomorphic Purusha, which constitutes the primal cosmos ruled by the Titan Chronos, and the second marked by the rise of our universe from the sunken Heaven, Dyaus, in the nether regions of Earth.7 This universe is ruled by the god Zeus. The divine forces that rule in our universe are related to those that ruled the original cosmos even though their operations and effects are different.
Of the several divinities including the Titans of the primal cosmos and the gods that rule our universe, the one that we need to focus on in a study of the gold of the Rhine is Ouranos/Varuna, who sinks to Earth as the Lord of Earth surrounded by the endless Okeanos.
This primal divinity (known to the Romans as Uranus) is not a sky god, as many Indologists have unfortunately envisaged him, but the original golden light of the Cosmic Egg (Skt. ‘Hiranyagarbha’ = Golden Womb) that continues to be possessed by Ouranos/Varuna in its fallen condition in Earth. This is made clear by the Iranian cognate of Varuna, Xvarenah, which, unlike Varuna, has the special attribute of being a golden glory that is derived from its original cosmic source.

Further evidence of the identity of Aegir and Varuna is to be found in the figure of Aegir’s wife, Rán, who captures drowning men in a net and is, as we have seen, the one that Loki gets a net from to gather the gold of the Rhine. Varuna himself, as the Lord of Earth — that is, of the material matrix and not of our planet — is frequently characterized as holding a net, the Varuna Pāsha, which ensnares souls to corporeal manifestation.
We perceive now that the gold of the Rhine is in fact related to the tragedy of incarnation, a tragedy that the gods too are afflicted with as they are all destined to die in the Ragnarök.15 This is in contrast to the Indo-Iranian — as well as Osirian, Dionysian and Christian — sublimation of the misery of the manifest world through the rise of the sun in it.

## The Dragon
We may next consider the significance of the dragon that guards the gold of the Rhine. The original serpent that the Germanic dragon is derived from is clearly that which is combated and killed by Indra/Zeus/Thor and is called variously Vrtra, Typhon and the Midgard Serpent.
In the Germanic Prose Edda (“The Deluding of Gylfi”), the slaying of the serpent is represented in the battle of Thor against the Midgard serpent, which is, in the end, consigned to the ocean around earth, which it then encircles.
The root of the Yggdrasil tree that reaches to Niflheim also harbours the serpent Nidhögg, which must be related to the Midgard serpent.
The same serpent is killed by Siegfried in the stories related to him in the Edda as well as in the Nibelungenlied. The serpent, however, has the dragon form of Fáfnir, son of Hreithmar and brother of Regin and Otter. We see thus that Siegfried has assumed the role of Thor in these 13th century texts. The serpent of the Abyss/Okeanos is transformed into a dragon of the Rhine and the golden luster of Xvarenah is celebrated as the gold of the dwarfish Nibelungs. The name of the Nibelungs is then transferred, along with Siegfried’s gift of the hoard, to the Burgundians, who were ruled in the 12th and 13th centuries by the Swabian Hohenstaufen, who were called Wibellingen.16
More light is shed on this serpent in the Indic literature where, for instance, in Bhagavata Purāna V,25,1, the serpent Sesha in the underworld upon which the Lord reposes is described as being the tamasic, or Māya-associated, aspect of the supreme Lord that sustains this universe by the magical effect of sympathy. In the Indian system of Kundalini Yoga, we obtain a clearer understanding of the psychological significance of this serpent. The Kundalini serpent (which is analogous to the cosmic Vrtra) is represented in the microcosm as the force of vitality as well as of sexuality coiled at the base of the spinal cord.17
The aim of the yogic discipline is, as Cook puts it,
…to awaken this sleeping force and get it to climb the spinal tree, piercing the various spiritual centers (chakras) along its way, until finally it is released [like Brahman from atop the petals of the lotus in the Puranas or the sun from atop the sycamore in Egypt] from the Sahasra Chakra, the Thousand-petalled Lotus, at the top of the head.

The sublimation of the serpentine force marks the rise of the soul, Ātman, to its original brilliance as the divine Consciousness, Brahman.
In the Vedas, Vrtra is a serpentine cosmic phenomenon represented as being located within a turbulent wind. Vrtra is a demon of resistance which prevents the “mountain” from ejecting its life-giving seed. In Krishna Yajur Veda II,5,2, Vrtra is called Vrtra because “he enveloped these worlds”.19
Indra is the hero chosen by the gods to defeat the dragon, Vrtra, when all the Ādityas, Vasus, Rudras and gods were paralyzed by the monster (Rig Veda 10,48,11). Indeed, Indra’s freeing of the waters from the restriction imposed on them by the dragon Vrtra is associated with the creation of our heaven and earth, which are formed out of Vrtra’s body (Rig Veda I,36,8).
The slaying of Vrtra not only forms heaven and earth out of the latter’s body but also allows the elevation of the sun to the mid-region between them: “As you, Indra, killed Vrtra with power, you raised the sun in heaven to be seen” (Rig Veda I,51,4).20 In the Atharva Veda IV,10,5, the sun is said to be “born from the ocean, born from Vrtra”. According to Rig Veda X,121,7, it is Indra’s deliverance of the waters from the grasp of Vrtra and their subsequent outflow which allow the waters to give birth to Agni (i.e., his third form as Āditya the sun). The vital solar energy, too, rises from the depths of the Abyss in this flood, since Indra/Soma is said in Rig Veda IX,42,1 to engender the sun in “floods” along with the other stars.
## The Golden Otter
Finally, we may attempt to decipher the significance of the stuffing of the gold of the Rhine into the skin of the slain Otter in ‘Reginsmál’. For this we must turn to the literature related to the fire-rituals of the Indo-Āryans.

These rituals are based on the original sacrifice of the Ideal cosmic macro-anthropos, Purusha, as well as on the repetitions of this sacrifice in the manifest cosmos and in the underworld, for it is these sacrifices that result in the formation of the sun.21 The primary aim of the Vedic ritual is to restore the disintegrated Purusha and, especially, his solar energy.
As Gonda pointed out regarding the construction of the Vedic fire-altar:
In building the great fireplace one restores and reintegrates Prajāpati [Purusha], whose dismemberment had been the creation of the universe and makes him whole and complete. At the same time and by means of the same ritual act, the sacrificer, who is identified with Prajāpati (cf. Shatapatha Brāhmana VII,4,15) constructs himself a new social personality and secures the continuance of his existence.22
One of the most important of the rituals of the ancient Indians is the Agnicayana, which revives the original golden form of the Purusha/Prajāpati and facilitates the rise of the sun in our universe.23 The first rites of this ceremony are dedicated to the engendering of Agni as the elemental solar fire. This is done through an initial sacrifice of five forms of animal life, starting with a human.
These five victims also symbolize the five layers of the altar that is to be built during this ceremony (Shatapatha Brāhmana VI,2,1,16).
The fire-altar is constructed in five layers as described in Shatapatha Brāhmana VII,4,1-VIII,5,1. A lotus leaf representing Earth is placed in the center of the altar site. The lotus leaf is imagined as being surrounded by waters, ‘for the ocean swells around this earth’. On the lotus leaf is placed a gold plate that the sacrificer has been wearing for a year. This plate represents the orb of the sun. On it is placed a golden man representing the Purusha finally manifest within the sun (SB X,5,2,6):
He is Prajāpati, he is Agni, he is the Sacrificer. He is made of gold, for gold is light, and fire is light; gold is immortality, and fire is immortality. It is a man (purusha), for Prajāpati is the Man.
During this ritual Purusha recovers his golden form. The sacrificer sings over the golden man to transfer his virility or semen into it (SB VII,4,1,24). The identification of the yajamāna with the golden man stresses the identification of the sacrificer with Agni as the Purusha.

Then the Purusha sāman is sung and the laying of the bricks is begun, the first brick being the svayamātrrina (‘the naturally pierced’) placed on the golden man to allow him to breathe. Macro-cosmically, Purusha's feet represent earth, legs intermediate space, waist the mid-region, chest intermediate space, and head heaven.24 The lowest level of the altar in which the image is embedded represents the Svarloka (Heaven), the third level the Bhuvarloka (the Mid-region of the stars), the fourth level Brahman and the immortal regions, and the fifth the Bhurloka (Earth). However, although vertically the altar represents the Purusha, horizontally, it represents the solar force in the form of a sunbird with outstretched wings facing the east. The fire-altar in the form of a sunbird that may fly to the heavens25 represents the phallic force of the Purusha that generates the sun.
After the completion of the five layers, the altar is sprinkled with gold-chips to confer a golden form to the “body” of the Purusha (SB X,1,4,9) as well immortality on Agni (SB VIII,7,4,7). Layers of soil are then scattered in between the bricklayers to represent the Purusha's marrow, bones, sinews, flesh, fat, blood, and skin (SB X,1,4). After the generation of Agni as the sunbird Āditya in the form of the fire-altar, Agni is led to the fire altar (SB IX,2) and installed there (SB IX,3).
The aim of the Agnicayana ritual is the flight of Āditya, the sun, to the heavens.26 The sacrificer too is thereby borne by the altar, or the sunbird, to heaven. However, the sacrificer does return to the earth after his journey to the other world. As Tull points out, the purpose of the construction of the fire-altar is “to reunify man’s material being with the essential aspect of existence and thereby regain the original state of wholeness.”27
It is evident that the golden man of this Vedic ritual as well as the final golden form of the Purusha and the sunbird represented by the fire-altar are the source of the gold-filled skin of Otter in the Eddic story of ‘Reginsmál’.
The legends of the gold of the Rhine are indeed Germanic transpositions of the cosmic mythology of the fallen Ouranos/Varuna/Xvarenah within the material matrix of Earth enveloped by an ocean (Okeanos). The dragon that Sigurd/Siegfried kills is the serpent Vrtra/Typhon, whose identity is correctly preserved in the Thor stories of the Edda but not in the Siegfried ones. The otter filled with gold in the ‘Reginsmál’ is a reminiscence of the golden man that is a principal part of the restorative ritual of the Agnicayana among the Indo-Āryans.

## Epilogue: The Migratory Routes of the Ancient Indo-Europeans
For a quick comparison of the migrations of the eastern and western branches of the Indo-Europeans we may start with the first coherent list of Indic gods that appears in the treaty of the West Asian Mitanni-Hurrian king Šattiwaza and the Hittite king Šuppililiumas I dated from the fourteenth century B.C.E. which includes the names Mitra-Varuna, Indra, and Nāsatyas,
The text (CTH 51 and 52)28 reads “Dingir Meš Mitraššiel, Dingir Meš Uruwanaššiel, Dingir Meš Indar, Dingir Meš Našattiyana.”29 Since Indra and Naonhaithya came to be considered as demons by the Zoroastrians, clearly this document predates the Zoroastrian reforms. The Mitanni were present in northern Syria and southeastern Anatolia since around the 16th century B.C.E.
We may also note that the Hurro-Akkadian version of the Lord of the Waters among the Mitanni is ‘Uruwana’ or ‘Aruna.’ In the Vedic Gopatha Brāhmana, I,1,7, Varana30 is indeed the secret form of the name Varuna,31 and this repeats the penultimate vowel of both (Mitanni) “Uruwana” and (Greek) “Ouranos”. The form “Aruna” is perhaps related to the Hittite term for “ocean” “arunas,”32 which exhibits the vowel-shift of “a” to “u” that resulted in the Vedic Varuna.
The presence of Indo-Āryan cultural elements in later Mesopotamia is attested also by the occurrence of the divine names Mitra, Gishnu [Vishnu]33 and Sūrya [as ‘Suliaat’, from Hittite-Hurrian ‘Suwaliyatta’]34 in the Sumerian god-list, which, though dating from Assyrian times, around the seventh century B.C., must reflect a more ancient list of Sumerian deities translated into Akkadian.

The fact that some of the esoteric names of the Indo-Āryan deities, Agni (as Agri) and Sūrya (as Ud), are possibly of Sumerian origin suggests that the Sanskrit language itself may have been fully developed only after the establishment of the Sumerian Uruk culture (dating from the fourth millennium B.C.E.).
Nevertheless, it is to be noted that the later Āryans of India designated the Dasyus or non-Āryans, as Anagni, the fireless. The reference in Manusmrithi X:43-45 to “the Dravidas, the Kāmbojas [Persians],37 the Yavanas [Ionians], the Sakas [Scythians], etc.” as Kshatriya races which have sunk to the level of Shūdras on account of their neglect of the sacred rites and the authority of the Brāhmans suggests that Brāhmanism, though based on the spiritual insights of a proto-Dravidian race that included the Semitic Akkadians and the Hamitic Sumerians, was formulated by the Indo-Āryans as an exclusively fire-worshipping cult.
From 2200-1700 B.C.E. evidence of typical Indo-Āryan settlements emerges in the Bactro-Margiana Archaeological Complex (BMAC). The BMAC is not far north of Mundigak, where from 3000 B.C.E. we notice extensions of Elamite culture resembling that of the Indus Valley.
It is difficult to determine whether the Āryan settlements of BMAC represent a continuation of the early Elamite Hurrians of Mundigak or are new immigrants from the Sintashta-derived Andronovo culture associated with the Indo-Āryans (1800-900 B.C.E.).38
