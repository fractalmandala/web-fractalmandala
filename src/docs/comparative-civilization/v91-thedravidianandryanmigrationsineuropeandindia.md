Much has been written about the relationship between the Indo-Āryans and the Dravidians. Debates have proliferated on the question of whether the Āryans invaded Dravidian India or whether they were indigenous to Dravida. If we consider the literary evidence of the ancient Indians we can inform a different view of the origins of Indian Brāhmanical religion and the historical relationship between the two major population groupings of India.
## Satyavrata, King of Dravida
In the Bhāgavata Purāna VIII, 24, 13, the survivor of the flood, Manu (the counterpart of the biblical Noah), is called Satyavrata, King of Dravida. In the Matsya Purāna, too, Manu is described as practicing austerities (tapas) on Mt. Malaya, which is normally identified with a mountain in South India.1 Mt. Malaya may also have been the name of a mountain outside India associated with the proto-Dravidians.
In the Babylonian history of Berossos, the boat of Xisouthros (corresponding to the Sumerian Ziusudra, the Babylonian Atrahasis2, and Utnapishtim of the Gilgamesh epic) lands in Armenia. According to Nikolaos of Damascus, a contemporary of Augustus,3, the Armenian mountain on which the boat landed is the Baris mountain. The Baris mountain may be the same as Mt. Ararat (north of Lake Van) mentioned in the biblical Flood story of Genesis 8:3. According to Berossus, the Babylonians moved to different parts of Babylonia from Armenia.4
In the Ethiopian Romance of Alexander, the Brāhmans were called the sons of Adam’s son, Seth5.

Noah was considered to be a transmitter of the wisdom of Seth.6 Since Adam is indeed the Cosmic Man (Purusha) of the Vedic scriptures and not the first man (Manu/Noah), we may assume that the Brāhmans referred to here are associated with the preservation of the Divine Consciousness of Brahman which arises from the Cosmic Egg (Hiranyagarbha) and is later conveyed to humanity by Manu/Noah.
Noah himself is described in Genesis 10 as having three sons: Shem (the Semites), Ham (the Hamites), and Japheth (the Indo-Europeans). Josephus declares that Seth strove after virtue and left descendants who imitated the same virtues. All of these, being virtuous, lived in happiness in the same land without civil strife, with nothing unpleasant coming upon them until after their death. And they discovered the portion of science regarding the heavenly bodies and their orderly arrangement.7
Josephus identifies the land of Seth as being located around “Seiris,” which is also the land of Noah, who is said to have preserved the wisdom of Seth. In the Christian Opus Imperfectum in Matthaeum of Pseudo-Chrysostom, the books of Seth were supposed to have been hidden by Noah in the land of Šir. The so-called “cave of treasures” in which they were hidden is identifiable with Mt. Ararat.8
Further, in Genesis 14:6, the Horites, or Hurrians, are identified with Mt. Seir; thus, we see a close identification of the proto-Hurrians with the proto-Dravidians of the Bhāgavata Purāna (BP), where Manu is King of Dravida. The Hurrians, who spoke an agglutinative language — like the Dravidians and unlike the Āryans and Semites — are attested to have lived in south-eastern Anatolia and upper Mesopotamia from the third millennium B.C. and are associated with the Mitanni kingdom of the 16th c. B.C., where the first written evidence of the Sanskritic language and religion emerges.
The Brāhmans, who are considered “sons of Seth,” originally constituted the priesthood of the proto-Hurrian/proto-Dravidian population.9 It is most likely that the Noachidian race was a proto-Dravidian one, and it is probable that all the yogic knowledge that informs Brāhmanical religion is derived from it. BP VIII,14,3 states that the role of a “Manu” is to maintain the cosmic order at the time of the creation of the universe.

So, we may assume that Dravida and its king, Satyavrata, represent the first fully enlightened men. We have seen also that the Brāhmans were considered the “sons of Seth,” the son of Adam. Since Noah is a descendant of Seth, and since Noah is the same as Manu, who is a Dravidian “king” in the Bhāgavata Purāna, we have here a confirmation of the proto-Dravidian origin of the Brāhmanical religion.
In this context, F.E. Pargiter maintained that Brāhmanism itself was not originally Āryan but rather was adopted into Indo-Āryan religion by the Dravidians.10 However, Pargiter did not consider the possibility that both Āryan and later Dravidian may have been derived from a proto-Dravidian/Hurrian spiritual culture. Pargiter suggested that the Dravidian “Brāhmanical” institution was also considerably transformed by the Āryans.
While the original [proto-] Dravidian priesthood was characterized by the practice of yogic austerities (tapas), which gave them magical powers, the Āryan religion was preoccupied with the performance of sacrifices, especially revolving around the worship of fire.11 The Indo-Āryan religion, thus, seems to have combined the ancient proto-Dravidian wisdom of the Elamite/Mesopotamian Hurrians with more northerly fire and soma rituals as well as horse-sacrifices.
## The Dravidian Migrations
In identifying the proto-Dravidian people, the writings of a now controversial (long seen as racist) German scholar, Ernst Haeckel, who wrote Natūrliche Schöpfungsgeschichte (The History of Creation),12 Volume II are referenced. Unlike other early and similarly bigoted anthropologists who focused on the shape of the skull or the color of the skin or language in their attempts to distinguish the different human populations, Haeckel considered language and hair formation as the best indicators of human types.
Haeckel focused on the formation of hair and distinguished three major types of humans: those with woolly hair, those with straight hair, and those with curly or wavy hair and strongly developed beards.
Of the first group, he claimed that there are four types. Of the second group, he delineated five types.

Of the last, he asserted that there were three types: Dravidians, Nubians (located in Nubia in North Africa as well as in Central Africa), and the Mediterraneans, who included the Basques, Caucasians, Hamites and Semites, and the Indo-Germanic peoples.
Clearly, if this writer was correct, it is the last group that we may identify as Noachidian. Regarding the Dravidians, Haeckel thought that they exhibit characteristics of both the straight-haired and the curly-haired people:
The Dravidian (Homo Dravida) seems to stand very near the common primary form of the Euplocomi (curly-haired) and perhaps of Lissotrichi (straight-haired). At present, this primeval group is only represented by the Deccan tribes in the southern part of Hindustan, and by the neighboring inhabitants of the mountains on the north-east of Ceylon.
But in earlier times, this group seems to have occupied the whole of Hindustan and to have spread even further. Perhaps Haeckel’s inclusion of contrary hair types here was due to a confusion of the original Dravidian grouping with the diverse aboriginal tribes of India, with which it might have become mixed over time.
Haeckel also considered the Nubians as a variant of the Semites. If we follow Haeckel, the Nubians, in the northeast of Africa, may be regarded as an offshoot of the primeval Semitic tribes, who migrated far across central Africa, almost to the western shores.
As for the Indo-Germanic language family, it separated at a very early period into two diverging branches, the Ario-Romaic and the Slavo-Germanic branches.
Out of the former branch arose the Arians (Indians and Iranians), on the other the GrecoRoman (Greeks and Albanians, Italians, and Celts). Out of the Slavo-Germanic branch developed, on the one hand, the Slavonians (Russian, Bulgarian, Czech, and Baltic tribes), on the other, the Germani (Scandinavians and Germans, Netherlanders and Anglo-Saxons).
Haeckel considered the entire Mediterranean grouping as having originated in India and wrote that they moved westwards. The principal portion of the latter population, he claimed, was the Mediterranean branch, which migrated from their primeval home (Hindustan?) to the westward, and they peopled the shores of the Mediterranean, southwestern Asia, northern Africa, and Europe.
For a different homeland of the original Dravidian population, we may consider another important work on the extensive migrations of the ancient Dravidians.
This is N. Lahovary’s Dravidian Origins and the West.13
In this pioneering research, Lahovary identified the Dravidians as part of the Mediterranean, which he considered as being the original inhabitants of the ancient Near East “in its largest meaning.”
By that, he meant the people who lived in “Anatolia, Syria, Palestine, Caucasia, Persia, and Mesopotamia  with its extensions towards India, as well as Arabia and the African regions facing Arabia, i.e., from the Nile valley to the high tablelands of East Africa.”14
Thus, Lahovary’s Dravidians, or the Mediterranean people, included the Dravidians, Nubians, Hamo-Semites, Basques, and Caucasians of Haeckel’s Mediterranean but not all the Indo- Germanic tribes - only the Caucasians, Iranians, and Indians. Lahovary did not discuss the northern European members of the Indo-Germanic tribes, whom he considered as having overrun the Dravidian people, especially in Europe.
As regards the diffusion of the Mediterranean/Dravidian people, Lahovary remarked that:
It was from this world of Asia, where the foundations of civilization had been already laid, that the bearers of the neolithic and chalcolithic civilizations of the Near East spread, by successive migrations, in general of relatively small groups, over a period of more than three thousand years, first towards Northeast Africa, and later, during the fourth, third and second millennium, towards Europe.15
The colonization of Europe by the Mediterranean people, which lasted for centuries from the fourth millennium onwards, was propelled by the Anatolian and North African branches, according to this writer.
The first agricultural European civilizations, the Danubian and the Thessalian, were due to the immigration of the Anatolian people. From these groups are derived, according to V. Gordon Childe, the neolithic civilizations of Southern Russia, Germany, and Northern Italy.16

Lahovary describes the physical appearance of the Mediterranean people:
From the Nile and Anatolia to western India, and perhaps further still to the Ganges valley, you had the same brownish-white, long-headed population with a long, straight, and narrow face, a narrow nose, and a pronounced chin, slim limbs, of gracile and small build. In pre-dynastic Egypt and in a great part of India, we meet with still another variety of this type, perhaps a little more primitive, the so-called Eurafrican or proto-Mediterranean type.19
He takes care to add that:

Unlike Haeckel, Lahovary believed that the ancestors of the modern Dravidians of India emigrated from Anatolia and Mesopotamia to India sometime over the fourth millennium.21 Lahovary noted, in this context, the important linguistic fact that the central and western Dravidians, such as those in Kannada and Telugu, seem to have remained closer to Basque and the ancient non-Aryan languages of southern Europe than Tamil and Malayalam.

“At the same time, lexical and even morphological correspondences with Semitic have been retained in several central Dravidian idioms which are lacking in South Dravidian.”22 This confirms the north-south route of the Dravidian migration into India and dismisses any theories of an indigenous Indian origin of the Dravidians.
In this context, we see that the earliest archaeological evidence (ca. 1200-80 B.C.) of the entrance of the Tamils into South India is from dolmen burial sites in Adichanallur, like those in Palestine and Cyprus. Some of the finds, such as golden “mouthpieces,” bronze representations of cocks, and spearheads, may be related to the worship of Muruga/Marduk.23 The megalithic graves of the Madurai district dating from around 1000 B.C. also reveal resemblances to the early Iron Age graves of the Caucasus and Sialk Necropolis B.24
Thus, the civilization of South India exhibits a close affinity to that of Southern Europe, just as the languages of North India are related to those of Northern Europe. As Lahovary put it:
If North India partakes of the beliefs and languages of ancient Northern Europe, whence the name of “Indo-European,” we see that South India, where pre-Aryan India best survives, has quite as strong ties with Europe, but this time with Southern Europe, where a more ancient civilization was transformed and later submerged by invasions of warlike peoples from the North.25
It is not certain that the arrival of the Indo-Āryans in India was marked by warfare, but the cultural difference pointed to by Lahovary is certainly an important one.
## The Āryan Migrations
As regards the Iranians and the Indo-Āryans, who are, unlike the Dravidians, characterized by their fire worship, it should be remembered that this fire worship is employed in a religion that formed the basis of the solar religions of Mesopotamia, Anatolia, and Egypt as well.
Thus, in the Sumerian religion, the chief solar god is equated to Girra, the fire god (in an Assyrian exegetical text)26, and Ra in Egypt is the same as the solar force, Agni.

Therefore, it is possible that the adoration of the solar force as divine fire may have been an integral part of the original proto-Dravidian yoga-based religion that was shared by Semites, Japhetites, and Hamites. The actual fire rituals may have been preserved more carefully by the Japhetic stock, a population that had migrated at an early date northward to the Yamnaya and Andronovo cultures. They moved southwards again later, in the second millennium B.C., towards northern Mesopotamia (as the Mitanni dynasty), Iran, and India.
Many tribes of the Indo-Germanic family-maintained fire worship. For example, fire worship is also observed among the Prussian-Lithuanian cult of szwenta (holy fire), as well as among the Greeks and Romans, who maintained a cult of Hestia or Vesta.27
Plutarch (Numa, II) informs us that:
Numa is said to have built the temple of Vesta in circular form as protection for the inextinguishable fire, copying not the fire of the earth as being Vesta but of the whole universe, at the center of which the Pythagoreans believe fire to be established, and this they call Hestia and the monad.
The Scythians, too, worshipped a goddess called Tabit, whose name is probably related to the Sanskrit ‘tapti’ denoting heat. However, fire worship has been found to exist in an advanced form only among the Indians and the Iranians. In this context, we must first recall Herodotus’s statement that the Iranians, indeed, did not worship fire originally.28
In the Purānas, too, Purūravas, the early Aila29 king, was reported to have obtained sacrificial fire from the “Gandharvas,” who also taught him the constitution of the three sacred fires of the Āryans.30 The Purānas report that Purūravas was an Aila king of Pratishthana. The Ailas themselves are designated as Karddameyas; this relates them to the river Karddama in Iran, particularly in the region of Balkh.31
Fire worship was thus perhaps not universal among the earliest Iranian Āryan tribes.

The fact that the Purūravas are said to have learned the fire rituals from the Gandharvas suggests that the early Hurrians of Elam and the earliest Iranians did not worship fire and that they learned it from a more northerly wave of Āryans who must have, at a very early date, moved northwards from their Armenian homeland.32
However, even the Gandharas are included among the Aila [=Elamite?] dynasties in the Purānas, which suggests that these Āryans, too, were a northern and eastern branch of proto-Hurrians identifiable with the Japhetites.
The Japhetic tribes that moved northwards to the Pontic-Caspian steppe created the Yamnaya culture there,33 and are considered the major source of the Āryan tribes.34 The eastward movement of proto-Dravidians-Hurrians with Elamite forms of the Brahmanical religion may have encountered the more northerly fire-worshipping Gandaridae tribes moving south to form the typically Indian branch of the Āryan family.
The Gandaridae are mentioned by Herodotus (III, 91) as one of the Indian tribes of the seventh satrapy of Darius I (550-486 B.C.) and can be located near the Bactrians of the 12th satrapy. The archaeological evidence of the early Gandharvas may be found in the Gandhara Grave culture of the Swat settled from 1700-1400 B.C., which followed the Bactro-Margiana Archaeological Complex (BMAC).
From 2200-1700 B.C., there is unambiguous evidence of typical Indo-Āryan settlement in the BMAC. The BMAC is not far north of Mundigak, where from 3000 B.C. onwards, we notice extensions of Elamite culture resembling that of the Indus Valley.35

However, it is difficult to determine whether the Āryan settlements of BMAC represent a continuation of the early Elamite Hurrians of Mundigak or whether we find this group to be new immigrants from the Andronovo culture associated with the Indo-Āryans (1800-900 B.C.).36 The latter is indeed the more probable.
The Andronovo culture associated with the Indo-Āryans is itself derived from the Hut Grave and Catacomb Grave culture of 2800-2000 B.C.37 and from the Sintashta culture of the southeast Urals (2300-1900 B.C.),38 which is marked by chariot burials, and which may have been proto-Āryan rather than proto-Indo-Āryan.
I would argue that the fact that there is unmistakable evidence of fire-worship in the BMAC and little evidence of it in Mundigak suggests that the former is derived from the Andronovo rather than from the Elamite colonies. Elaborate fire altars are evident in the ruins of the BMAC complex, which correspond to the Āryan fire-sacrifices. The temples also contain rooms with “all the necessary apparatus for the preparation of drinks extracted from poppy, hemp and ephedra” that may have been used for the somarituals.39
The Bactro-Margiana Archaeological Complex, thus, may have been the center of cultural contact between the Iranian peoples of Mundigak and the later Indo-Āryans. It is interesting to note, too, in this context, that the Avesta (which is geographically centered in eastern Iran) mentions the Māzanian devas as worshippers of the Indian gods. According to Burrow, Māzana is known in Iranian sources as the territory between the southern shore of the Caspian Sea and the Alburz mountains.40 It may also be related to Margiana and to the Indo-Āryan culture noted there.
Also, the occupants of the Bactro-Margiana Archaeological Complex may have been related to the same family as the later Gandhara. Since the Gandhara culture also bears the first evidence of cremation rituals in South Asia, we may consider them to have indeed consolidated the Vedic customs of the Indo-Āryans. It is also interesting in this context to note that Herodotus, in his History of the Persian Wars, III, page 102, refers to other Indians who “dwell northward of all the rest of the Indians“ and describes them as following “the same mode of life as the Bactrians“, which shows that the Indian Āryans may have been originally derived from the BactroMargiana Archaeological Complex.
It must be observed also that there are indeed fire-altars even in the Harappan sites of Kalibangan, in Rajasthan, and Lothal, in Gujarat, which may be dated to around 2500 B.C. So, it remains a moot question as to whether the BMAC fire-altars were introduced from the north or the south or whether they formed part of an extensive north-south Aryan cultural continuum. Indeed, the Allchins surmise that there were also fire-altars in Harappa and Mohenjaro Daro, although they have been missed in the mass diggings conducted at these sites.43
We note that the Indo-Āryan migration towards India is not mentioned in the Sturluson account of the migration of the Aesir from Central Asia to Anatolia and then Europe. The Gandaridae may have taken a more direct southward route to India than the western Indo-Germanic tribes who migrated in a southwestern direction towards Anatolia. Also, of the Indo-Āryans, Iranians, and Germans, only the first seem to have preserved the full cosmological significance of the fire rituals in their Vedic scriptures.
Despite their northern origins, the Indo-Āryans seem to have been long resident in India and to have come to consider it their home. For, in the Manusmrithi,44 II, the land of the Indo-Āryans is described in fully Indian geographical terms:

Let the twice-born men seek to dwell in those [lands], but a Shūdra, distressed for subsistence, may reside anywhere.
Further, according to Vishnusmrithi, LXXXIV, 4, “Those countries are called barbarous (Mleccha) where the system of the four castes does not exist. The others are denoted Āryāvarta.”
Thus, compared to the Āryans who settled in India, many of the other branches of the Japhetite branch of the Indo-Europeans — who are characterized as noble kshatriyas seem to have sunk to a lower social level, that of the fourth caste, or shūdras, on account of their disregard of the brāhmanical rites. According to Manusmrithi X:
As a consequence of the omission of the sacred rites and of their not consulting Brāhmanas, the following tribes of Kshatriyas have gradually sunk into this world to the condition of Shūdras.

The Paundrakas,49 the Kodas, the Dravidas,50 the Kāmbojas [Bactrians?],51 the Yavanas [Ionians, Greeks], the Sākas [Scythians], the Pāradas, the Pahlavas [Parthians?],52 the Kīnas, the Kirātas, and the Daradas [Dardic tribes].
Finally, we note also that, in India, even though the original Noachidian wisdom of Manu may have been due to the proto-Dravidians, it is best preserved in the cultivated [Sanskrit = refined] and inflected language of the upper castes of the Indo-Āryans, though Sanskrit retains several Dravidian elements in it.
This suggests that, whether there was an Āryan invasion or not, the Dravidian bearers of the proto-Dravidian yogic wisdom that the fire-worshipping Āryans adopted were relegated to a lower level by the latter in India. This subordination seems to correspond to the mastery of the Achaeans over the local Mediterranean populations that Lahovary noted in Greece in the 16th century B.C.
However, the historical development of the Brāhmanical religion in India in the direction of exoteric temple worship rather than the esoteric fire-rituals of the IndoĀryans53 reveals the strong spiritual — as well as artistic — contribution of the Dravidians to Āryan India.
