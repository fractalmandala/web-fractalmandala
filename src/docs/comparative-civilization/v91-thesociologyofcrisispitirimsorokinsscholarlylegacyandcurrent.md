## Abstract
In his writings on crises, Sorokin highlighted their complexity and their multiple consequences on a personal, cultural, and social level. In this paper, crises in the 21st century are analyzed from Sorokin’s incisive perspective. The significant points include:
- (1) the study of crises cannot be enclosed within the confines of individual disciplines but rather must incorporate interdisciplinary approaches.
- (2) the category “crisis” does not encompass only those conditions that are the consequences of specific disastrous events such as natural disasters, wars, and terrorism but also all those phenomena that produce the conjunction of harm to human beings and social disturbances.
- (3) successive crises teach us that vulnerability is on the increase, and this makes it necessary to prepare for prevention and to undertake prompt intervention at local, national, and international levels. This cannot be separated from the existence of the community, particularly the resilient community. This is one that allows for the affirmation of social commitment, respect for rights and freedoms, the balancing of needs and civic responsibilities, and the reconstruction of satisfactory relationships between individuals, and, therefore, ultimately, the avoidance of crises.
Keywords: Crisis; Sorokin; Calamities; Social changes; Resilience.
Pitirim A. Sorokin Legacy: The Reconstruction of Humanity to Overcome Crises Crises can be defined as any sudden, destabilizing events or situations that threaten the equilibrium, security, and/or everyday life of an individual, a community, an organization, or society as a whole. They are also differentiated by their nature.
Pitirim Sorokin argued in his book The Crisis of Our Age (1941a), written during the Second World War, that the crisis then being experienced was not one that could be defined as ordinary:
“It is not merely economic or political maladjustment but involves simultaneously almost the whole of Western culture and society, in all their main sectors. It is a crisis in their art and science, philosophy and religion, law and morals, manners, and mores, in the form of social, political, and economic organization, including the nature of the family and marriage — in brief, it is a crisis involving almost the whole way of life, thought, and conduct of Western society. More precisely, it consists in a disintegration of a fundamental form of Western culture and society dominant for the last four centuries” (1941a, pp. 16-17).
In this book, Sorokin gives a detailed description of the crisis of Western society in the different spheres of action of individuals. He highlighted the diagnoses and different points of view that have developed on the subject, two of which were prevalent and opposed to each other. Sorokin defined the first as optimistic and the second as pessimistic.
The first diagnosis (optimistic in identifying both causes and remedies) considered the crisis to be an ordinary economic crisis, which could, therefore, be overcome with certain interventions. To resolve this crisis, it proposed the adjustment of different conditions (economic and political) as well as the elimination of wicked men to hope for a return to prosperity (the reference was to Hitler and Mussolini, the two dictators of the Nazis and Fascists respectively; today, there would be no shortage of names to mention). This diagnosis was optimistic in identifying both the causes and remedies.
The second diagnosis, on the other hand, had a pessimistic outlook (particularly in the United States) since the crisis was seen as the agony that precedes the future death of Western society and culture: “The present crisis is but the beginning of the end of their historical existence. No remedy can avert this destiny; no cure can prevent the death of Western culture” (Sorokin, 1941a, p. 16).
Believing both diagnoses to be fallacious, Sorokin presented a third one. This one is neither reduced to political and economic oppositions nor to the destruction of culture and society since “the total sum of social and cultural phenomena of Western society and culture has never been integrated into one unified system. What has not been integrated cannot, it is evident, disintegrate” (Sorokin, 1941a, p. 26).
These are the theoretical foundations on which the diagnosis of the extraordinary crisis that Western societies were experiencing is based. Sorokin also analyzed them in the different spheres of society.
Included were the following: fine arts, science, philosophy, and religion; ethics and law; family, government, economic organization, freedom, and international relations; and, again, crime, war, revolution, suicide, mental illness, and impoverishment. The analysis of the Russian American scholar, however, was not limited to diagnosis but also elaborated a theory to escape the crisis.
In the chapter entitled The Disintegration of Sensate Culture; The Roots of the Crisis and the Way Out, he identified the way out of the crisis — a change of cultural mentality was needed. He called this sensate culture. His analysis showed how the crisis that was being experienced represented a disintegration of the sensate form of Western society and culture. It must, therefore, be followed by a new integration. This transformation consisted of correcting the errors recorded in the sensate phase of society and culture by creating what Sorokin saw as a mental, moral, and socio-cultural revolution in Western societies.
It was a revolution that had to take place through five steps. These were: 1) a rapid recognition of the extraordinariness of the crisis; 2) the recognition that the sensate system is not the only possible form and that, above all, it is not immune to flaws; 3) the shift from one system to another when it is understood that the creative force of the former is being exhausted; 4) a profound re-examination of values and the rejection of pseudo-values through the strengthening of real values; and, finally, 5) a transformation of the forms of social relations and forms of social organization, in tandem with the change in cultural mentality.
He recalled for the first time in this chapter the Sermon on the Mount, which he would revisit several times in later works on creative altruistic love (Sorokin, 1954). He called for a change in people’s mindsets and attitudes concerning the standards outlined in the Sermon on the Mount because, in addition to cultural or personality changes, such a shift would also require a reshaping of the political and economic systems.
The change was to modify the forms of social relations, replacing antagonistic behavior or compulsory and contractual relations with purer relations to transform the value system and the behavior of individuals towards others, cultural values, and the world in general. Sorokin can, therefore, be considered a promoter of overcoming modern (sensate) culture in the direction of transforming relations between individuals, as well as between them and institutions through the rediscovery of the positive values of man (Mangone, 2020; Mangone & Dolgov, 2020).
It is no coincidence that the terms used by Sorokin are not conflicting and cooperative. He makes a significant choice and uses — as early as the volumes that earned him his doctorate in sociology (Sorokin, 1920) — the terms solidaristic and antagonistic behavior, or compulsory behavior.
They were also used in Contemporary Sociological Theories (Sorokin, 1928) when describing the forms of interaction and behavior that, for him, represent a pivotal element of his studies.
A similar typology (Harmonious and Antagonistic Interactions) appears in an unpublished and undated typescript1 (Sorokin n.d., chap. V: 8), but the pages describing this typology are missing. The first form represents any interaction in which the aspirations of both parties coincide and in which one party does not obstruct but, on the contrary, helps the other party to realize its aspirations. The second (opposite to the first) refers to an interaction in which one party prevents or obstructs the other party's aspirations.
For Sorokin, the protection of social weaknesses is entrusted to solidarity, which is a form of interaction that presupposes reciprocity. The primary problem of an everchanging society is the disappearance of the bonds of mechanical solidarity of Durkheimian memory (Durkheim, 1893/1960). Therefore, the individual's action is a causal dependency between physical involvement and the pressure exerted by the social environment on the individual.
The term “solidarity” presupposes the involvement of all the parties that are interacting in the social system, not allowing for the abandonment of social protections for the weakest but rather urging for energetic and autonomous initiatives by individuals to strengthen the protection and guarantees for everyone. The solidaristic form of human behavior, however, will later be replaced by the love relationship (love/altruism). For Sorokin, the words love and altruism are interchangeable, with both referring to all those actions that produce and maintain the psychological and/or physical good of oneself and others through positive actions.
Sorokin considers the love relationship as “the supreme and vital form of human relationship” (Sorokin, 1954, p. 76). This, before reaching conceptual maturity with the work produced during the years of his activity at the Harvard Research Center in Creative Altruism (1949-1959), had already appeared in some earlier writings (Sorokin, 1950a, 1950b).
Based on Sorokin’s analysis, the way out of the crisis is to change the culture by transforming interpersonal relations and relations between people and institutions with the rediscovery of the positive values of man. Sorokin writes:

A careful study of the situation accordingly shows that the present crisis represents only a disintegration of the sensate form of Western society and culture, to be followed by a new integration as notable in its own way as was the sensate form in the days of its glory and climax” (Sorokin, 1941a, p. 24).
According to Sorokin, this remedy, transforming cultural mentalities through modifying interactions between individuals, is not based on what is desirable but rather on a sociological induction that repeats itself in the same way for all previous crises. It can be summarised with the formula: “Crisis-ordeal-catharsis-charisma-resurrection” (Sorokin, 1941a, p. 321).
All previous societies were preserved from dissolution not through the manipulation of economic, political, genetic, or other factors but primarily through the transformation of values, a kind of spiritualization of mentality, and the socialization of behavior toward positive social relations.
The causes and significance of crises can also be found in the book that Sorokin (1951) published ten years after The Crisis of Our Age. In this later book, Sorokin addresses the three phases of the crisis in the decade of the 1940s: the murderous trend, polarization, and totalitarianism. The book opens with a sharp critique against “Man, the Great Destroyer,” who produces death wounds for entire generations of humanity by demanding absolute loyalty (see totalitarianism such as Nazism and Fascism). Of this type of man, he writes, “In a few weeks out of millions of good youths, he organizes armies of terrific killers. In a few hours, he atomizes millions of human beings into smoky nothingness. In a few minutes, he leaves an abomination of desolation of what was before a metropolis or prosperous country. In a few days, he utterly uglifies the beautiful face of the Earth” (Sorokin, 1951, p. 11).
The two world wars highlighted the power of technology and mass armies and the ease with which they inflicted devastation and ruin in a relatively short time. He presents evidence of this “murderous trend”.
For Sorokin, “the Great Destroyer” is embodied in the ruling groups and their staff. He reaffirms that humanity could only be liberated and move towards a new life by eliminating the “man-eat-man doctrine of the Great Destroyer” with the acceptance of the logic of “love your enemy, bless them that curse you.”
Sorokin states this after clarifying that among the possible effects of frustration in human beings, there is not only aggression, which appears to be the most common effect, but also apathy, creative activity, or conversion to a new life. The latter is the least common effect. This usually occurs following something that disrupts the lives of individuals, such as one of the calamities (the four monsters) identified by Sorokin (1942/2010) in Man and Society in Calamity (war, revolution, famine, and pestilence).
Having concluded his description of the “murderous trend” with numerous historical examples (this examination occupies the first three chapters of the book), Sorokin discusses polarization and totalitarianism by enunciating two laws that then become the central focus of the book.
The first law is the “law of polarization in frustration and crises.” This polarization transposes the one he had previously identified for the effects of calamities (law of diversification and polarization of the effects of calamity). In its generalized form, it now presents this structure: “A crisis, calamity, or frustration tends to make explicit and open the implicit and hidden contradictions, whether in one’s mind and conduct, or in a social group and institution, or in a given culture” (Sorokin, 1951, p. 100). Sorokin, as he often did, does not provide a precise definition of “crisis”, “calamity” or “frustration.” However, in this case, this is justified as these are all concepts that he discussed extensively in his earlier works (Sorokin, 1941a, 1942/2010, 1948). To counteract the negative effects of this law, there should be a reintegration of culture “along integralist lines.”
The second law, illustrated in the last chapter, is formulated as follows: “As soon as any important emergency or calamity occurs in a given society, the control and regimentation of its government begins to expand and grow; as soon as the emergency subsides, the government control declines” (Sorokin, 1951, p. 144).
This simply reinforces the well-known proposition that “war always tends to breed totalitarianism” to such an extent that Sorokin drew a generalized conclusion that: “the medicine against Communism and totalitarianism is neither war, nor rearmament, nor threats, nor propaganda of hate, but peace, and lack of emergency” (Sorokin, 1951, p. 169). This book, unlike its predecessors, does not offer practical advice on how to achieve “peace” and “lack of emergency.” It emphasizes what should be but does not indicate how to achieve it.
Sorokin’s “diagnostic” activity continued even after the 1950s. In the first essay in the collection, The Basic Trends of Our Time (Sorokin, 1964), Sorokin identified three trends that he considered to be fundamental to modern times. The first trend is the “shift of creative leadership” away from the large and powerful continents such as Europe and America to the new renaissance cultures: “The stars of the next acts of the great historical drama are going to be — besides Europe, the Americas, and Russia — the renascent great cultures of India, China, Japan, Indonesia, and the Islamic world.” (Sorokin, 1964, p. 15).
To this, he added that change was already underway and the effects of this would be much greater and more important than Western alliances and governments.
The second trend is the disintegration of the order of culture caused by the breakdown of moral, legal, and more general values that, usually from within society, control and guide the behavior of individuals and groups. Under such conditions, human beings no longer behave as Homo socius but rather as Homo homini lupus (Hobbes docet) driven by their biological impulses, passions, and lust. The struggle for existence takes on forms that explode on an unprecedented scale.
The third trend refers to the transformations that are already underway to produce the new order. This is because in stages of transition, when societies do not perish completely, new energies are mobilized (slowly at first and then more forcefully) that counteract the destructive energies, allowing for a new socio-cultural order to be designed and built.
It almost seems as if Sorokin was beginning to emerge as one who, close to his death, asks himself how correct the belief was that modern man is peaceful.
“‘Peaceloving’ declarations by the modern governments, leaders, and ordinary people have been indefatigably professed and repeated millions of times; they have become a sort of habitual, routine utterances like ‘how do you do’ and ‘good-by’ for the modern man” (Sorokin, 1968, p. 3).
The answer he provided is that this belief is largely an illusion or self-deception of modern man since historical and everyday events show that modern man is not a man of peace but rather a man of war. With this, Sorokin does not claim that there are no men of peace but that men of war outnumber them.
Similar illusions also exist in other dimensions of everyday life. For Sorokin, the individual is a complex creature made up of several elements (mind, body, and soul), thus a veritable coincidentia oppositorum. We see a split personality that, at times, embodies virtues and vices (vital, mental, moral, aesthetic, and socio-cultural) in completely opposite ways. The hope, therefore, is that a full rebirth of the true, good, and beautiful creative forces of humanity may blossom, ending this self-deception of modern man.
In summary, for Sorokin, there was only one way to take the road out of the crisis, regardless of its nature: strengthening relationships and interactions that produce positive effects for all members of society. “A peaceful, harmonious, and creative society can exist only when members possess at least a minimum of love, sympathy, and compassion ensuring mutual aid, co-operation, and fair treatment. Under these conditions, its members are united in one collective ‘we’ in which others share the joys and sorrows of one member. In such a group, a member is not as an isolated atom, but a vital part of a creative community” (Sorokin, 1948, pp. 57-58).
In summarizing the remedies for resolving the crisis of the sensate society, the Russian American sociologist argues that, beyond the complexity of mental phenomena, the main reason for man’s impotence in being creatively altruistic is the neglect of these phenomena by science during the last four centuries. According to Sorokin, it is a priority for science to correct the misconception of man and the socio-cultural universe, and for this reason, research plans studying human energies must be promoted and pursued (Sorokin, 1958a, 1959a).
The behavior of individuals is not always positively oriented towards others. However, such behavior can be transformed by a revolution of minds and hearts, obviously a revolution without violence. The transition from “tribal altruism” or “tribal egoism” (altruism that expresses itself exclusively in-group, making one indifferent if not aggressive towards other groups or out-groups) to “universal altruism” (altruism that extends beyond one's own group membership to encompass the whole of humanity, excluding no one) is necessary because the whole of humanity is faced with a dilemma that has yet to be resolved.
By the mysterious forces of destiny mankind is confronted with a stern dilemma: either to continue its predatory policies of individual and tribal selfishness that lead it to its inevitable doom, or to embark upon the policies of universal solidarity that brings humanity to the aspired situation of heaven on Earth. It is up to everyone of us which of these two roads we prefer to choose” (Sorokin, 1954, p. 489).
This dilemma still has no solution. Those proposed by Sorokin have, especially for modern social scientists, only represented “foolish” ideas (Sorokin, 1955), not a starting point for reconstructing humanity in crisis.
In an article entitled The Mysterious Energy of Love (1959a), Sorokin argued that even though he had little knowledge of this energy and how it is produced and utilized, it is sufficient to justify the hypothesis that the “grace of love” is one of the three highest energies known to man (along with truth and beauty). A peculiarity of this vital energy is identified in the force of altruistic creative love and is considered the only antidote (Sorokin, 1958a). In Sorokin’s own medical metaphor, to save humanity affected by the crisis, [the goal is] to find an effective cure in the affirmation of universal altruism.
According to Sorokin, this change must start from the rediscovery of the positive values of man. Science also acts as a guide by overcoming strictly sensate models of knowledge. Sorokin’s is not just a “sociology of crisis,” but a “critical sociology,” one which does not stop at an analysis of the processes of degeneration of society, but rather, one which searches for its deep roots by denouncing the negative factors that determine it and presenting possible ways of overcoming these negative aspects.
The application of these principles and assumptions implies an understanding of the mechanisms through which human beings make their decisions; this is the problem of choice. These, in turn, must be evaluated with respect to the temporal dimension and to the degree of knowledge of situations, as well as with respect to who and how individuals or their representatives make decisions.
## Social Space, Crisis, and Current Problems
The relevance of Sorokin’s theories on social change and the crisis of society should be acknowledged, even if his thought must be considered as framed in the historical reality he experienced (Mangone, 2023a). Despite this, though decades have passed since he studied crises, not only do they continue to recur (undoubtedly in different forms), but often, they produce the same devastating effects and require the exact same remedies that Sorokin identified.
In our contemporary society, we can think about the crisis described by Sorokin, starting with the one that has plagued the Western world since 2008, the financial crisis, and which has been exacerbated by the spread of the Covid virus (since the end of 2019), with the resulting global pandemic and increased vulnerability (Gaynor & Wilson, 2020). This has produced adverse effects that still cannot be definitively quantified on a material, social, and psychological level. The state of crisis that, by now, seems perennial can no longer be the exclusive subject of natural disciplines or economics as they are inextricably intertwined with the misuse of resources on the one hand and the representation of individual and collective well-being on the other.
We return to the problem posed by Sorokin (1958b) regarding constructing an integrated knowledge system that would hold together the three forms of knowledge: empirical-sensory, reason, and intuition. This is a knowledge system capable of providing as many elements as possible for the understanding of superorganic (sociocultural) phenomena and, therefore, where possible, one that can also predict their transformations.
All this points towards the need to return to the idea of putting human beings at the center since the scaffolding of the welfare state no longer holds (Žižek, 2020). The Covid pandemic has also demonstrated this even in the countries considered to be most advanced; it has been seen with the collapse of the most advanced healthcare systems. A new discourse on the importance of the “social, human and environmental dimension” — both at the level of nations and at the supranational level — has been initiated, aiming at collective well-being and, thus, a possibly better world.
Social protection is no longer just recognized as a cost but also seen as a productive factor. It is not only inspired by the criteria of conflict containment but also, primarily, by the principles of inclusion and social justice, recalling praxis.
The classical notion of praxis that Arendt (1958) revived addressed “political action” by linking it to factors such as freedom, which is not to be understood so much as freedom of choice (dear to the liberalist tradition) but, rather, as the capacity to initiate something new and plurality. If action is an activity that amounts to introducing something new, then this is something that cannot be done in isolation from others in a way that is completely independent of the presence of a plurality of actors.
Sorokin (1947/1962) pointed out that the complexity of socio-cultural phenomena is determined by the fact that meaningful interactions take place between interdependent and inseparable aspects and elements.
For this reason, the analyses of the common properties of socio-cultural phenomena cannot be considered as “simpler units” but rather as an interweaving of personality (the subject of interactions), society (the set of interactions and sociocultural phenomena and processes), and culture (the set of meanings, values, norms, and the set of means that objectify, socialize and transmit these elements).
Culture, on the one hand, enables legitimization. On the other, it has an intrinsic value that is independent of its usability. These peculiarities characterize the functions of the socio-cultural system, enabling individuals to survive in relation to the external environment, fostering integration within the community to which they belong, and reducing the anxiety that can be triggered in individuals mired in unforeseen or changing situations such as crises.
Crises are perceived as an increase in disorder within an individual and collective system (Morin, 2021), a “social space”. They cause a blockage of the organizational devices, determining rigidities, unrest, and the dissolution of conditions that had been suppressed. This also results in increased uncertainty due to the difficulty of knowing – if not predicting – future events.
The concept of crisis, which is usually given a negative meaning, also brings with it opportunities. One can understand how the concept is complex and difficult to explore, as well as how it is linked to the social space constituting the object of study of the meso dimension of the daily life of individuals. The concept faces the relationships between the social system and life (a set of meanings and representations of culture).
This “social space” includes both physical/material aspects and socio-psycho-relational aspects. Within this well-defined and delimited space (it has its own boundary, the latter being understood as the “something” that delimits it), developments partially or totally independent of individuals break out (such as pandemics, wars, and natural disasters. Socio-cultural changes occur when crises have multiple effects.
Turning once again to Sorokin’s intellectual legacy, there is the distinction between geometric space (length, width, and depth) and social space (Sorokin, 1927)2. The latter is not a container where individuals move and/or simply gain experience. It is the Greek “agora” (ἀγορά) within which the daily religious, civil, and political life of the inhabitants of the polis took place, thus the construction of social reality.
Social space manifests itself in different forms since the ways of relating between the elements and the subjective propensities of local actors are diversified both in the political-economic and socio-cultural spheres. Every social space is characterized by its own internal functioning scheme, one which depends on history, cultural and social phenomena, and combinations of multiple factors.
We see that the implication of social space is complex and that social networks find their closest relationships within it. This follows the logic that the sustainability of initiatives is addressed by considering those multiple dimensions (sociocultural, environmental, and economic) that develop in the social space. Within the relationship between the social space and individuals or their groupings, the multiple needs of the social system related to cohesion and integration are inserted and related.
The social processes accompanying industrialization (including secularization, rationalization, and individualization) rediscovered the relationship between the individual and social space. This then produced a sort of “rupture” (understood as a profound transformation) of rhythms and lifestyles, influencing the well-being of individuals. The latter has always been affected by the effects of development models that especially characterize the West. These lead to increased social inequalities, poverty, and a general worsening of living conditions.
These effects, accompanied by a state of perennial crisis, have led to the need to bring the nexus between “freedom” and “social justice” and between individual and collective responsibilities back to the center of global debates. These debates have emerged, as have political responsibilities for overcoming ever-widening inequality gaps.
Let us clarify some concepts closely connected to the idea of crisis. One need not draw up an exhaustive theoretical framework of this concept, but it is necessary to recall some key theoretical aspects and consider practices to overcome crises.
As to political representation, individuals and their aggregates, both the bearers of the general and of interests, have taken on an increasingly central role as a resource for sustainable development.

One cannot ignore the heightened cohesion and integration emerging among the members of society. In his definition of crisis, Habermas (1988) notes that despite being faced with a single structure, there are different dimensions (economic, political, and socio-cultural) that push toward a crisis. In the political space, there are crises of legitimation, representation, and participation. These essentially materialize in the mobilization of citizens in reference to their condition, aimed at more forcefully expressing their point of view towards the institutional powers.
This leads to a series of subsequent problems related to the role of institutions and political representation within the system, including a growth in uncertainty and mistrust in decision-making and deliberative processes. This wariness is not only oriented towards the search for new tools of participation but also, above all, is a basic prerequisite for the relaunch of processes that allow for the total expression of the right of representation in the political life of a country.
The economic sphere issues inevitably give rise to an economic and financial crisis. In recent years, there has been the worsening of the economic crisis beginning in the first decade of the 21st century, followed by the ill-health effects aroused during the COVID19 pandemic. The total suspension of human activity, the distancing and temporary cessation of the freedom of movement of people and businesses, and the limitations on productive activities profoundly changed the lifestyle and consumption habits of individuals with strong economic repercussions. (Harvey, 2020).
Despite being outside the emergency, the financial policies of the central banks (European, American, and world) were unable to give an adequate response. The current political and economic systems result from a series of changes and reforms imposed by the need to combine growing expectations with increasingly higher quality standards (not always corresponding to real needs) and the shortage and decrease in financial resources.
Economic systems, which can influence political systems, are the result of a progressive affirmation of the “economic market” (Doti & Lee, 1991), one which has dramatically brought political control into difficulty. Even if political systems arise from the collaboration between different levels of political responsibility (international, national, and local), they cannot control financial turbulence and guarantee a system of goods and services that are fair and responsive to the real needs of citizens. This happened even before the pandemic.
The crises that develop within the sociocultural space directly result from the processes that originate in the social, political, and economic spaces. They take the form of crises of social cohesion; that is, the direct pressures on the integration processes within the sociocultural system are not endogenous but derive directly and/or indirectly both from the political system — through its legitimation — and from the economic system that motivates the achievement of certain goals.
From a sociological point of view, it is worth recalling the two elements (the goals and the means) which, according to Mertonian theory (Merton, 1968), give origin and direction to the pressures to which the members of a society are subjected. The social condition resulting from the pandemic caused by the COVID-19 virus has also forced a reconsideration of the relationship between the national public sphere and the horizons of a hypothetical global or transnational public sphere; this is especially problematic when the demands of globalization seem to be put into the discussion.
If legitimacy posed the central question of the debate at the beginning of the 21st century, today, the focus is on analyzing the boundaries and articulation of the public sphere with respect to the private sphere. This is because of different protagonists, whether they belong without distinction to the two spheres indicated by Arendt (1958), the public sphere and the private sphere. The response to contemporary crises guarantees struggles over cohesion, integration, and social justice in society.
In the “environmental” social space, the crisis presents itself in various forms. The most well-known ones are climate, energy, and biodiversity. They are forms of crisis that reverberate in all the other environments.
The physical environment is the space within which the other dimensions interact. These crises can be overcome through new political-institutional and educational strategies focused on social eco-justice (Cubeddu & Mangone, 2024). Their aim is to implement a change in society focused on raising awareness of the value of the environment to society. Laurent (2011) defines a social ecology that starts from the needs of individuals, including the primary need to use environmental resources for our own satisfaction and well-being while safeguarding the same resources for future generations.
This brief examination of the social spaces within which crises can occur is not exhaustive but only illustrative. It moves towards the analysis and understanding of the manifestations (actions) through which the community members within different social spaces (considered the different spheres of contemporary society) respond to crisis conditions.
## Resilience, Community, and Social Capital
A world free from crisis conditions (social, economic, and political) is not conceivable. However, we can imagine a world where negative consequences are reduced or even avoided.
The chain of crises of recent decades has demonstrated that vulnerability (UN, 2003) is increasing due to both socio-cultural and natural transformations (Phillips et al., 2010), not least the global spread of the COVID-19 virus (De Marchi, 2020). This was not only for those segments of the population that are traditionally considered vulnerable (the poor, disabled, immigrants, children, elderly, and young people) but also for all the other segments of the population.
This makes it necessary to provide response actions to crisis conditions at a local, national, and international level.
These responses should promote the resilience of communities (Norris et al., 2008; Wright, 2021) of the population groups that, due to phenomena of various natures (economic, political, health-related, for example), are more vulnerable than others.
The effects of crises are not identical for all individuals and groups. This is not only due to the level of involvement (direct or indirect) but also includes the ability of the individual and society to resist their negative effects (social vulnerability). For these reasons, we start from the idea that collective damage requires collective strategies (Mangone & Zyuzev, 2020). What happens in a crisis and/or emergency? What changes occur, or must be promoted, so that individual and collective dynamics move towards resilience and, therefore, towards the construction and strengthening of bonds and social capital?
Crises like disasters are not to be considered sui generis. They represent a normal moment in the flow of life, which also recognizes the characteristics of social systems that might not otherwise be obvious as the related events cause consequences on the vital level as well as on the mechanisms of socio-psychological regulation and social change (Sorokin, 1942/2010).
Thinking about the future during or after an emergency cannot ignore the existence of a community. A community is one of the many manifestations of social space and, therefore, an expression and resource of a territory. This includes a set of relationships that should be positive. The community is founded on individuals considered in their entirety rather than in one of the roles people play within society. The community is also a combination of experiences and thoughts, of tradition and commitment, of participation and will. At the same time, it enhances the social dimension of existence and, above all, the sense of belonging to a common destiny.
Events produce a rupture in the existing network of social relations, making the definition of the social structure chaotic, a process that itself becomes a powerful factor of socio-cultural change. Consider what happened with the Covid pandemic, which led to the quarantine of part or all of the population.
Changes are produced both in individuals and in the social structure. They prompt the need to start again, reconnecting the life thread between individuals and the community. Thus, this results in trying to imagine a possible future (Mangone, 2021), including seeking a plan to bring the entire community out of a crisis (of imbalance) and channel it towards new balances.
These projects are identifiable with those individual and organizational processes through which community members are empowered.
They strengthen the ability to choose, plus self-determination and self-regulation, simultaneously developing the feeling of one’s own value and control over life by reducing feelings of mistrust, fear, anxiety, and/or negative tension.
A key concept is that of “resilience” (Hoggett, 2014). This is generally defined as the ability of an individual or a group to return to normality after events have produced negative effects through two components: one of a personal nature (the individual responding to events) and the other of a situational nature. This last aspect focuses on the organized forms of groups and those that can be defined as communities in the form of resilient communities (Wright, 2022). This is closely linked to the concept of social capital (Lin, 2002) and the meaning it takes on in the building of resilience, together with other components that contribute to adaptation to a disturbance (crisis).
Through the resilient community, social commitment is affirmed, along with respect for rights and freedoms, the balance between needs and civil responsibilities, and the reconstruction of satisfactory relationships between individuals. A new form of community takes shape, which recalls Lévy’s collective intelligence (1997). The basis is the mutual enrichment of individuals and not the cult of the community. It contributes to the understanding of socio-economic structures and territorial dynamics, becoming a tool at the service of the parties involved.
These characteristics defend against the processes of exclusion. The resilient community inevitably looks to the future and, above all, to a new and possible world. It becomes an instrument of action if considered as an “open space,” one in which environmental and social networks are interrelated. It ensures the sustainability of development and social protection initiatives.
According to Norris (Norris et al., 2008), resilience arises from resources present in the community:
a) economic development b) shared capital c) information and communication d) the competence of the community
This returns to what Sorokin (1958b) argued regarding the future of humanity and its development. It is not law nor education, much less religion or economy, or even science, that is sufficient for this task that is assigned to humanity itself.
Thinking about the future means thinking about the existence of a community founded on individuals. They express themselves through their unity and completeness, and not in relation to the roles they personally have played within society.
It is the set of daily experiences, and for this reason, it enhances the social and cultural dimension of existence.
The community is the social structure that recognizes the individual's dignity, protecting freedom with civic sense and respect for oneself and others (guaranteeing rights and respect for duties). It is social capital which, due to its interconnections with many other elements of society, contributes significantly to the building of community resilience.
Introduced for the first time by Loury (1977, 1987), this concept should be understood as the set of resources found within family relationships and in the community's social organization. They are useful for the development of individuals. Bourdieu (1979) also considered this perspective.
The most influential contribution to the definition of the concept has been that of Coleman (1990), who maintains that it develops through the relationships of individuals (for this reason, it is not tangible) adapted and transformed to facilitate action. These relationships are to be considered forms of capital because, like other capitals, they produce symbolic and material value despite their intangibility. The value of social capital is inherent in that it identifies certain aspects of the social structure based on function.
Social capital is an inherently holistic concept that does not stop at the economic aspects. It is connected to the degree of civicness (Putnam, 1992), the freedom of the community, and, above all, to the adoption of behaviors that are based on trust (Gambetta, 1988; Fukuyama, 1995). These are elements that lead back to belonging and reciprocity. Since it directly involves individuals, social capital stimulates actions that lead to the sharing of a path aimed at achieving the common objective: redefining a new system of needs destroyed by the crisis. It presents itself as the “multiplier of the possible.” It only makes sense if aimed at multiplying its potential, producing and reproducing.
Social capital makes sense when it differentiates itself infinitely and continuously from the objectives it pursues and achieves. It contributes to the resilience of the community (Mangone, 2018a). It has value only when it is in movement (dynamic) in the continuous search for well-being. The social, value, cultural, and relational components can represent the multiplier of well-being without which any intervention, good, structure, or service is sterile.
The community, therefore, is not only an operational concept of social space that manifests itself through consolidated identity sets and shared traditions, but it is also a social organization attentive to the needs of individuals with the strength to face and overcome crises and social pathologies.
Due to its intangibility and the collective impact of its benefits, social capital cannot be considered the property of the actors from whom it arises. It must be considered a public good (Coleman, 2005) and, as such, must be protected. For the accumulation and not the dispersion of social capital to occur, only one path can be followed: strengthening social bonds through trust and citizenship. Consequently, those territories which, upon superficial analysis, seem without hope become places of production. They also become producers of development processes that allow for the survival of the community, along with improving the quality of life.
## Final Remarks
When thinking about the future, during or after a crisis, we cannot ignore the existence of a community or groups of individuals. It is from the significant interactions that are established in them (which in themselves can be considered positive) that the planning and reconstruction of identity and sets of values arise, as Sorokin argued.
The community is founded on individuals who express their unity and completeness and not in relation to the roles they play within society. It is the set of daily experiences, and for this reason, it valorizes the social and cultural dimensions of existence. It is through a resilient community (Everett, 2022) that social commitment, respect for rights and freedoms, the balance between needs and civil responsibilities, and the reconstruction of satisfactory relationships among individuals are affirmed.
What Sorokin theorized and anticipated would later become a widely adopted approach to studying crises and disasters from the 1980s onwards. It is now based on the concepts of social vulnerability (Phillips et al., 2010) and resilience (Manyena, 2006; Wright, 2022), two concepts closely related to each other. As argued by Sorokin, the effects of some phenomena (calamities) are not identical upon all individuals and their communities. These effects are not only due to the different direct or indirect involvement of individuals, but they are also related to the nature of the different crises in which humanity is involved.
Crises that result from or coincide with wars or terrorist actions are distinguished from other crises by the generalized sense of fear they generate, by the lack of trust in institutions they arouse, by their unpredictability, and by effects that extend beyond the limited area of the actual conflict from which they originate (Mangone, 2018b).
Crisis conditions cannot only be the breeding ground for unprecedented forms of social solidarity and social support, but they can also become the premise for renewed collective participation. This results from individuals' unprecedented relationship with the place where they live, the problems that afflict them, and the collective solutions that can be achieved.
Community resilience is not only the result of top-down social and institutionally generated actions stimulated by plans for the territory drawn up by governments at different levels (local, national, or international), but it can also emerge as the result of unexpected events. Acting as the community means acting on multiple levels (individual, family, institutional, and social), and it holds the whole community as the object since a normalization process of the community needs to be undertaken by reinforcing existing networks and structures, reestablishing the previous ones and creating new ones (Mangone & Zyuzev, 2020, pp. 191-192).
It would be desirable to build models of best practices derived from these forms of positive experiences, templates that can be replicated in different territories and for different target populations in fragile conditions. Such models should be able, in crisis or non-crisis situations, to support interventions, especially from below. Thus, they would put into practice what Sorokin strongly supported, based on his argument that the salvation of humanity rests only in the hands of humanity.
