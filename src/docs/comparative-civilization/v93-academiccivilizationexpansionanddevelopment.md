Inbar Gotlib Rivkin School of Education Tel-Aviv University, Israel inbargotlib@gmail.com Abstract
The concept of academic civilization was developed out of and in relation to that of modern civilization. Prominent characteristics of modern civilization are reflected in institutions of higher education, and they can therefore be regarded as constituting a civilization. The six main characteristics of institutions of higher education that render them a civilization include expansion in terms of scope and growth, flexibility and institutional diversity, center-periphery relations, academic culture, autonomy and academic freedom, and the monopolization of professional training and the professional middle class. This paper addresses the first characteristic — expansion of institutions of higher education — and demonstrates that this expansion process is parallel to that of modern civilization.
Academic civilization includes a constant process of expansion in similar patterns all over the world. The socio-cultural process of the expansion of academic civilization is analogous to the expansion process of modern civilization that began in Western countries and from there spread throughout the world, especially after World War II. Academic civilization is also a secularization process that developed in Western countries — mainly Western Europe and the United States — and from there spread throughout the world.
Keywords: academic civilization, modern civilization, civilization, institutions of higher education, secularization, expansion of academic institutions
## Introduction
The concept of civilization is regarded as a process (i.e., Elias, 2000). The expansion of institutions of higher education is a broad process. This process maintains many civilizational patterns and has adopted prominent characteristics of modern civilization. In addition, civilization is defined as a socio-cultural development (Shils, 1997). The development of institutions of higher education is a worldwide sociological phenomenon; therefore, the concept of civilization could serve to provide a deeper explanation of the development of institutions of higher education.
The expansion of institutions of higher education was similar to that of modern civilization, which originated in the West and spread all over the world, especially after World War II (Eisenstadt, 2010). Modernity as a global phenomenon, which intensified after World War II, affected actions, interpretations, and habits of humans across countries, regardless of their specific civilizational roots. The processes that occurred in the modern age required various cultural entities to adopt a set of globally accepted ideas and patterns of action (Wittrock, 2000, 2001), leading to the development of a common collective identity (Eisenstadt, 2003). That is, modernization produced a uniform social and political structure across different countries and cultures (Fukuyama, 2020).
The expansion of modern civilization was made possible thanks to the growth of worldwide trade, industrialization, urbanization, the increase in literacy, social mobility, and the growth in scientific knowledge (i.e., Huntington, 2003; Fukuyama, 2020). Modernity also allowed the expansion of institutions of higher education in a process in which thousands of people passed through these institutions and were exposed to their academic culture (Bauman, 2012). The expansion of modern civilization also created universal, worldwide institutional, and symbolic frameworks and systems (Eisenstadt, 2003). Hence, the modernization process may cause largescale social changes, such as the social transformation into urban and educated middleclass societies (Fukuyama, 2020). This understanding requires a new view of institutions of higher education because higher education systems operate according to similar worldwide patterns and produce uniform structures across countries and cultures. Institutions of higher education also contribute to social and economic mobility (Eisenstadt, 2010; Marginson, 2016).
This paper is the first in a series of papers that describe the ways in which the prominent characteristics of modern civilization are reflected in institutions of higher education all over the world and allow them to be defined as a new civilization — namely, that of academic civilization. This paper argues that, like the expansion of modern civilization that began in Western countries and spread all over the world, especially after World War II, academic civilization also developed in Western countries — mainly Western Europe and the United States — and from there spread all over the world. The expansion of academic civilization necessarily includes a global, uniform, and continuous development process that requires a new analysis of institutions of higher education worldwide. This paper demonstrates that the expansion and spread of a global network of institutions of higher education around the world created the foundation for the formation of academic civilization.
## The Scope and Growth of Expansion
Modernity is the growth engine of the expansion of institutions of higher education (Windolf, 2018). Universities have a very long history and have maintained their status over the years by cultivating generations of students (Dill, 1982). Universities are the European social institution with the longest tradition, alongside the monarchy, the judiciary, and the Catholic Church (Ostling, 2018). In the modern era, the university has become a central institution of society. It is therefore important to note that universities constitute centers in which modern identity is shaped (Bourdieu, 1984; Dill, 1982; Schofer & Meyer, 2005; Wittrock, 1998) and where the collective identity of academic civilization is established.
## A Brief History
Institutions of higher education existed as far back as ancient times. For example, in 129 BC, the purpose of academies in Confucian China was to hand down the religious rituals of the ancient empire of China to future generations (Eisenstadt, 2010). Another ancient institution of higher education was Nalanda University in India (Altbach, 2014). Nalanda University was founded in the sixth century AD and attracted students and academic staff from all over the Buddhist world. It was the first academic institution that embodied the idea that the university should attract students and academic staff from all over the world (Altbach et al., 2009). Half a millennium after the founding of Nalanda University, institutions of higher education began to emerge in Europe. The universities of Bologna and Paris were established in the 11th century and were followed by universities in other parts of Europe (Eisenstadt, 2010). The universities of Bologna and Paris, in which the language of instruction was Latin, continued the tradition of enrolling students from all over Europe (Altbach et al., 2009).
Islamic institutions of higher education existed as early as ancient times. The Great Library of Alexandria, which was founded in 283–328 BC, attracted many scholars who helped establish Alexandria as a center of knowledge and learning (Welch, 2012). Additional Islamic institutions of higher education included Al-Qarawiyyin University in Morocco, which was founded in 859 AD, Al-Azhar University in Egypt, which was founded in 970-972 AD, and Nizamiyya Academy in Baghdad, which was established in 1091 AD (Sabki & Hardaker, 2013). In the 10th century AD, there were about 60 centers of learning spread across the Islamic empire (Altbach, 2016).
However, the modern university is a European invention that permeated the rest of the Western world (Altbach, 2014). The modern Western university developed from the medieval schools known as the Studia Generalia, which were institutions open to students from all parts of Europe. The main purpose of these institutions was to train monks and government officials.
The earliest Western university was the medical school in Salerno, Italy, which was founded in the ninth century and attracted students from all over Europe. The first secular university in the West was founded in Bologna at the end of the 11th century. Over the years, the University of Bologna became a highly revered institution for canonical and civil law. The first university established in Northern Europe was the University of Paris, which was founded in the 12th century. The University of Paris became known for teaching theology and served as a model for other universities in Northern Europe, such as Oxford University. The universities of Paris and Oxford were founded as corporations managed by students and masters, who adopted their patterns of conduct from popes, emperors, and kings (Perkin, 2007).
Halle University, founded in Germany by Lutherans in 1694, was one of the first institutions of higher education to renounce religious orthodoxy in favor of rational and objective intellectual inquiry, and it was the first to teach in German – that is, in the vernacular, and not in Latin. The innovations of Halle University were adopted by the University of Göttingen, which was founded in 1737, and then by most of the German universities and many American universities (Targowski, 2011).
This brief history could demonstrate that the process of the expansion of institutions of higher education was similar to the process of the expansion of modernity in Western Europe and then to North America and from there to Asia (Eisenstadt, 2003). This process of expansion around the world created unity and assimilation, and is based on global perceptions that include a complex process that could be explained by invoking the concept of civilization.
Furthermore, the expansion of modern civilization is a secular process, similar to the process of expansion of institutions of higher education, which were founded by various Christian denominations (Ford, 2017). In ancient times, training by institutions of higher education was necessary for employment as an administrator and was almost entirely entrusted to priests. Universities maintained their place as a core social institution because they provided society with values like those provided by religious institutions. Due to a process of secularization, the role of the church within the universities gradually diminished. Over time, universities offered a greater variety of areas of study, which included secular fields, and the academic staff no longer consisted solely of clergy. The Sorbonne University in Paris, founded in 1160, was an example of a university that was founded by the church. However, there were also several secular universities, such as the University of Bologna. Other major secular institutions such as Harvard University — founded in 1636 — were the first institutions of higher education in the United States (Arieli, 1967; Targowski, 2011).
Another secular university was Halle University in Germany, established in 1770, and included the first chair of pedagogy.
The University of Göttingen, which was established in 1737, adopted a practical approach, introducing research seminars and bringing training to the forefront of academic education. This approach served as a model for universities all over Europe and later in the United States. In 1810, Wilhelm von Humboldt established the first research university in Berlin, which became a model for many other research universities (Targowski, 2011). New research universities — such as Harvard University, Cornell University, and the University of Chicago, which imitated the model of Humboldt University of Berlin — were later founded in the United States. The main purpose of the research university was to develop new knowledge (Ford, 2017). Research universities have developed a common structural pattern that characterizes most institutions of higher education around the world.
The formation of civilization is regarded as a worldwide historical process (Arnason, 1988). The process of the expansion of institutions of higher education is also a worldwide historical process. Therefore, it isn’t enough to demonstrate the expansion of institutions of higher education as a phenomenon that stands on its own. Rather, it is useful to view this expansion as forming a unique civilization, which I have termed academic civilization. The purpose of this brief history was to point out the following global patterns that characterize the parallel development of modern civilization as well
- as academic civilization. Both modern and academic civilization can be said to be: 1) A process that began in Western Europe and the United States; 2) characterized by a gradual process of secularization; and 3) a gradual process of expansion.
## From Elite Institutions of Higher Education to Mass Higher Education
From a historical point of view, universities can be said to have gone through three main periods. The first period is from the end of the 18th century to the beginning of the 19th century. During this period, the viability of the university as a central social institution was gradually accepted in European countries (Wittrock, 2014). Only a small part of the population was literate, hence the small number of institutions of higher education. In addition, most educational institutions were religious, and they mainly trained clerics. The number of secular institutions began to increase while monasteries slowly began to decline, and the church lost its monopoly in higher education (Altbach, 2016).
The second period, which ranged from the early 19th century to the end of the 20th century, was a period of changes that had global consequences. During this period, conducting research became the main purpose of many universities, and universities became key institutions of modern countries. Under royal patronage in the 19th century, instead of royal academies and aristocratic salons universities became the home of intellectual activity, economic growth, and technological innovation, first in Europe and finally in the rest of the industrialized world, from California in the West to Japan in the East (Wittrock, 1998, 2014).
From the second half of the 19th century, universities that imitated the German model, which emphasized scientific expertise and pure applied research, were founded. The German university has shaped the university as an institution in the whole world from that time until the present day (Ostling, 2018). Therefore, the development of the research university and its influence on countries around the world is parallel to the influence of modern civilization, which began in Western Europe and the United States and from there spread all over the world. Furthermore, this institutional form established in Germany has become universal over time (Altbach, 2016). Like academic civilization, modern civilization originated in Western Europe and spread to the rest of the world, especially after World War II (Eisenstadt, 2010).
The influence of institutions of higher education in Western Europe and the United States is similar and even parallel to that of modernity. As modernity affected actions, worldviews and ideas, patterns of action, and the culture of humans globally (Wittrock, 2000, 2001), institutions of higher education have also had global effects. Institutions of higher education, which developed in Europe, first in Bologna and Paris and then in Germany, had a global orientation, and had a significant impact on the development of the elite institutions of higher education in the United States, such as Columbia University, Cornell University, Harvard University, Stanford University, and the University of Chicago (Altbach, 2016; Arieli, 1967). In turn, institutions of higher education in the United States have influenced higher education worldwide (Altbach, 2016). These processes of expansion and adaptation that occurred in tertiary education were parallel to processes that occurred in modernity, which expanded worldwide in that new action patterns and ideas were adopted.
Finally, the third period is the era of expansion and change that began in the second half of the 20th century, when there was a move away from the elitism of education and a transition to mass systems of higher education (Arieli, 1967; Wittrock, 2014, 2019). The rapid expansion of higher education began in the last half of the 20th century, parallel to that of modern civilization (Altbach et al., 2009). Since then, due to the increase in the demand for education in the labor market, there has been a gradual increase in the number of participants in tertiary education. In addition, the value of a high school diploma decreased as more and more people had one (Wallerstein et al., 2013). More and more employers demanded an academic education, which, in turn, led to even more students enrolling in universities (Bourdieu, 1984). During this period, modern civilization also experienced a parallel worldwide expansion (Eisenstadt, 2003).
Since World War II, industrialized countries have experienced continued growth in their institutions of tertiary education. What marked the transition from one historical stage of higher education to another was the transition from elite education to mass higher education in Europe, and from mass to universal education in the United States (Trow, 1972).
The end of World War II marked the turning point; tertiary education was no longer only for the elites but became a necessity for the masses (Targowski, 2011). In many countries, higher education began to be seen as essential for achieving social mobility and securing economic prosperity (Altbach et al., 2009). The expansion of institutions of higher education, together with the expansion of modern civilization, created a tendency to develop universal, worldwide institutional and symbolic frameworks and systems.
Higher education systems, including universities, colleges, and community colleges, expanded exponentially from the end of World War II to the end of the 20th century due to several factors. One factor was the huge number of war veterans who entered institutions of higher education (Brown, 2015). The GI Bill in the United States, for example, allowed World War II veterans to be admitted to institutions of higher education or colleges that provided professional training (Windolf, 2018). Other factors were the entry of populations that had historically been excluded, such as African Americans, the working class and the lower middle class, immigrants, and women (Brown, 2015). As of the 1970s, women began to enter institutions of higher education in growing numbers, and the percentage of women in these universities eventually overtook that of men (Freeman, 2010). In recent years, women make up at least half of the student population in the United States and Europe. In Japan, the number of women studying in institutions of higher education grew from 20 in 1960 to 40 percent in 1992 (Windolf, 2018). These figures illustrate the process of expansion of institutions of higher education, and in turn, that of academic civilization.
A broad perspective is required to understand what underlies the growth of institutions of higher education. The notion of academic civilization provides a framework that explains the global structure created by institutions of higher education. That is, institutions of higher education no longer belong only to small groups of the population, but to the entire population, which means that much of professional training worldwide is in institutions of higher education, which in turn has led to their expansion. However, the expansion of higher education institutions is only one aspect of the development of academic civilization.
The expansion of institutions of higher education was made possible thanks to various transformations. The universities in the modern era were largely dependent on the intensifying processes of urbanization (Wittrock, 1998), the development of the welfare state, an increase in the percentage of high school graduates, and a growing demand for graduates in the labor market (OECD, 2020). The expansion of institutions of higher education affected children of the working class, who were given the opportunity to enter tertiary education, allowing their children to be part of the educated class in the future (Windolf, 2018).
The global development of universities was accelerated by the internet and advanced technologies, allowing distance learning across the globe (Altbach, 2016). In other words, digital learning platforms and online programs have made higher education increasingly available (OECD, 2020). This was especially pertinent to the period of the COVID-19 pandemic.
As noted above, the expansion of institutions of higher education was made possible once it became a system open to the masses, compared to the period when only members of the social elite participated in higher education. A widely accepted division of the higher education system is into three categories: elite, mass, and universal. An elite system is a system in which access to higher education is a birthright, a right arising from talent, or both. In effect, this means that less than 15 percent of the population in the age group of 18 to 23 participate in higher education in elite universities. Conversely, higher education as a mass system is one in which access to higher education is a right for only those with certain qualifications. This means that up to 50 percent of the population in the relevant age group actually has access to higher education. The role of higher education in the mass system is to impart marketable skills and prepare the population for a wider range of technical and economic roles (Trow, 2006). More and more, the higher education system today has become an education system for the masses (Wittrock, 2014).
However, there are countries in which higher education functions as a universal system. In these education systems, access to higher education is mandatory to allow social and economic mobility for the middle and upper classes. This means that over fifty percent of the age group needs to complete higher education. The role of higher education in the universal system is to help the entire population adapt to social and technological change (Trow, 2006). To illustrate the expansion process, as of 2018, out of 176 countries, 60 countries (or 34%) have a universal higher education system. The countries with such systems are mainly in North America and Western Europe, followed by Central and Eastern Europe. Sixty-eight countries (or 39%) have a mass higher education system or are in transition to a universal system. The region with the highest proportion of countries in this category are Arab states, followed by East Asia and the Pacific, Latin America, and the Caribbean. Forty-eight countries (or 27%) have an elite higher education system, and these are mainly in sub-Saharan Africa (Calderon, 2018).
The meaning of the transition from mass higher education systems to a universal system is that most of the population in each country is entitled to participate in higher education (Calderon, 2018). It is difficult to imagine a world without institutions of higher education, without many students studying in them, without contributions of higher education to solving the most complex and important economic, technological, social, and health problems in a country (Lazerson, 1998).
Modern institutions of higher education have had a significant effect on prosperity, power, and prestige, have expanded in size and number, and have become linked to innovation processes in the economy. They have led to better jobs, higher income, higher prestige, social and economic mobility, and job security. Finally, they became gateways to the world of modernity (Lazerson, 1998; Wittrock, 2014, 2019).
## Data Demonstrating Developments in Higher Education
The data presented herein demonstrate the expansion in the number of institutions of higher education, in the number of undergraduate and graduate students, and in the number of academic staff members. These data may demonstrate the expansion process of civilization.
The expansion process of tertiary education can be seen in the number of institutions of higher education. For example, in 1950, there were 453 institutions of higher education in the United States. Approximately 72% of these institutions were affiliated with churches or religious organizations (Arieli, 1967). Additional data on the expansion of the number of institutions of higher education in the United States illustrate a growth from 1960 to 1980; initially, there were 2,008 institutions, and 20 years later, the number increased to 3,231 (Freeman, 2010). That is, the total growth in the number of institutions of higher education in the United States between 1960 and 1980 was 235% (Windolf, 2018). In fact, between 1950 and 1990, the number of academic institutions almost doubled. That is, the number of colleges and universities in the United States grew from 1,851 to 3,535 (Lazerson, 1998). Furthermore, between 1980 and 2005, the number of institutions in the United States increased from 3,231 to 4,276, that is, by 32% (Freeman, 2010). The number of tertiary institutions in the United States continued to grow and reached 4,583 in 2015/16 (Calderon, 2018).
Another example of the growth in the number of institutions of higher education is that of Great Britain (Blanden & Machin, 2004). At the beginning of the 1990s, there were 46 universities in Great Britain. By 2004, there were already over 160 public institutions of higher education in Great Britain. From 1992 to 2004, the number of universities in Great Britain more than doubled (Bryson, 2004; Ostling, 2018). Following the growth in Western countries, another example is that of China. In 1998, there were 1,095 private institutions of higher education, and in 2004, the number was 1,415 institutions (Wan, 2006).
The International Association of Universities (IAU) lists over 19,000 institutions of higher education that offer at least a bachelor's degree or an equivalent professional certificate in more than 180 countries. This number has grown continuously since it was first published in 1959 (OECD, 2019, 2020).
As of July 2020, the leading country with respect to the number of institutions of higher education is India, with 4,381 institutions, followed by the United States and China with 3,254 and 2,595 institutions, respectively (Szmigiera, 2021).
Another dimension that illustrates the process of expansion is the increase in the number of students. Before World War II, university students came mainly from the middle or upper classes and were mainly from the families of educated professionals — clergy, medicine, law, business entrepreneurs, and civil servants. After World War II, there was a growth in the number of enrollments in universities and colleges due to government support provided for the academic education of military personnel. In addition, some governments promoted better conditions for admission to universities and for tuition for candidates who, without financial support, could not have enrolled in these institutions (Shils, 1997). Moreover, progress in science, technology, and industrialization led to an increase in industrial production. The resulting improvement in the standard of living further encouraged increasing participation in higher education, mainly in the United States and Europe (Schofer & Meyer, 2005; Wallerstein et al., 2013). The worldwide expansion of institutions of higher education led to the development of assimilation patterns across different countries and was the basis for the development of a global institutional configuration, namely, that of academic civilization. The data presented below demonstrate the expansion of civilization, which is also reflected in the increase of the number of students applying for tertiary education.
In 1900, approximately 500,000 students were enrolled in institutions of higher education worldwide, representing a tiny portion — a mere 1% — of the relevant age group (Schofer & Meyer, 2005). From a worldwide perspective, by 1920, the number of undergraduate students had more than doubled to about 600,000, which constituted approximately 8% of the relevant age group. By 1940, the number of students registering for a bachelor's degree was almost 1.5 million, which means that almost 16% of the age group was now participating in higher education. In 1960, there were about 31 million students who registered for a bachelor's degree, about 33% of this age group. Thus, during a 60-year period, the rate of enrollment doubled approximately every 20 years. By 1970, the total number of students enrolled for a bachelor's degree, about 61 million students, represented 45% of the age group. During these seventy years, the graduate student population grew even more rapidly, from less than 6,000 at the beginning of the 20th century to over 850,000 in 1970 (Trow, 1972). Between the years 1970 and 2013, the number of students in the world increased by 6.12, compared to the rate at which the global population increased, which was 1.93 (Marginson, 2016).
Moreover, the estimated number of students in institutions of higher education worldwide grew from 51,160,000 in 1980 to 139,395,000 in 2006 (Altbach et al., 2009). In 2000, there were approximately 100 million students, representing an increase of approximately 206% since 1970 (Schofer & Meyer, 2005; Wallerstein et al., 2013).
Between 1970 and 2006, the number of students enrolled in institutions of higher education increased from 29 million to over 141 million (Freeman, 2010). As of 2007, there were about 150.6 million students in tertiary education worldwide — an increase of about 53% compared to UNESCO's 2000 estimate of 98.3 million students in tertiary education worldwide (Altbach et al., 2009). The estimated projections for the future are 377.4 million students worldwide by the year 2030, 471.4 million by the year 2035, and 594.1 million students by the year 2040 (Wallerstein et al., 2013).
In the United States, there was a dramatic growth in the number of students, from 170,000 students in 1950 to a peak of 5.5 million students in 1992 (Windolf, 2018). Between 1960 and 1980, student enrollment increased almost threefold, from 3.3 million to 12.1 million. Between 1980 and 2005, the number of students increased from a little over 12 million to 17.5 million — an increase of 45% (Freeman, 2010). Additional data on the growth in the number of students in Western European countries reveals the same trend. In Germany, for example, in 1870, less than 0.5% of the population studied at university, 2% in 1930, and 5% in 1960. However, in 1980, there were already 21%, and in 1992, 32% of the population had studied at university. In Germany, in the period between 1960 and 1992, there was a dramatic growth in the number of students from 270 thousand to 1.6 million.
Further evidence of the constant expansion that occurred from the second half of the 19th century until World War II is the data from Great Britain. The number of new students (men only) increased from 1,323 in 1870 to 2,507 in 1903, representing an average annual increase of 1.9% (Windolf, 2018). In Great Britain, the number of students increased from 400,000 in the 1960s to two million in the early 2000s. These data indicate that in the 2000s, one out of three people participated in higher education compared to one out of sixteen in the early 1960s (Blanden & Machin, 2004). In 2004, there were 1.8 million students in Great Britain (Bryson, 2004). In fact, from 1920 to 2016, enrollment increased by 54% (Carpentier, 2018). In 2021, approximately 2.6 million students studied in the British higher education system (Bolton, 2023; Universities UK, 2022). France is another Western country in which the number of students increased dramatically between 1960 and 1965, from 200 thousand to 400 thousand (Trow, 1972). From 1920 to 2016, enrollment in France grew by 47% (Carpentier, 2018).
The expansion of tertiary education in terms of the increase in student numbers also occurred in Asian countries. In China, since the early 1990s, there has been a dramatic increase in student enrollment (Altbach, 2016). In 1956, about 185,000 students were enrolled in institutions of higher education, and by 1960, the number had increased to 323,000 (Wan, 2006). The number of students in China was 924 thousand in 1993 and grew to almost 5.5 million students in 2006. This is an increase of 5 to 22 percent of the age group (Freeman, 2010; Wan, 2006). In 2000, the number of undergraduate students in China was approximately 7.5 million (Ostling, 2018). The total enrollment in China reached 7.3 million in 2006, 3.5 times higher than in 1997.
The enrollment rate reached 22% of age group in 2006 compared to only 3.4% in 1990 (Wu & Zheng, 2008). In 2009, almost 30 million students studied in institutions of higher education in China (Wang, & Liu, 2011). In 2021, more than 44 million students were enrolled in institutions of higher education for a variety of degrees in China (Textor, 2022). Furthermore, in India, student enrollment increased from approximately 300,000 in 1950 to nine million by the end of the century, and in 2012, the number of students reached 29 million, which means that the annual growth rate was sometimes even as high as 10% (Altbach, 2016).
The data presented constitute evidence of the expansion of academic civilization and show that higher education, which used to be the privilege of a small number of affluent people in high-income countries, spread all over the world in the last part of the 20th century and at the beginning of the 21st century (Freeman, 2010). These data also indicate that the expansion of institutions of higher education occurred alongside the parallel expansion of modern civilization and had the same effects of growth all over the world.
Like the growth in the number of undergraduate students, the number of PhD students also increased. Since a PhD degree is considered critical for advanced research, and in turn for increasing the stock of knowledge in a country, it ultimately affects the country’s economic growth (Altbach et al., 2009).
Doctoral education is mainly concentrated in the major research universities. For example, in the early 2000s, more than 40,000 PhD degrees were awarded annually in the United States (Nerad, 2004). The number of recipients of science and engineering PhD degrees outside the United States has increased sharply since 1975, while the number of PhD degrees awarded in the United States has stabilized at about 26,000 per year before a modest increase to 29,000 in 2006 (Freeman, 2010). In 1900, approximately 250 doctoral degrees were awarded from a dozen research universities in the United States. In 1916, 667 doctorates were awarded, a number that rose to 1,000 in 1923, grew to 33,755 in 1973, and reached a peak in 1998 with 42,683 doctorates. This growth represents an average increase of about 7% per year since the early seventies. By 1999, the number of doctoral degree recipients was more than 41,000 from approximately 400 institutions of higher education throughout the United States. That is, more than half of the doctoral degrees were awarded between 1980 and 1999. By the end of the 20th century, more than 1.36 million doctoral degrees had been awarded by 426 American tertiary institutions (Thurgood et al., 2006).
Parallel to the expansion of higher education described above, modern civilization expanded from the West to the East. The greatest increase in the number of PhD students occurred in China. In 1975, almost no doctoral students were trained in science and engineering in China. In the year 2004, 23,000 PhD students graduated in the year 2004, approximately 63% in science and engineering (Freeman, 2010). Data on the number of those eligible for a PhD in Israel demonstrates the same trend. The number of PhD students who graduated in Israel increased from 1,288 in 2006/7 to 1,811 in 2019/2020, which is an increase of about 41% (Israel National Academy of Sciences, 2022). As of 2020, approximately 1,800 students received a PhD, a figure that indicates an increase of 10.3% compared to the previous year and an increase of approximately 4.8% per year since the 1990s (Israel Central Bureau of Statistics, 2021).
In Brazil, the number of PhD graduates represents more than half of the doctoral degrees awarded in Latin America (Altbach, 2016). From the year 2000, the Brazilian tertiary education system awarded about 10,000 doctorates each year, a 300% increase in 10 years (Altbach et al., 2009). Figure 2 illustrates the growth in the number of PhD graduates in three different countries: the United States, Israel, and China. All three countries experienced growth; from 1998 to 2006, the number of PhD degrees awarded increased by 7%, 380%, and 260%, respectively (Israel Central Bureau of Statistics, n.d; National Center for Science and Engineering Statistics, n.d; Snyder & Dillow, 2012; Wu & Zheng, 2008).

Parallel to the growth in the number of students, the number of faculty members also expanded, though at a slower pace (Yudkevich, 2020). Although the number of academic faculty members at colleges and universities in the United States has increased steadily from the 1970s, from the late 1990s to the early 2000s, the number of permanent faculty members did not increase at the same rate as the growth in the student population (Nerad, 2004). In 1900, there were approximately 25,000 faculty members in the United States. By 1970, there were almost half a million faculty members (Trow, 1972). In Great Britain there was also an increase in the number of academic staff members, with an increase of 60%between 1986 and 1997. In 2004, approximately 130,000 faculty members were employed by institutions of higher education in Great Britain (Bryson, 2004). By 2021, the number of academic faculty members in Great Britain had increased to approximately 440,000 (Bolton, 2023; Universities UK, 2022).
The increase in the number of academic staff members also occurred in China, although it has been relatively slow, increasing from 404,000 in 1997 to more than one million in 2006 (Wu & Zheng, 2008). In Israel, the number of senior academic faculty members in universities has increased since 1980 by approximately 40% from approximately 3,900 to 5,426 in 2018. In 2021, there were 35,325 academic faculty members in Israeli universities and academic colleges, compared to 34,237 in the previous academic year, which represents an increase of approximately 3.2%. Over a period of ten years, between 2011 and 2021, 9,200 faculty members were added, an average annual growth rate of 3% (Kuprak, 2022).
The expansion of higher education all over the world was due to the fact that the demand for tertiary education was deemed essential for the labor market (Windolf, 2018). During the 19th century, the focus of institutions of higher education was the creation of new knowledge. Rapid expansion began in the number of universities, in the number of countries that have universities, and in the number of students enrolling in these universities (Meyer et al., 2007). The growth in the number of institutions of higher education and the growth in the number of students also led to a parallel expansion of modern civilization (Huntington, 2003). The expansion of modern civilization was the growth engine fueling the expansion of institutions of higher education (Windolf, 2018) and of academic civilization.
## Conclusion
The aim of this paper was to demonstrate a basis for understanding the global process by which a network of institutions of higher education was created around the world, especially after World War II, that may be regarded as civilizational development. This article demonstrates only one of the six characteristics of academic civilization cited at the beginning of this article. The worldwide expansion process of institutions of higher education illustrated above can best be explained by employing a broader concept namely that of academic civilization — since it best conveys the similarity of this broad concept to modern civilization, a sociological phenomenon or process that operates according to similar patterns.
The expansion in the number of institutions of higher education and in the number of students constitutes a global socio-cultural phenomenon. Higher education around the world has expanded over the years; that is, higher education, which was previously limited to only a few small groups, has become accessible to the masses. Institutions of higher education could therefore be considered a network that trains people and operates according to similar patterns all over the world. This expansion is a worldwide process that has similar socio-cultural patterns that can be demonstrated as civilizational patterns.
Understanding modern civilization may help identify similar patterns and frameworks in institutions of higher education around the world, which have formed a civilization in their own right. Academic civilization, like modern civilization, developed in Western countries, mainly in Western Europe and the United States, and from there spread throughout the world, especially after World War II. Also, like the expansion of modern civilization, institutions of higher education also expanded both quantitatively and qualitatively (i.e, Wittrock, 2019). The process of expansion began
- at the end of the 19th century and has continued since then (Windolf, 2018).
The purpose of this article was to provide an initial description of the formation of academic civilization, namely, an institutional and social structure that applies to every country around the world. The expansion of institutions of higher education, and mainly research universities, has led to the development of universal, worldwide institutional, and symbolic frameworks and systems (Eisenstadt, 2003), thus forming a civilization.
Institutions of higher education in general, and especially research universities, have played an increasingly central role in almost all modern societies. Therefore, the modern research university continues to maintain its centrality as a research institution and as the most important institution for training personnel for almost all professions in the modern era (Shils, 1997). Conceptualizing actions of institutions of higher education in civilizational terms facilitates an understanding of this broad phenomenon. This understanding includes how institutions of higher education influence society, culture, the economy, and politics, and in what ways academic civilization developed in parallel to the development of modern civilization.
This article is the first in a series of articles that will describe how prominent characteristics of modern civilization are reflected in academic civilization. The following articles will refer to institutional flexibility and diversity, center-periphery relations, academic culture, autonomy and academic freedom, and the monopolization of professional training and the professional middle class. Furthermore, subsequent articles will emphasize the interrelationships between these characteristics and present the academic civilization in the light of the theory of multiple modernities, the convergence theory, and the clash of civilizations.
As demonstrated in this paper, the development of modern civilization, especially since World War II, was made possible thanks to the expansion of institutions of higher education. In addition, institutions located in the West also influenced institutions of higher education in non-Western countries. Through institutions of higher education, Western countries have established themselves in the world as determining many economic, social, and political developments. In other words, institutions of professional training and research have allowed Western countries to firmly establish and shape modern civilization. Understanding the characteristics of academic civilization facilitates a broader discussion of institutions of higher education — core institutions of society — and of modern civilization. This might also facilitate an understanding of the similar civilizational patterns of both academic and modern civilization in historical, cultural, and sociological terms as a broad worldwide process.
