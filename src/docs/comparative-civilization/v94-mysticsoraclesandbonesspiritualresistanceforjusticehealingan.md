Leah Hollis Penn State University ljh5876@psu.edu Abstract
This conceptual essay examines the enduring human impulse to seek meaning, justice, and healing through mystical traditions in response to suffering and harm. When traditional solutions through grievance, the law, or mediation fails, some individuals may turn to spiritual practices not as superstition, but as embodied strategies of survival, reclamation, and clarity.
Comparing diverse traditions from a variety of cultures, including oracle bone divination in ancient China, the Oracle at Delphi in Greece, West African Ifá, Benin’s Voudou, diasporic Hoodoo, Celtic Druidic law, and Sufi mysticism, this paper explores how mystics have long served as interpreters of suffering and perceived facilitators of moral balance. The analysis also considers contemporary echoes in cultural narratives, such as The Matrix and Star Wars, where mystical figures guide the oppressed toward liberation. These traditions offer more than metaphors; they are vehicles for spiritual resistance, especially for those marginalized by bureaucratic systems.
Ultimately, this essay argues for the recognition of “mystical resistance” as a global and historical phenomenon.
Keywords: justice, mysticism, bones Introduction
Two seemingly unrelated thoughts inspired this analysis. First, a recent visit to Delphi in Greece gave firsthand insight to how the culture for hundreds of years sought an oracle to forecast success, failure, and mystery (Fontenrose, 2021). Wealthy patrons paid handsomely to receive priority in ceremonies through prothysia, an advanced offering that allowed them to skip the line (Côté-Landry, 2014). Messages from the oracle guided leaders and politicians but did such with ambiguous replies, allowing for the patrons to exercise wide interpretation of such messages.
Second, my studies in workplace bullying led to an article where research asked targets of workplace bullying to use a voodoo doll to find justice (Liang et al., 2018). Study participants who were subject to abusive supervision (Tepper, 2000) were invited to use the voodoo doll, representing their supervisor, and then retaliate through that doll.
Liang et al (2018) wrote: “engaging in symbolic retaliation reduced the extent to which recalling abusive supervision led to implicit injustice perceptions” (p. 451).
These two ideas together led me to think of how people have sought mystical and metaphysical support to exact justice and alleviate suffering throughout history, even if through unconventional methods. After visiting Delphi, I considered mystical traditions extant in various cultures across time and history, even in the mind’s eye of the cinematic imagination.
Throughout civilizations, cultures, and centuries, those who suffer, especially under the cruelty of power imbalances and interpersonal harm, ask a haunting question: Why is this happening to me? This is not just an individual cry for clarity, but a collective inquiry into injustice, insecurity, and the unseen forces that seem to govern suffering. For example, in post- apartheid South Africa, many communities are still facing acute political and economic problems. Stemming from a spiritual insecurity, throughout decades of apartheid regimes, witchcraft-related crimes are attempts to exact justice in politics (Petrus, 2012). However, South Africa is not alone. Geschiere (1997) has argued that ‘Nearly everywhere in Africa, discourses on power continue to be marked by these notions [of the occult, sorcery, and witchcraft] (Petrus, 2012, p. 180).
Economic and political abuse engulf many global communities over centuries of war, unrest, and domineering societies abusing others. Consequently, several cultures have traditions where people seek understanding and resolution not through confrontation, but through communion with the divine, with ancestors, and with the unseen. Therefore, this conceptual essay draws on comparative traditions to illuminate how mystical practice functions as a response to injustice and as a pathway to resolution.
While Native American, South Asian, and Latin American systems are equally rich, they are only briefly acknowledged to maintain a coherent thematic frame. For example, in Latin American contexts, Curanderismo has blended indigenous, Catholic, and African elements into a syncretic healing tradition used to restore spiritual and physical balance, especially amid colonial and systemic trauma (Hendrickson, 2013; Trotter & Chavira, 1997). Similarly, in South Asia, East Indian spiritual systems like Tantra and Ayurveda incorporate energy work, mantras, and divination to navigate suffering and uphold dharma, often turning inward when external institutions fail (Feuerstein, 1998; Stoddart, 2007).
This paper does not claim to document exhaustively all mystical practices across the globe. Rather, it offers a focused comparative analysis of select traditions, Ifá, Voudou, Sufism, Delphi, and Celtic Druidry, representing different regions and epistemologies.
## Defining Mystical Resistance
Mystical resistance is the embodied practice of drawing on ancestral wisdom, spiritual ritual, and non-institutional forms of knowing to confront harm, reclaim dignity, and restore personal power. Mystical practices are invoked when formal systems – legal, academic, and bureaucratic systems – fall short of generating a resolution that ends suffering. At times, these very systems advance suffering instead of justice.
In turn, people often will seek alternative pathways to resolve a problem, for example, through various symbolic acts such as animal sacrifice (Smith & Doniger, 1989). They seek prayer, divination, and energy work, with some practices relying on a conduit such as a shaman, rootworker, or Druid to translate messages from nature. Regardless of the cultural pathways from which these practices emerge, they mainly have origins that predate Western thought.
This counter epistemology seeks alternative truth and resolutions from the earth, the altar, the ancestor, and the intuitive self, decentering Western mainstream thought. Mysticism has been treated with healthy skepticism regardless of its cultural origins (Qadi & Tiaynen-Qadir, 2025). Consequently, mystically-informed practices invite justice into a space that is beyond unjust. Such practices move through the perception of spiritual grounding to address the political, cultural, and deeply personal. Mystical perspectives recognize the tremendous harm and hold that healing also is possible, even if through alternative pathways (Harris, 2017).
## Society is Suffering: The Health Toll of Invisible Harm
While mystical figures in film offer metaphorical guidance and symbolic truth, realworld suffering often lacks such clarity or catharsis. Outside the realm of myth and metaphor, people navigating injustice and institutional harm must contend with tangible psychological and physical tolls.
To understand why mysticism continues to hold appeal, we must first examine the lived realities of suffering that drive individuals toward alternative forms of healing and resistance. Abuse, bullying, and discrimination are not simply social justice problems. They are at the root of profound human suffering, with direct implications for physical and psychological health (Gerhardt et al 2021; Hollis, 2024; Steinmann, 2016). Targets of sustained of aggressions experience symptoms consistent with complex trauma, including insomnia, anxiety, depression, and somatic complaints such as migraines, digestive disorders, and elevated blood pressure (Einarsen et al., 2011; Hollis, 2021). These conditions are not incidental; they emerge from prolonged exposure to psychosocial stress in environments where one’s safety, dignity, and self-worth are systematically undermined.
For those from historically marginalized groups, injustice often takes the form of racialized or gendered microaggressions, producing what scholars term “racial battle fatigue” (Smith et al., 2007). The cumulative weight of exclusion, surveillance, and silencing becomes a form of internalized warfare. In such conditions, traditional institutional remedies are often ineffective or complicit, leaving the bullied to seek healing elsewhere.
Enter mystical solutions for those desperate for relief, not as escapism, but as a countermedical and counter-institutional tool of care; such practices can include psychedelic experiences, to cope with depression and anxiety (Wießner et al., 2023). Rituals, ancestral invocation, and spiritual cleansing become responses to psychic and physiological injury. When one’s health is compromised by invisible harm, the act of reclaiming the body and spirit through ritual is not just cultural; the practice espouses restorative medicine.
## Mysticism Across Several Cultures
Across diverse cultures and epochs, mystical systems have arisen as vital mechanisms for restoring ethical balance, interpreting suffering, and resisting forms of structural injustice. Despite differing rituals and worldviews, these traditions converge in function: they offer clarity, healing, and moral recalibration when institutional or legal systems are failing. This essay will explore several such traditions, illuminating how mystical resistance serves as an alternate pathway toward justice and transformation.
## African Voudou and Ifa
In Benin and other parts of West Africa, mystics serve not as performers of dark arts, but as conduits for justice, prosperity, and healing. Through their ignorance, colonists often misunderstood Voudou as a dark and unholy African set of practices. Its rituals are grounded in accountability, communal balance, and honoring of the ancestors. This lineage brought the colonialist view that it was too black and too African.
Nonetheless, Voudou with ritual ground in accountability and honoring communal balance and ancestral influence is a hybrid of Yoruba and Catholic practices (Clark, 2020). Those harmed, including victims of interpersonal aggression and chronic injustice, may turn to rituals not out of superstition, but out of a desire to restore equilibrium. In a world rife with racial discrimination, Voudou practitioners seek power and justice (Clark, 2020).
When Voudou merged with Christianity through the Atlantic slave trade, it evolved into Hoodoo and the Creole Voodoo of New Orleans. Throughout the African diaspora, it includes Santeria in Cuba and Umbanda in Brazil (Fandrich, 2007).
Here too, the mystic, as known as the conjurer or the root worker, becomes a healer, often sought not for vengeance but for peace. Herbs, candles, ancestors, and prayers are not weapons, but rather they are tools to reclaim dignity in a system that has denied such dignity to marginalized people
In West African cosmologies, mysticism is not an escape from the world; it creates a blueprint for restoring moral and spiritual balance. Systems like Voudou in Benin and Ifá among the Yoruba people of Nigeria are not “superstitions” but rather deeply structured methods of seeking justice, clarity, and personal alignment (Cavender,1988). Ifá, specifically, is a sophisticated divination system that involves a trained babalawo (diviner) using sacred palm nuts and the opele chain to consult the Odu Ifá, a corpus of poetic wisdom and cosmological insight. The divination process is dialogic, rooted in both spiritual communion and practical reasoning.
Babaláwo serve as check and balance for the kings and their subjects in ancient Yorùbá society; they were the custodians and enforcers of ethical codes. Therefore, they ensure proper political and administration of justice to make the then society free from corruption, nepotism, favoritism, and all sorts of social vices that characterize the modern Yorùbá society (Ọláléyẹ̣ & Adekoya, 2024, p. 107).
Through Ifá, individuals and communities seek guidance not only about personal dilemmas, but also about how to uphold justice, resist exploitation, and maintain spiritual integrity amid harm (Aina, 2022). The presence of Orisha deities further underscores the moral infrastructure of the practice; every spirit embodies virtues, taboos, and lessons for how to live ethically even under duress.
An example of how this tradition is used is found in 19th-century New Orleans. There, enslaved and free Black communities turned to Voudou not as superstition, but as a structured system for protection, balance, and spiritual justice, particularly when legal systems denied them rights or protection. One prominent figure was Marie Laveau, the legendary Voudou priestess. When marginalized people, especially Black women, experienced betrayal, abuse, or social injustice, they came to her not to curse others, but to restore balance through ritual (Chireau, (2003; Laughlin, 2023).
## Delphi: Spiritual Bureaucracy and Seeking the Divine
The Oracle at Delphi was one of the most revered religious and political institutions in the ancient Greek world (Salt & Boutsikas, 2005). Located at the sanctuary of Apollo on Mount Parnassus, the Pythia, or priestess of Apollo, served as the mouthpiece for divine insight (Tully, 2008). Seated above a chasm from which sacred vapors were said to rise, the Pythia would enter a trance-like state and deliver cryptic prophecies, often composed in poetic or ambiguous phrases (Spiller, et al 2002).
Far from being mere spiritual entertainment, the Oracle’s pronouncements shaped decisions of war, colonization, justice, and diplomacy (Crawford, 2016).
Consulting the Oracle was a formalized ritual involving purification, sacrifice, and payment (Scott, 2014). As noted above, wealthy or influential petitioners could offer a prothysia, a pre-sacrifice or bribe, to secure earlier access, illustrating how mystical knowledge was both sacred and stratified. Despite this hierarchy, the perceived neutrality and spiritual authority of the Oracle gave her pronouncements immense weight. Even powerful leaders like Croesus of Lydia and the city-states of Athens and Sparta deferred to her wisdom before undertaking major actions (Balcer, 1994).
What made Delphi unique was not just its mystical atmosphere, but its fusion of spiritual and civic life. The Oracle operated as a kind of spiritual bureaucracy, mediating between human desire and divine will. As Maurizio (1995) argues, the Pythia’s ambiguous speech allowed communities to project meaning, responsibility, or divine endorsement onto decisions they were already considering. In this way, Delphi offered not just prophecy, but permission, a mystical infrastructure that shaped public and private morality in equal measure (Bowden, 2005).
In the 630s BCE, Athens faced a plague and unrest. Many believed the gods were punishing them and sought out the Oracle at Delphi for a resolution. On the word of the Oracle, the Alcmaeonidae, an elite group that practiced sacrilegious executions was expelled. In this case, the Oracle guidance superseded the wealthy class, and Solon was installed as the new leader. This change led to significant political and economic reform at the origin of Athenian democracy (Rood, 2013).
## Celtic Mysticism: Nature, Druidic Law, and Sacred Rhythm
Celtic people from ancient Britain and Ireland at times sought out Druids, a learned priestly and judicial class. Julius Caesar mentioned Druid mystics and how they held a significant role in Celtic culture which exempted them from military service and taxes (Caesar, 1951). Though they carried names like “worshippers of nature, prophets, magicians, occultists, diviners and priests, some might claim Druids were mythical” (Anczyk, 2012, p. 99). However, Chadwick (1966) deemed this group to be advanced, even more so than their Roman and Greek contemporaries. To arrive at this conclusion, Chadwick had to rely on the writing of another culture, Latin, because Druids did not leave extensive written records (Turner, 1966).
Celtic mysticism wove together nature, justice, and spiritual rhythm into a single, living system. At the heart of this system stood the Druid, not simply a priest or magician, but a philosopher, judge, healer, and seer (Winters, 2024). They served as legal experts and educators relying on nature and their additional connection with the sun and stars.
Hence, Druids united with nature to serve as the interconnective figures of Celtic culture who facilitated law and justice (Kirner, 2024).
Druids, who often trained for close to two decades, learned about natural power and the use of plants and herbs to generate restorative services (Winters, 2024). Their rituals often took place in nemeta, that is sacred groves, underscoring their reverence for the earth as a site of divine wisdom (Green & Watson, 1915; Wittingslow, 2015).
Central to Druidic practice was the belief that all actions ripple through a sacred order of rhythm uniting humans, nature, and the unseen. To commit injustice was to create imbalance, not only in society but in the fabric of the natural world. Thus, the Druid’s role was not to punish, but to restore harmony. Through rituals, poetic incantations, and judgment, they worked to re-align the community with what some scholars call imbas forosnai, a practice of ingesting raw flesh and seeking another plane of consciousness through chanting (Mulligan, 2009).
In pre-Roman Celtic societies, Druids served as the principal interpreters of customary law, playing a crucial role in maintaining social order and delivering justice. As Julius Caesar noted in Commentarii de Bello Gallico (Book 6), Druids presided over both civil and criminal cases, holding the authority to issue binding judgments. Those who defied their rulings could be banished — a severe penalty that effectively rendered individuals outcasts from community life.
By mediating conflicts and offering trusted decisions grounded in oral legal traditions, Druids helped prevent cycles of violent retribution. In the absence of written law, their role was essential in promoting fairness, limiting the abuses of powerful elites, and safeguarding weaker clans from domination (Ellis, 2002; Green, 1997).
## Sufism: Transforming the Wound into Light
Sufism, the mystical dimension of Islam, invites believers into an inward journey where suffering is not denied but transfigured. Rooted in the Qur’anic emphasis on remembrance (dhikr) and divine proximity, Sufi practice centers on purifying the heart and aligning the self with God’s love (Ernst, 1997; Schimmel, 1975). Their practice has a creative approach with whirling and poetry couched in repetition. Sufis seek tawḥīd, which Shapoo (2018) states is when one seeks a unity with the unquestionable singularity of Allah. In Islam, God does not have partners; Sufi practice at times has translated a mystical experience to a unity with God. This spiritual path offers not just transcendence, but transformation: a reordering of suffering into sacred meaning.
The notion that pain is a spiritual teacher lies at the heart of Sufi mysticism. Bond (2021) examines how Sindhi Sufi performers view expressions of pain in music and poetry as acts of Islamic devotion.
Such pain is seen as spiritually beneficial, offering tranquility and heart purification. Bond (2021) also explores how the theme of pain, particularly separation, reflects shifting Hindu-Muslim dynamics in a region marked by rising religious tension. For those who endure injustice, betrayal, or marginalization Sufism offers an alternative theology: suffering is not failure, but formation.
As Rumi, the 13th-century Persian Sufi, wrote, “The wound is the place where the Light enters you.” (Rumi, 1995). Rather than viewing harm as divine abandonment, Sufism sees it as an aperture for revelation. The heart, cracked open, becomes more receptive to love, mercy, and divine presence. Sufi thinkers like Al-Ghazali describe trials as catalysts that strip ego, purify intention, and awaken the soul’s latent capacities. AlGhazali emphasizes the mystical inner transformation as a direct experience of the divine, and the soul’s purification as a path to truth, all core features of Islamic mysticism, Sufism (Al-Ghazali, 2001). Salvation and peace emerge when one relinquishes material and earthly materials. (Nasr, 2007). In this view, the path to God is not smooth but scarring, and those scars shimmer with spiritual light. Sufism thus stands with other mystical traditions in converting harm into healing, offering not just solace but transformation.
An example of how Sufism was used in social justice initiatives occurred in North African conflict. During the 19th and early 20th centuries, Sufi brotherhoods played a pivotal role in resisting colonial oppression.
In Algeria, one of the most influential figures of this spiritual-political movement was Emir Abd al-Qadir, a devout Sufi. Far from retreating into mysticism, Abd al-Qadir drew upon the ethical foundations of Sufism to lead organized resistance against French colonial rule, establishing a governance model rooted in justice, discipline, and moral integrity (Achrati, 2007). Even amid active colonialism, Abd al-Qadir used Sufism to advance significant transformation. With Sufism incorporated into the campaign, instead of setting it aside, he showed that ethical practices and inner purification aligned with his mission. This delayed the onset of French rule for over twenty years (Sessions, 2005). Despite the surrender, Abd-al Qadir then saved thousands of Christians in the 1860 Damascus riots (Fawaz, 2001).

## Messages Cast in Bone
Bones in Zulu divination are far more than tools; they are sacred conduits between the living and the ancestral, material anchors for interpreting spiritual messages. As Cumes (2002) explains in Zulu Dreamscapes, reading the bones is akin to interpreting a dream: each bone cast is a sensory and symbolic act, requiring intuition, training, and ancestral permission. The tactile engagement, touching, arranging, and discerning their positions, translates otherwise invisible forces into visible signs. The bones map not only spiritual realities but also moral and emotional landscapes, offering insight into illness, conflict, or destiny.
Similarly, in Chang’s study (2013) of ukuthwasa initiation rituals, bones are central to a diviner’s transformation. Initiates must undergo rigorous training to interpret bone configurations, and acquiring a personal bone set (amathambo) is a major rite of passage. These bones are ritually empowered, often tied to the lineage of divining ancestors, and they become an extension of the body’s own sensory awareness.
In ancient Chinese mysticism, particularly during the Shang dynasty (ca. 1600-1046 BCE), practitioners used bones in certain situations as a pathway to the spirit world. With the practice of oracle bone divination, or pyro-osteomancy, practitioners would write questions for ancestral spirits onto ox scapulae or turtle plastrons (Helle, 2017), then apply heat until cracks emerged. These cracks were interpreted as answers from the spirit realm.
As Hung-hsiang Chou (1979) explains, this early writing system, etched into bone, represented one of humanity’s oldest surviving forms of recorded communication, blending spiritual inquiry with linguistic innovation. The bones became a material interface for dialogue with ancestors, deities, and natural forces, asking about rainfall, childbirth, warfare, illness, and dreams.
In the Yoruba Ifa tradition, bones have played a symbolic and functional role in divination, particularly through their use as Ibo, divinatory instruments often paired with cowries. These items are placed on a white cloth during divination sessions and are understood to hold communicative power between the physical and spiritual worlds. While cowries are more commonly recognized, the bones function as part of the interpretive mechanism, offering binary or directional guidance during the consultation process (Owumi & Okewole, 2014, p. 158).
When a client presents a problem, the Ifa priest, or babaláwo, employs tools such as the Opele (divination chain), sacred palm nuts (Ikin), and ibo, including bones, to determine the appropriate odu or corpus that will guide diagnosis and healing. This practice highlights the embodied and metaphysical interplay between material objects (like bones) and divine messages from Orunmila, the deity of wisdom and prophecy.
More than simple tokens, the bones used in Ifa serve as votive instruments during consultations, representing decisions or directions from the divine when tossed and interpreted. Their placement and orientation in relation to other divinatory objects are believed to convey responses from the orisa or ancestral forces (Owumi & Okewole, 2014). While herbal and sacrificial elements form the core of therapeutic action in Ifa medicine, the consultation process mediated through these sacred objects, including bones, is considered essential to uncovering the spiritual root of illness and determining the appropriate remedy. The interaction of Ibo with the Opele and other sacred instruments underscores the Ifa belief that health is governed by a balance of visible and invisible forces, and that bones, as conduits of meaning, help to decode the unseen world through ritualized practice.
According to Vest (1988), in Lakota spiritual practice, bones, especially those from sacred animals like the buffalo, are revered as vessels of spiritual power, often included in sacred bundles used by healers and vision seekers. Many Lakota saw the bones as harboring a spiritual essence that connected then with ancestors and the spiritual word. Therefore, given the belief, bones were ideal for healing situations and sacred guidance.
Similarly, the Crow Indians used bones for healing and generating mystic visions. Medical bundles in the Crow tradition included bones as a source of supernatural power. Their practices inform a deeply shamanistic worldview in which bones functioned as material evidence of spiritual engagement (Liberty, 1970).
## Prominent Historical Figures Using Mysticism
Throughout history, mystics and seers have advised the powerful, the persecuted, and the visionary. Alexander the Great consulted the Oracle of Ammon in the Siwa Oasis before advancing into Persia (Kuhlmann, 1988). Queen Elizabeth I employed astrologer and mystic John Dee, whose scrying and esoteric knowledge influenced both court politics and the early British Empire (Woolley, 2002). Napoleon Bonaparte sought guidance from card readers and dream interpreters before major battles (Willett, 2024). In the United States, President Abraham Lincoln is said to have held séances in the White House, turning to spiritual mediums for solace and clarity (Martinez, 2009). Also, Harriet Tubman, known for her strategic genius on the Underground Railroad, reportedly relied on visions and spiritual intuition, shaped by her deep religious beliefs (Taylor-Stinson, 2023).
More recently, figures like Mahatma Gandhi and Malcolm X drew from spiritual practices, Hindu fasting and Islamic prayer, respectively, as sources of moral compass and resistance (Kaplan, 1969; Sharma, 2001). Also, Ronald and Nancy Reagan famously consulted astrologer Joan Quigley to schedule major events, especially following the 1981 assassination attempt (Seaman, 2002).
These examples show that mystical counsel is not a fringe phenomenon, but a recurring and global feature of human leadership, often invoked at moments of uncertainty, injustice, or profound transition. Whether in empires, revolutions, or liberation movements, mystics have helped shape the moral and strategic paths of history.
## Mysticism in Cinematic Culture
The mystic gives voice to the unseen. Even in modern mythologies, we see this figure reappear. In the 1999 film The Matrix, a world engineered by surveillance and control still carves out space for mysticism. The Oracle, a seemingly ordinary woman baking cookies in a simulated apartment, holds divine insight coded within a corrupted system. She does not fight with weapons but instead with prophecy. Her visitors, rebels like Neo and Morpheus, regard her as sacred. Their reverence for the Oracle is not because she tells them what to do, but because she helps them uncover who they are. She is not separate from the overbearing program that terrorizes human existence, but she resists it from within. Through her practice, she mirrors the work of ancestral mystics: to guide us through illusion, to return us to self, to speak truth in systems built on deception.
In a world run by binaries, yes or no, choice or control, red pill or blue, the Oracle is the third space. She bakes cookies, yes, but she also destabilizes destiny. She tells Neo he is not The One but knows he must choose to become it. Her brilliance lies in trusting that truth must be lived, not dictated. Her ambiguity is not evasion, but an invitation.
A similar mystic and great teacher holds a leading role in the Star Wars narratives. Yoda, the diminutive Jedi Master, is not merely a warrior monk; he is a mystic sage. Living in exile on Dagobah, he trains seekers through ambiguity and paradox. “You must unlearn what you have learned.” Like the Oracle, Yoda does not hand over truth; he insists it be earned through stillness, discipline, and surrender to the Force. He embodies the mystic path: not domination, but alignment – not certainty but awakening.
Yoda and the Oracle are sci-fi mystics in programmed and galactic empires, yet they mirror the same lineage: the sangoma, the Druid, the diviner. They teach that even in futures full of data and warfare, the spirit leads to resolution. These cinematic mystics echo ancient mystics from Delphi to Ifá. The Pythia’s responses were often cryptic. Ifá diviners recite verses that must be interpreted in context. These mystics do not provide answers but chart a path to truth. The one seeking peace and healing must engage the transformative actively. In every age, under every sky, even in imagined futures, humans pursue peaceful and balanced peace.
## Harm and the Turn to Mystical Resistance
The psychological and spiritual injury that disenfranchised people endure can be a gateway to mystical and transformative experiences. From another vantage point, when formal processes through medical, legal, and other process intended to resolve problems fail people, humans often still seek a resolution whether, ancestral, symbolic, and unseen solution. Lighting candles, throwing bones, reciting sacred poetry, or invoking deities become more than ritual; these non-traditional activities are the refusal to be erased (Cavender 1988; Sebald, 2013).
Just as the Oracle at Delphi offered cryptic truth to those seeking clarity (Scott, 2020), modern oracles, whether energy workers or ancestral mediums, provide language, ritual, and orientation for those bullied into silence (Green, 2021; Schmidt, 2015). These practices resist invisibility and assert dignity in hostile spaces. The mystical, then, becomes a form of non-institutional justice, a sacred archive of survival when HR departments and legal systems collapse under the weight of bureaucracy or bias.
## Conclusion: The Peace We Keep Seeking
These traditions, whether rooted in African soil, Greco-Roman myth, Celtic forests, or Arabic poetry, affirm that peace is not passivity (Donald, 2023). Often peace is the posture of defiance against meaningless tyranny. Peace and stability are universal human rights, one which society will continuously seek (Hollis, 2022). Lighting a candle, calling an ancestor, walking a labyrinth, visiting a psychic are activities which seem superstitious to some, but to many they are acts of resistance. From Delphi to Benin, from the New Orleans Voudou to the Celtic grove, humans have sought resolution through the mystical.
Such a search occurs not because logic has failed, but because suffering demands meaning. Given the documented goal of mystical traditions over centuries to recalibrate justice and society, one may ponder why modern institutions continue to ignore these practices throughout restorative frameworks.
