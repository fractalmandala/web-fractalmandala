The International Society for the Comparative Study of Civilizations (presently known as the Comparative Civilizations Forum) recently converged in South Korea for its 54th conference.
The forum entitled Science, Civilization, and State: How Civilizations are Affected by Science and the State - Past, Present and Future took place on hospitable grounds of beautiful Hallym University / Doheon Academy campus in the city of Chuncheon (Republic of Korea) from September 10 to September 15, 2025.
Picture 1. ISCSC/ CCF-2025 Conference Participants among other visitors on the Observation Deck above historic city of Chuncheon. Photo by Vlad Alalykin-Izvekov.
Hallym University is a private university located in the city of Chuncheon (“The city of lakes”) about one hour by train north-east of Seoul. The city of Chuncheon has been named one of the best places to live in South Korea based on, among other things, climate, cost of living and social conditions.
The university was founded in 1982 and is one of the most prominent private universities in the country. The school has received the status “Distinguished University” by the South Korean Ministry of Education several times.
The university campus is situated at the foot of Bong Ui Mountain in central Chuncheon. The university’s student population is just over 8,000. Since its foundation in 1982, this ambitious institution has strived to nurture its students into leaders of tomorrow.
The campus is tastefully and abundantly decorated with the arts. Among others, conference participants immediately identified Michelangelo’s David (Florence, Italy), an iconic Fountain of Apollo (Bassin d’Apollon) from Versailles, France, August Rodin’s Thinker, as well as The End of the Trail sculpture by American artist James Earle Fraser.
Next to the university’s Central Library sits Auguste Rodin’s brooding statue The Thinker. It has famously become an iconic symbol of thinking and contemplation in the West.
The inquisitive conference participants soon discovered a no less iconic and charismatic counterpart to Rodin’s masterpiece at the National Museum in Chuncheon. It was a beautiful marble Bodhisattva (The Enlightened One). Displayed in its own darkened gallery, the figure was illuminated with a subtle, focused light that made it appear almost otherworldly, shimmering like a celestial presence.
Carved from luminous white marble during the Goryeo period (918–1392 CE), this serene bodhisattva reflects the refined spiritual and artistic sensibilities of medieval Korean Buddhism. Once enshrined at Hansongsa Temple in Gangneung, it welcomed worshippers as a symbol of compassion and enlightened guidance.
In 1912, during the Japanese occupation, the statue was taken to Japan. Its repatriation in 1965 came to symbolize cultural restitution and historical justice. Now exhibited at the Chuncheon National Museum, its tranquil face and graceful form continue to inspire a sense of calm across the centuries.

In the end, comparing Rodin’s Thinker and the Goryeo Bodhisattva becomes less a contest of “which is greater” and more a conversation across civilizations. One embodies the restless, analytic striving of the Western mind - muscles tense, thought wrestled into form. The other radiates the poised stillness of Eastern contemplation wisdom understood not as struggle, but as serene insight.
Encountered within the same city, these two icons remind us that civilizations do not merely coexist; they illuminate one another. At Chuncheon - on a university lawn and in a quiet museum gallery - East and West appear not as opposites, but as complementary expressions of humanity’s enduring search for meaning. Perhaps this is the deeper lesson our conference participants carried home: that the world’s great traditions of thought are not rivals, but partners on a shared, long journey toward understanding.
