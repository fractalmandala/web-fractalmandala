Hisanori Kato Chuo University, Tokyo, Japan kato.15x@g.chuo-u.ac.jp Introduction
Indonesia is a country of cultural as well as religious diversity. Even before the advent of Islam, Buddhism, and Hinduism were widespread in the region. Some think that Islam in Indonesia spread to the archipelago through interaction with local civilizations (Legge 1964: 3-7). Although Islam has become a majority religion in Indonesia today, long-standing local cultural traditions, ones that have existed throughout its recorded history, are often still maintained and practiced widely in Indonesia. These pre-Islamic traditions have a profound association with nature, and the people tend to appreciate animistic entities (Legge 1964, 24).
One of the examples of these adat-based1 traditions is sedekah bumi, which is practiced for a better harvest in agriculture and fishery. The basics of this festivity involve making offerings to the deity that rules nature. Apart from sedekah bumi, we also find adat-based practices in Indonesia. For example, the customary visit to the grave of family members, known as ziarah, is popular among Muslims even though the original teachings of Islam forbid ancestral worship. It is also true that mediators between the almighty Allah and the believers are acknowledged in Indonesia, especially wali, or saints who are popular objects for reverence.
These local cultural traditions have considerable divergence when compared to the core idea of Islam, which is tauhid, or the oneness of almighty Allah. In addition, some sexual minority groups, including transvestites and homosexuals, are supported by Muslim scholars, which obviously contradicts the orthodox interpretations of Islam. We also know that several terrorist attacks were perpetrated in Indonesia in its modern history. Although Islam acknowledges the right to self-defense and allows its followers to take up weapons, we never see teachings that justify the indiscriminate killing of fellow humans.
Observing these socio-cultural phenomena in Indonesia, we see that divergences may exist between orthodox teachings and the actual practices of Islam.
1 Adat originated in Arabic and means customary law. In Indonesia and Malaysia, it is widely applied
to the social relationship among humans or muamalat (economic transactions or dealings with others). Adat is usually understood as traditions and customs that have considerable resonance with indigenous socio-cultural practices.
This fact leads us to the question of whether the authenticity of Islam can be maintained over time and space as it has spread to other parts of the world from the Arabian Peninsula.
V.S. Naipaul was skeptical about the eternality of orthodoxy in religion. He states that “religious and cultural purity is a fundamentalist fantasy” (Naipaul 1998:59). We will explore the adequacy of Naipaul’s theory in this paper, taking three examples: sedekah bumi, sexual minorities, and the terrorist mentality in Islam.
## Authenticity and Sedekah Bumi
Sedekah bumi is a festival for harvesting, conducted on Java Island (Prasasti 2020:110). This local tradition is of socio-cultural significance for several reasons. Some indicate that sedekah bumi functions to consolidate social coherence in the community. Lestari et al., for example, state that “all elements in the society” participate in the event to “preserve” (melestarikan) their own culture (Lestari et al. 2018: 10). It is also suggested that sedekah bumi features specific cultural values, such as “ancestral customs” and “respecting natural surroundings” (Hastuti et al. 2020:13). As the participants of this ritual are peasants and fishermen, the appreciation of, and determination to protect, nature is emphasized in sedekah bumi (Hastuti et al. 2020: 13). In that sense, this local tradition has resonance with the contemporary world where environmental problems are perceived to be essential for the wellbeing of humans.
It has also been found that urbanization has contributed to changes in the practice of sedekah bumi. This agricultural ritual is practiced in the rural areas where a conventional means of agriculture is still employed, rather than in places where more modern methods are applied to the agricultural industry (Supriatna et al. 2020:194). This suggests that such a time-honored local tradition can be influenced by societal changes, including modernization.
Another pivotal manifestation from the literature on sedekah bumi is its relationship with Islam. Although the origin of sedekah bumi remains unaccounted for, previous research has found not infrequently that the influence of Islam is observed in this tradition (Umam 2020:130; Prasasti 2020:119, 121). The literal meaning of sedekah bumi is the “alms of the earth,” and the almighty, Allah, is understood as the source of the harvest in this traditional ritual today (Kiftiyah et al. 2020:198). The closeness to Allah is heavily emphasized in sedekah bumi (Arinda 2014: 108-109). We can assume, then, that this core Islamic idea penetrated the practice of what was originally a Javanese ritual after its arrival in the archipelago. We can conclude that Sedekah bumi, a Javanese agricultural festivity, to some extent has been imbued with Islamic colors.
However, Hidayatulloh points out that some deeds practiced in sedekah bumi contradict Islamic values (Hidayatulloh 2013:16).
For example, one of the traditional dances performed in sedekah bumi, called ombyok sapi, is at variance with Islam (Hidayatulloh 2013:8-9). This adat symbolizes fertility by using animals such as cows and water buffalo, which is not allowed in Islam (Hidayatulloh 2013:17).
We should remember that some conservative Muslims in Indonesia are reluctant to accept a syncretic practice with local adat (Kato 2021: 118-119). There is also a contemporary indication that Islamic conservatism is growing in Indonesia (Sebastian et al. 2021). Several researchers suggest that the cause of the rise of the Salafist2 movement in the umat (or Islamic community) can be found in the “socio-psychological perspective” that religious identity plays an important role in deciding one’s attitude in society (Muhtadi et al. 2021:33).
Sedekah bumi is a ritual for appreciating the harvest based on Javanese adat. Although we find several versions of this agricultural celebration in various areas in Java, it is true that sedekah bumi has already adopted Islamic values, such as the idea of tauhid, which means Javanese traditional values and Islamic tenets have been harmonized in practice. Nonetheless, Salafist Muslims believe that the original teachings of Islam and the practice of Sedekah bumi in theory contradict each other. We must admit that this is a clear example of the divergence that exists between the teachings of Islam and the practices of its followers in a socio-cultural context in Indonesia.
## Sexual Minorities in Indonesia
Recent medical research suggests that homosexuality is one of the natural states of humans (Nature: 18 Feb 2015). However, we still find Salafist Muslims who reject and deny sexual minorities based on their unchangeable idea of two genders, that is, male and female. That includes conservative Muslims who are determined to follow the authentic teachings of Islam. It is true that the holy al-Quran denounces what is seen as the wrongdoings of homosexuals, who in the end are punished due to their sexual orientation.
Will you persist in these indecent acts which no other nation has committed before you? You lust after men instead of women. Truly, you are a degenerate people.
(al-Quran 7:80~81)
Indeed, the degree of acceptance of homosexuality in Islamic-dominated countries is low compared to non-Islamic countries. Merely nine percent of the population of Indonesia accepts homosexuality (Pew Research Center).

Despite this unsympathetic attitude towards sexual minorities, we find a remarkable development in Indonesia. Several cross-dressing homosexuals established an Islamic boarding school, or pesantren, with the support of certain Muslim scholars in the Javanese town of Yogyakarta in 2010.
This school, named al-Fatah, has faced several crises since its establishment. The first challenge came to them when one of the founders of the pesantren, Mariah, passed away. As the venue of the school belonged to Mariah, students were forced to find an alternative location to operate the school. Shinta, a co-founder of al-Fatah, managed to move the school to property belonging to her family in Yogyakarta.
With the leadership of Shinta, the school managed to conduct activities that supported the life of sexual minorities and brought about awareness of egalitarianism, which is highly valued in Islam (Kato 2017). However, another serious impediment for al-Fatah took place when a group of radical Muslims demanded the closure of the pesantren in 2016. Although al-Fatah was temporarily closed at that time, the school was able to recommence its operation with the support of Muslim scholars and other community members (Kato 2017). Most recently, the pesantren was in a crisis when Shinta suddenly passed away in January 2023. The al-Fatah was again forced to relocate the school from the family property of Shinta to another appropriate site. With the support of Muslim scholars and NGOs, al-Fatah moved to a city center in Yogyakarta and has managed to continue its activities since the middle of 2023.
The role of Arif Safri, a Muslim scholar and expert of Koranic studies based in Yogyakarta, cannot be overlooked. Since its establishment in 2010, Safri has cast his support for the activities of al-Fatah as a mentor. He, in fact, has taught Islamic theology to the students of al-Fatah every Sunday. More significantly, Safri has published and made presentations on the validity of the sexuality of the students at alFatah.
He expounds the view that all humans, including sexual minorities, are the creation of the almighty, Allah, and thus they should be blessed (Safri 2020: 33-34). In addition, he also states that the people of Lot were punished due to their social misconduct rather than their homosexuality (Safri 2020: 33-34). His interpretation of Islamic teachings on sexual minorities clearly contrasts with that of the orthodoxy of Islam.
An Indonesian philosopher and writer, Soffa Ihsan, also argues that Islam acknowledges genders other than male and female (Ihsan:100-101). He urges that Muslims should revalue the orthodox interpretation of the teachings to appreciate the well-being of all humans (Ihsan:106-107). These unconventional attitudes of Muslims towards sexual minorities in Indonesia could be related to those civilizational values that still steadfastly remain in the country. The Javanese ancient civilization, for example, cherishes harmony among humans, nature, and deities, and devalues any conflict.
It is also common for males to play a woman’s role in traditional dancing in Indonesia (Fukuoka 2014). Gender deviation is by no means peculiar, at least in cultural perspectives in Indonesia.
Needless to say, sexual minorities have never been fully accepted in Indonesia, as some Salafist Muslims, who follow the orthodox teachings of Islam, still reject them vehemently. Nonetheless, sexual minorities are still able to practice Islam with support and understanding provided by fellow Muslims.
## Terrorism and Islam
We have witnessed terrorist attacks that have been executed in the name of Islam in recent recorded history. However, we can find no Islamic teachings that justify such actions. Islam, in fact, forbids the indiscriminate killing of fellow humans without appropriate reasons. One of the verses in al-Quran reads:
…whoever killed a human being, except as punishment for murder or other villainy in the land, shall be deemed as though he had killed all mankind; and that whoever saved a human life shall be deemed as though he had saved all mankind. (al-Quran 5:32)
The acts of terrorists contradict the authentic teachings of Islam. Former terrorists themselves have found theological rationales to justify their deeds; however, the substantiation they have provided has been, indeed, brought about by their own interpretations of the teachings. The self-claimed grounds of terrorist acts are induced by the socio-political conditions of society.
Some former terrorists explained that their motivation for militant actions was based on international relations, such as the Palestine/Israel conflict in the Middle East, while others claimed that they intended to subvert the secular government of the Republic of Indonesia.3
With these motivations, they have upheld their conviction that Muslims are allowed to attack their enemies, who bring suffering to Palestinians and never implement syariah as a base of national law.
Yet, again, the perpetrators of terrorism disregard the substance of Islam: they merely follow their own interpretations. In the process of de-radicalization, former terrorists have started to realize that their own interpretation, indeed, is a deviation from authentic Islam.4

## Understanding Religion as Conclusive Remarks
We have discussed three socio-religious phenomena observed in modern Indonesian society.
Obviously, sedekah bumi is an important event in the community, which brings about social coherence with enhanced binding amongst people, and this adat-based event contains elements that contradict the orthodoxy of Islam. The long-standing Javanese civilization has been the foundation of this cultural festival, which exerts greater influence than Islamic orthodoxy.
From orthodox theological perspectives, sexual minorities are regarded as evildoers. However, occasionally, Muslim thinkers have presented arguments that consent to the practices of these minorities in Indonesia. The evolutionary interpretation of the teachings has played a significant role in the endorsement of sexual minorities.
Muslim terrorists employ their at-will interpretation of Islamic teachings to justify their militant deeds. Socio-political conditions in the world have obviously affected the practice of these Muslims.
These three cases are vivid examples of the divergence of religious teachings and the practices of religious followers. We need to understand the holistic picture of religion as such. In the eyes of its followers, religion is a sacred set of ideas generally bestowed by a superhuman existence, God. These ideas are usually described in their own scriptures, which can be the base of orthodoxy. In Islam, for example, Salafists are the major guardians of this rigid understanding of its tenets.
Simultaneously, we need to understand that the practices of religious followers resonate with local traditional values, individual interpretations of the teachings, and societal conditions where the religion exists. Naipaul’s analysis of religion, that “religious and cultural purity is a fundamentalist fantasy,” seems to be valid as a comprehensive understanding of religion. Divergences between teachings and practices surely exist, and it is a natural phenomenon that religion is an organic entity in society.
It is more correct to understand that religion consists of two major elements, that is, an ideological tenet and a social tenet. The former is appreciated by fundamentalists or Salafists in Islam, who are religiously more rigid, while the latter is taken by most of its followers, who are more accommodative to social conditions. However, we need to remember that these two different elements never exist separately, as even “accommodative Muslims” could take certain Salafist-type attitudes.
In addition, religion potentially fulfills a social role other than the mere theological meaning upheld by its followers.
In the case of Islam in Indonesia, it helps and protects minorities, preserves the old civilization, and diminishes militarism. We might call this the function of religion, as Radcliff-Brown states religion has the potential to contribute to the “formation and maintenance of social order,” and “religions perform a social function” (RadcliffeBrown 1965:154,177). This social function of religion is obviously affected by the condition of a given society, including its history, the depth of the local pre-existing civilization, and its relationship with other international communities, as we have discussed. As this function alters depending on the situation, the behaviors of the followers also vary, which could be seen as contrary to a religion’s authentic teachings.
In this regard, hypothetically speaking, sedekah bumi contributes to the preservation of social capital that ensures mutual solicitude in society. Yet it is also plausible that this traditional ritual metamorphizes, as modernization progresses in Indonesia.
Another important query in this research is whether so-called religious purity can be maintained beyond time and space. For example, we should ask whether Islam, which was brought to human society about one thousand and four hundred years ago in the Middle East, can be practiced in its original form in Indonesia today. V.S. Naipaul is correct, as both unchangeable ideological and changeable social elements exist in Islam, which nonetheless can be seen as an organic entity.
