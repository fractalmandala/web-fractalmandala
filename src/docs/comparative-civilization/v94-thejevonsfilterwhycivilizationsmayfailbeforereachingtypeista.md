Giray Fidan Ankara HBV University, Jagiellonian University, Beijing Language and Culture University giray.fidan@hbv.edu.tr Abstract
Humanity’s accelerating energy use poses profound questions about the long-term viability of technological civilization. This paper introduces the “Jevons Filter” hypothesis: a self-reinforcing feedback loop between efficiency improvements and rising aggregate consumption, rooted in the classical Jevons Paradox that systematically drives civilizations toward resource depletion and environmental thresholds.
Here we argue that this feedback constitutes a critical developmental barrier, hindering sustainable progression toward Kardashev “Type I” energy mastery. We develop a simplified thermodynamic model linking energy efficiency, economic expansion, and civilizational dynamics, and engage with counterarguments regarding absolute decoupling and transformative technological innovations.
Building on the classical Jevons Paradox, this paper conceptualizes a self-reinforcing Jevons feedback loop. At a civilizational scale, this feedback functions as a Jevons Filter — a systemic barrier that may prevent societies from reaching stable “Type I” energy status. Thus, our analysis suggests that without fundamental structural transformations to break the growth-consumption cycle, civilizations are likely to face collapse or stagnation before reaching stable, planetary-scale energy management
Keywords: Jevons Paradox, Jevons Filter, rebound effects, energy efficiency, Kardashev “Type I” civilization, Fermi Paradox
## 1. Introduction
The swift advancement in human technology prompts us to consider what the future holds for our species. We wonder about the potential for intelligent life beyond Earth and what kinds of futures might be possible elsewhere in the universe. Is it possible for human civilization to develop a sustainable way to use energy on a planetary scale while keeping ecological systems balanced?
Essentially, can we reach a state similar to what is considered a Kardashev “Type I” civilization — where energy consumption is integrated harmoniously with environmental stability? Does the fast pace of technological progress, especially regarding energy consumption, pose serious dangers?
Could it be that such growth pushes societies past environmental boundaries, causing collapse long before they achieve their most advanced developments?
Answers to these questions are closely tied to what is known as the Fermi Paradox, the notable tension between the strong likelihood that many advanced extraterrestrial civilizations should exist and the fact that we’ve yet to find any concrete evidence of them. This absence of signs is often called the “Great Silence” (Hart, 1975; Brin, 1983).
Further, the paradox points to the possibility that our assumptions about how often life arises or how civilizations evolve might be fundamentally mistaken, or that there are important obstacles that prevent intelligent societies from progressing to a point where they are detectable. Understanding this puzzle matters not just for how we see humanity’s role in the universe, but also for shaping the future of our technological society. It is a question that touches on both our origins and what lies ahead.
To explore these challenges, we introduce the concept of the “Jevons Filter.” Here we argue that a strong, self-reinforcing feedback loop, rooted in the well-known economic pattern described by the Jevons Paradox, may form a major developmental barrier, one that many civilizations confront as they attempt to manage planetary energy flows.
The Jevons Paradox, first described by William Stanley Jevons (1866) in his study of coal consumption in 19th-century Britain, captures the surprising observation that improvements in the efficiency of resource use tend to raise, rather than reduce, total consumption. Jevons showed, for example, that James Watt’s improved steam engine, by making coal-based power cheaper and more practical, triggered a massive expansion of industrial activity and led to a sharp increase in total coal demand, even as individual machines became more efficient.
That is, lower energy costs made possible the growth of new industries and applications, fueling wider economic expansion and increasing overall resource use.
Today, this effect is understood through the idea of rebound effects. Energy efficiency is typically seen as reducing the energy needed to deliver a particular service, for instance, achieving more lumens of light per watt of electricity, or traveling more kilometers per liter of fuel. Rebound effects occur when the potential savings from efficiency improvements are reduced, partially or completely, by shifts in behavior, economics, or broader patterns that follow the efficiency gain itself (Greening et al., 2000; Sorrell, 2009).
These rebound effects appear in several forms: direct rebounds occur when lower costs encourage greater use of the efficient service (e.g., driving farther with a fuel-efficient car).
Indirect rebounds arise when financial savings are spent on other goods and services that themselves require energy; and economy-wide rebounds result from lower production costs and rising productivity stimulating broader growth across many sectors.
When these rebounds cancel out the original savings, the overall effect is neutral; when they exceed 100%, leading to backfire, total energy use actually increases.
The Jevons Filter concept suggests that this tendency for efficiency improvements to drive higher consumption creates a strong positive feedback loop. We argue that such feedback can push technologically developing civilizations toward faster resource depletion and growing environmental pressure, driven precisely by their growing capabilities. In this view, the same advancements that make a civilization more powerful could also shorten its longevity.
A “Type I” civilization, following the Kardashev scale (1964), refers to a society able to capture the full amount of stellar energy reaching its planet. For an Earth-like world orbiting a Sun-like star, this level is estimated at between 10¹⁶ and 10¹⁷ watts. Achieving this threshold would require not only immense technological ability but also careful management of planetary energy flows.
Humanity’s current primary energy consumption, around 18 terawatts (TW), places it at roughly 0.73 on the logarithmic extension of the Kardashev scale, showing that we remain well short of “Type I” status (Zhang et al., 2023).
Our central argument is that the road toward “Type I” conditions may be self-limiting because of the processes described by the Jevons Paradox. Efficiency-driven technological progress, essential for managing greater energy flows, could at the same time accelerate overall resource use and environmental impact to unsustainable levels, leading to crises such as resource conflicts, climate instability, or ecological collapse, and preventing the establishment of stable planetary-scale energy control.
We propose that the Jevons Filter could represent a specific example of the broader Great Filter concept, first outlined by Robin Hanson (1998) and expanded by Bostrom (2008) to explain the Fermi Paradox.
The Great Filter concept suggests that at least one step along the progression from simple chemistry to advanced civilizations is exceptionally unlikely. If the main obstacle lies in the future rather than the past, it will imply that while life and intelligence might appear relatively often, the transition to lasting, expanding civilizations remains extraordinarily rare.
Many possible filters have been proposed—including the origin of life, the development of complex multicellular organisms, the dangers of nuclear war, artificial intelligence, or cosmic catastrophes. The Jevons Filter focuses attention on a different kind of problem: the possibility that civilizations collapse due to the internal pressures created by their own growth and resource consumption on a finite world. In this perspective, the greatest danger may not come from sudden disasters, but from the slow buildup of unsustainable pressures.
Exploring the Jevons Filter idea calls for an interdisciplinary approach.
 Economic history provides evidence of past efficiency gains, rebound effects, and the strong connection between energy use and economic growth over the last two centuries.
 Thermodynamics offers the underlying physical laws that govern energy transformations, resource constraints, and the operation of complex systems.
 Astrobiology helps frame the broader picture of how civilizations might rise, endure, or fail — and what their patterns might reveal about the search for intelligent life beyond Earth.
In this study, we bring together these different perspectives and apply a simplified thermodynamic growth model (drawing on Garrett, 2011) to sketch how rebounddriven feedbacks might affect the long-term survival prospects of technologically advanced societies, while acknowledging the challenges of extending Earth-based lessons to the universe at large.
The subsequent sections of this paper develop the Jevons Filter hypothesis in greater detail. Section 2 offers an expanded discussion of the Jevons Paradox and the complex nature of rebound effects, clarifying definitions, exploring the underlying economic mechanisms, and presenting a broader array of empirical examples drawn from diverse technological and economic contexts.
- Section 3 introduces and analyzes the simplified thermodynamic growth model designed to formally capture the Jevons feedback loop, explicitly examines the model's assumptions and limitations, and engages critically with major counterarguments related to economic decoupling and the potential for purely technological solutions to overcome resource constraints.
- Section 4 then elaborates on the Jevons Filter mechanism itself, exploring the likely severe environmental and structural stresses associated with approaching “Type I” energy scales, explicitly contrasting the requirements for achieving sustainable “Type I” status with the observed patterns of efficiency-driven expansion.
And undertaking a more careful evaluation of the potential outcomes — collapse, stagnation, or transformation — for civilizations confronting this developmental bottleneck.
Finally, Section 5 offers concluding reflections on the hypothesis's broader importance
- as a potential contributing factor to the Fermi Paradox and its implications for understanding the characteristic trajectories and longevity of technological civilizations in the universe. While necessarily incorporating speculative elements when extending the argument beyond the human case, the analysis remains anchored throughout in observable historical phenomena, established economic principles, and fundamental physical constraints.
If the Jevons Filter operates as proposed, it suggests that navigating the path towards advanced, sustainable technological existence involves formidable, perhaps frequently fatal, challenges embedded within the growth process itself.
This paper argues that the reinforcing feedback loop between technological improvements in energy/resource efficiency and subsequent increases in aggregate consumption, driven by Jevons Paradox rebound effects, systematically propels developing civilizations towards resource depletion and environmental limits. It argues why this pattern constitutes a plausible “Jevons Filter” — a critical developmental barrier — by potentially triggering societal collapse or stagnation before the stability and planetary stewardship required for sustainable “Type I” status can be achieved.
This proposed mechanism, rooted in observable economic and thermodynamic principles, offers a contributing explanation for the Fermi Paradox, suggesting that the apparent scarcity of advanced technological life may stem from built-in self-limiting tendencies of growth rather than solely from external hazards or the rarity of intelligence itself.
## 2. The Paradox of Efficiency: Historical Evidence and Rebound Effects
A thorough grasp of the proposed Jevons Filter necessitates a detailed examination of the Jevons Paradox and the complex mechanisms driving rebound effects.
As introduced, the paradox stems from William Stanley Jevons' (1866) careful analysis of the British coal economy, where he observed that the deployment of more efficient steam engines, contrary to simplistic expectations, led to a substantial increase in total coal consumption. The underlying economic principle is straightforward: improved energy efficiency — achieving more service or output per unit of energy input effectively reduces the marginal cost of the energy service being provided.
Standard microeconomic theory predicts that a reduction in the price (or cost) of a good or service generally leads to an increase in its consumption, all else being equal. Rebound effects quantify the degree to which this behavioral and structural response counteracts the initial energy savings potential built into the efficiency improvement. It is essential to recognize that rebound effects are often economically rational responses from the perspective of individual consumers (seeking greater utility or "comfort taking" by using more of a cheaper service) or firms (maximizing output or profits by making greater use of cheaper inputs).
The paradox arises when these individually rational responses aggregate to produce a socially or ecologically suboptimal outcome at the macroeconomic level, particularly undermining efforts aimed at resource conservation or emission reduction through efficiency policies alone.
The extensive literature on this topic categorizes rebound effects into several interacting types, the sum of which determines the net change in energy consumption resulting from an efficiency improvement (Greening et al., 2000; Sorrell & Dimitropoulos, 2007; Sorrell, 2009).
The Direct Rebound Effect pertains specifically to the increased consumption of the energy service whose efficiency was improved. This is driven by two main microeconomic mechanisms: the substitution effect, where consumers substitute the now-cheaper energy service for other goods, services, or time (e.g., choosing to drive rather than take public transport because driving is relatively cheaper), and the income effect, where the money saved due to lower energy costs acts like an increase in real income, allowing the consumer to purchase more of various goods and services, including potentially more of the energy service itself (e.g., affording to heat the house to a higher temperature or travel more often).
Empirical estimates for direct rebounds vary widely depending on the specific service, market saturation, consumer demographics, and geographic context, but frequently cited ranges for applications like personal transportation or residential heating in developed countries often fall between 10% and 30%, implying that a noticeable portion, but typically not all, of the engineering savings are eroded by increased use. However, for services with high unmet demand (e.g., refrigeration in developing countries initially) or high price elasticity, direct rebounds can be considerably larger.
Distinct from the direct effect is the Indirect Rebound Effect. This effect accounts for the energy and resource implications of how the monetary savings realized from energy efficiency are re-spent throughout the economy.
If a household reduces its monthly energy bill due to efficient appliances, the income saved is typically allocated towards purchasing other goods and services — perhaps new clothing, electronics, different food items (which might have higher embedded energy due to production or transport), entertainment, or travel. Each of these alternative consumption choices has its own associated lifecycle energy and material footprint, stemming from raw material extraction, manufacturing, transportation, use, and disposal.
Assessing the magnitude of indirect rebounds requires complex economic modeling techniques, such as input-output analysis tracking inter-sectoral purchases or computable general equilibrium (CGE) models simulating economy-wide responses to price changes. These models attempt to trace the flow of expenditures and associated energy intensities through various economic sectors. Studies employing these methods suggest that indirect effects can be substantial, potentially equaling or even exceeding direct rebound effects in some cases, depending on consumption patterns and the relative energy intensity of the goods and services purchased with the savings compared to the energy service where efficiency occurred.
Perhaps the most important, yet most difficult to measure precisely, are the EconomyWide Rebound Effects. These encompass broader macroeconomic adjustments and long-term structural shifts triggered by widespread energy efficiency improvements.
Acting as a form of technological advancement, efficiency gains can boost overall economic productivity, particularly when energy represents a major input cost. This can lead to lower production expenses across multiple sectors, often resulting in reduced
- consumer prices for a wide range of goods and services, which then stimulates overall demand. Increased productivity can also enhance national competitiveness, attract capital investment, free up labor for other activities, and in turn speed up the overall rate of economic growth (GDP expansion). A larger, faster-growing economy generally
- consumes more energy and resources in total, even if it does so more efficiently on a per-unit basis. In addition, major technological shifts linked to efficiency can drive structural changes in the economy, sometimes giving rise to entirely new industries or production models that might be more energy-intensive overall, even if individual processes are more efficient.
For instance, the development of efficient internal combustion engines and refining technologies did not merely make existing transportation cheaper; it opened the door to the automotive industry, suburban expansion, mass air travel, and global logistics networks deeply reliant on transportation fuels.
These long-term growth and restructuring effects are seen by many analysts as the primary drivers behind the possibility of total rebound effects approaching or surpassing 100% (backfire), thereby wiping out or even reversing the initial energy savings at the macroeconomic level. Estimating these effects requires advanced macroeconomic modeling and remains subject to considerable uncertainty, but major reviews suggest they are likely substantial and potentially large (e.g., Sorrell, 2009, reviewing available evidence and models, points to plausible economy-wide rebounds ranging from 30 – 60%, or even higher in certain contexts, though definitive proof of consistent backfire remains elusive).
The potential for large economy-wide rebounds, and the risk of backfire, forms the central idea behind the Khazzoom-Brookes Postulate (Khazzoom, 1980; Brookes, 1990). This postulate suggests that efficiency improvements which are economically advantageous (i.e., they reduce costs) tend to stimulate enough economic growth to drive up, rather than lower, total energy consumption [See, e.g., Saunders, 1992, 2000; Sorrell, 2009 for theoretical and empirical reviews.] Theoretical backing for this possibility was provided by neoclassical growth models under specific assumptions about energy’s role in production and its limited substitutability with capital (Saunders, 1992).
While careful empirical validation of consistent backfire across entire economies remains complex and debated — due to the challenge of separating the effects of efficiency from other growth drivers — the consensus across the energy economics literature is strong: rebound effects are a real and influential phenomenon, meaning the actual energy savings achieved through efficiency programs are usually much smaller than their technical potential would suggest. This empirical pattern has far-reaching implications for energy policy aimed at curbing demand and addressing climate change, and it forms the essential foundation for the Jevons Filter hypothesis.
The persistent link between energy consumption and economic output, despite continuous efficiency improvements, is also borne out by historical and international data. Kaufmann (1992), for instance, analyzed energy/GDP ratios and found that observed declines in energy intensity across several industrialized nations were often noticeably offset by simultaneous changes in economic structure (e.g., shifts between industrial and service sectors) and by broader scale effects, indicating a more durable connection between energy use and economic activity than simple metrics suggest.
From a thermodynamic standpoint, Ayres and Warr (2009) argued convincingly that much of 20th-century economic growth — typically credited to general technological progress in models like Solow’s (1957) — can be more accurately attributed to tangible improvements in converting primary energy into usable physical work (exergy), the true driver of physical production.
Their analysis strongly implies that efficiency gains have historically acted mainly as enablers of expanded economic output and scale, rather than bringing about a reduction in energy throughput for a given standard of living.
This pattern is captured in the reinforcing cycle: innovation improves efficiency, which lowers costs and fuels economic expansion, leading to greater overall resource use, which in turn often encourages further innovation aimed partly at controlling resource expenses through greater efficiency. While relative decoupling (decreasing energy use per unit of GDP) is happening globally, total energy consumption continues to climb because overall economic growth outpaces efficiency gains (BP, 2018).
Studies examining the possibility of sustained, global absolute decoupling find little historical evidence for its occurrence (York & McGee, 2016), suggesting that the expansion of the global economy still reliably drives increases in total energy and resource use, intensifying pressures on planetary boundaries (IPCC, 2022).
Concrete examples illustrate how this cycle plays out across different technological arenas:
 Steam Engines and Coal: Jevons' (1866) original observation remains highly relevant. Watt’s improvements to steam engines greatly reduced the cost of mechanical power. However, rather than simply making traditional tasks (like pumping water from mines) more efficient, these changes opened the door to the mechanization of factories, the spread of railways, and the growth of steampowered shipping. These transformations reshaped the economy, created vast new coal demands, and fueled the explosive growth of the Industrial Revolution — resulting in a dramatic increase in coal consumption. This case exemplifies how efficiency can drive broad economic expansion and rebound effects at the national level.
 Automotive Fuel Economy: The introduction of Corporate Average Fuel Economy (CAFE) standards in the United States from 1975 aimed to curb gasoline consumption in response to energy crises. While these standards drove measurable improvements in the fuel efficiency of new vehicles, the overall reduction in fuel use was partially undone. Vehicle miles traveled increased substantially, fueled by several forces: lower costs per mile encouraged longer trips; preferences shifted toward heavier, less efficient vehicles like SUVs and light trucks; and cheaper personal transport supported the spread of suburban development and longer commutes. As a result, after an initial period of stabilization, US gasoline consumption eventually rose again (Greene, 1992).
Key Point: This example shows how policies targeting technical improvements can fall short when broader consumption behaviors and infrastructure trends are not addressed. It suggests that technological mandates alone may not curb energy use unless paired with broader measures like fuel pricing reforms or urban planning initiatives. Within the Jevons Filter framework, it shows how efficiency advances often feed into the expansion of the very systems they are intended to moderate.
 Lighting Efficiency: Technological advances from incandescent bulbs to CFLs and LEDs have produced order-of-magnitude improvements in lumens per watt. However, global energy use for lighting has not decreased proportionally — and may have risen in absolute terms. The steep drop in the cost of lighting has fueled widespread growth: greater illumination in homes, workplaces, and public spaces; the proliferation of outdoor lighting; expanded use in displays, advertising, and entertainment; and countless new applications.
This expansion, sometimes called “lighting prevalence rebound” (Tsao et al., 2010), even raises the possibility of backfire in the sector.
Key Point: Lighting demonstrates how breakthrough efficiency gains can trigger huge expansions in demand, fully absorbing or even reversing the energy savings initially expected.
 Information and Communication Technology (ICT): The energy efficiency of computing — measured by computations per kilowatt-hour — has followed an extraordinary improvement curve for decades, outpacing even Moore’s Law (Koomey's Law). Yet despite more efficient hardware, the total energy demand of the global ICT ecosystem — including manufacturing, operation of data centers, networks, and user devices — continues to grow.
Efficiency improvements were a prerequisite for the digital revolution, enabling personal computing, the internet, mobile devices, cloud computing, large-scale data analytics, and artificial intelligence. While each device generation becomes more efficient, the soaring number of devices, volume of data managed, and sophistication of tasks continue to push total energy demand higher. Even though data centers have become much more efficient (e.g., with better Power Usage Effectiveness), their energy consumption still rises because of relentless growth in services like cloud computing and streaming media.
Key Point: The ICT sector highlights the Jevons Paradox operating powerfully in a field driven by fast-paced innovation. Here, efficiency advances have not shrunk the sector’s energy footprint; rather, they have fueled an enormous expansion in digital activities and energy use across the global economy.
These varied examples, spanning centuries and technologies, together strengthen the central argument: while efficiency improvements are often beneficial for particular applications and can drive economic growth, they frequently set off compensatory increases in consumption through a complex interaction of behavioral patterns, market forces, and broader structural shifts. The historical record indicates that rebound effects are widespread and often considerable.
This body of evidence offers strong grounds for exploring the Jevons Filter hypothesis: the idea that this built-in tendency presents a fundamental obstacle to the long-term sustainability of any technologically advancing civilization striving for elevated levels of energy use and may help explain why such civilizations appear to be rare in the cosmos.
## 3. Thermodynamic Growth Constraints and the Jevons Feedback
To analyze the relationship between efficiency, energy consumption, and civilizational growth more formally, we adopt a simplified systems model drawing on insights from thermodynamics and empirical economic data. A key observation supporting this approach comes from studies suggesting a strong link between a society’s energy use and its accumulated economic infrastructure.
Garrett (2011), treating the global economy as a thermodynamic system maintained far from equilibrium through continuous energy input, found a remarkably stable relationship between global primary energy consumption (E) and inflation-adjusted, historically accumulated economic output or wealth (W). This relationship can be expressed as E(t)≈λW(t), where λ is a constant of proportionality, empirically estimated
- at around 9.7 milliwatts per 1990 US dollar.
This constant reflects the approximate power required to sustain each unit of accumulated economic value (infrastructure, capital, organizational complexity). The importance of this finding lies in its consistency across several decades despite major improvements in energy efficiency. This empirical result points to a deep-rooted connection between the energy necessary to maintain the structure of civilization and the total historical economic activity that produced it.
We use this empirical relationship as a starting point for building a conceptual model, defining key variables (see Appendix A for detailed definitions). W(t) represents cumulative wealth, serving as a proxy for the size and complexity of the maintained structure. E(t) represents the rate of primary energy consumption needed to sustain W. η(t) represents average energy productivity, defined as the rate of new wealth generation (GDP, denoted Y=dW/dt) per unit of energy consumed (Y/E), reflecting the technological capability to translate energy into economic value. λ remains the empirically observed coupling constant (E/W).
Using these definitions, we can set out the central feedback mechanism. By definition, the rate of wealth generation Y=dW/dt equals ηE. Since E≈λW (per Equation 1), substitution yields: dW/dt=η(t)λW(t) (Equation 2).
Analyzing the Feedback Mechanism:
-  Equation (2) shows that the rate of wealth growth depends on three factors: the current stock of accumulated wealth (W), the level of energy productivity (η), and the energy-wealth coupling constant (λ). The positive feedback operates sequentially.
-  Step 1: Increases in η allow more economic value (new wealth, dW/dt) to be produced from a given flow of energy (E) or for a given stock of wealth (W), boosting growth rates.
-  Step 2: This enhanced wealth creation accelerates the accumulation of W over time.
-  Step 3: However, the stable λ observed by Garrett implies that a larger W demands proportionally greater energy flows simply to sustain itself (E=λW). This reflects the continuous energy input needed to counteract decay and maintain the complex structures accumulated over time.
-  Step 4: Thus, initial efficiency gains, by accelerating wealth accumulation, indirectly but strongly drive higher total energy consumption through economic expansion. If η itself improves over time, as has historically occurred, the overall system can experience faster-than-exponential growth in both wealth and energy consumption.
The Jevons feedback amplifies this process by creating a self-reinforcing loop: efficiency makes energy services cheaper, leading to increased consumption of those services (direct rebound), freeing up income for consumption of other energy-intensive goods and services (indirect rebound), and boosting overall economic productivity and growth, which scales up total energy demand across the economy (economy-wide rebound).
This amplification mechanism means that efficiency improvements, intended to reduce energy use, instead become a primary driver of its increase at the aggregate level.
The implications are significant: it suggests that technological progress, while making us more capable, simultaneously locks us into a cycle of ever-increasing energy and resource throughput, intensifying pressure on planetary limits.
This feedback structure captures the Jevons Paradox at the civilizational scale, portraying efficiency not merely as a conservation tool but as a powerful force for expansion within an economy tied to energy throughput. The model's broad alignment with observed long-term trends of exponential increases in both GDP and energy use lends support to this interpretation (Garrett, 2012b).
Methodological Considerations and Limitations: The strength of this model lies in its clarity and its empirical grounding in the observed near-constancy of λ. It offers a straightforward conceptual map of the proposed feedback. Nonetheless, acknowledging its limitations is essential for methodological transparency and rigor.
First, the model treats global civilization as a single thermodynamic entity, ignoring important variations across nations, sectors, income groups, and technologies, all of which can affect energy use and rebound patterns. A more complex model would ideally account for these heterogeneities.
Second, the reliance on Garrett’s formulation and the empirical stability of λ is a core assumption. Although Garrett presents compelling evidence for this relationship during the fossil-fuel era, it is uncertain whether λ would remain stable during future transformations — such as a complete transition to renewable energy, significant shifts in the material composition of infrastructure, or transitions toward radically different socio-economic structures like a degrowth economy.
The value of λ could potentially change if the energy intensity of maintaining accumulated wealth fundamentally alters. For instance, a civilization heavily reliant on physically dispersed infrastructure in harsh environments might have a higher λ than one with highly centralized, durable structures.
The model does not explicitly account for factors that could cause λ to vary over time or between diverse types of civilizations.
Third, the model is deterministic and omits agency; it does not capture the potential for deliberate policy intervention, cultural change, or behavioral shifts that could alter growth-energy dynamics. It describes a historical tendency but not an immutable law.
Fourth, applying Earth-based patterns to hypothetical extraterrestrial civilizations is inherently speculative. Different biologies, planetary conditions, resource endowments, and technological pathways could lead to vastly different outcomes, potentially altering the fundamental relationships captured by the model or the value and stability of λ.
Thus, while the model provides a useful conceptual tool for exploring historical trends and future possibilities, it should not be viewed as a precise universal predictor.
Being explicit about these assumptions improves the transparency and robustness of the Jevons Filter hypothesis. Future research using alternative models (such as agent-based simulations or frameworks that incorporate explicit policy choices and varying λ) could offer valuable complementary perspectives and assess the sensitivity of the hypothesis to these limitations.
Engaging with Counterarguments: Given these limitations, it is essential to engage critically with potential objections that challenge the persistence or generality of the Jevons feedback driving civilizations toward crisis.
One notable counterargument concerns the possibility of Absolute Decoupling. Could advanced economies eventually sever the link between economic growth (rising GDP) and energy or resource use (stable or declining E)? Advocates often cite the expansion of service economies, digitalization, and trends toward dematerialization as potential avenues. Yet, as previously discussed, empirical support for sustained, global-scale absolute decoupling remains limited and contested. What appears as decoupling in developed economies frequently coincides with the outsourcing of manufacturing and resource extraction (Peters et al., 2011), displacing rather than eliminating environmental pressures. Moreover, the material and energy demands of the digital economy and service sectors, though different in character, continue to expand.
Thus, while absolute decoupling remains theoretically possible and an appealing target for sustainability, presuming it will arise organically, or fully counteract rebound effects, without targeted strategies to curb overall consumption it appears overly optimistic.
A second category of counterarguments centers on Breakthrough Technologies. Could future innovations disrupt the historical Jevons cycle? Could abundant, clean energy from nuclear fusion remove energy scarcity as a constraint? Could advanced AI drive optimizations that neutralize rebound effects? Could a highly efficient circular economy reduce resource extraction to negligible levels?
Although such possibilities are often invoked in futurist discourse, they require careful examination. Historical evidence on energy transitions (Smil, 2019) shows that new energy abundance has typically fueled expansions in economic scale and complexity, shifting, rather than eliminating ecological constraints (e.g., from energy to material inputs, land, or pollution absorption). Even if fusion power were realized and made economically accessible, conventional economic principles suggest it could trigger dramatic rebounds through enabling large-scale material processing, global desalination, or other energy-intensive sectors — unless consumption itself is actively governed. AI-driven optimization would similarly depend on the goals embedded within systems; if directed toward maximizing output and productivity without structural limits, it could easily accelerate resource use.
Approaching 100% circularity faces substantial thermodynamic barriers: perfect recycling remains impossible due to entropy, and even remarkably high recycling rates require continuous energy and material inputs. Consequently, technological breakthroughs will undoubtedly be necessary for building a sustainable future, assuming that innovation alone can resolve the growth-consumption feedback, without deeper structural changes to economic models, consumption patterns, or societal objectives, risks underestimating the embedded momentum of the Jevons mechanism.
Recognizing these counterarguments and the model’s boundaries strengthens the overall argument.
The Jevons Filter hypothesis does not claim a deterministic or inevitable outcome but rather identifies a deeply entrenched historical tendency. It suggests that transcending this pattern would require more than incremental efficiency gains or technological optimism; it likely demands intentional structural reforms focused on moderating consumption, rethinking conventional growth paradigms, and realigning technological progress toward sustaining human and ecological well-being within planetary boundaries. The ongoing resilience of this feedback mechanism provides a compelling reason to consider it a candidate for a major civilizational filter.
## 4. The Jevons Filter: A Pre-“Type I” Civilizational Barrier
Integrating the historical evidence of rebound effects with the thermodynamic growth model allows us to articulate the Pre-“Type I” Jevons Filter hypothesis more fully.
This hypothesis posits that the deeply embedded positive feedback loop between energy efficiency and aggregate consumption acts as a critical developmental constraint, or “filter,” that many technologically advancing civilizations may encounter before reaching the energy scale and stability associated with Kardashev “Type I” status. The filter arises because the same force that propels early civilizational expansion improving efficiency to stimulate economic growth — also accelerates the approach to planetary limits, potentially triggering overshoot, crisis, and subsequent collapse or stagnation unless a fundamental transformation occurs.
The operation of this filter involves several interconnected elements. The first element concerns the accelerated arrival at planetary boundaries. As demonstrated by the growth model (dW/dt=ληW), efficiency gains (η) within a coupled system tend to drive exponential or faster growth in energy use (E) and material throughput. On a finite planet, this trajectory inevitably approaches limits related to finite resources (e.g., mineral depletion, freshwater scarcity, soil degradation) and the capacity of environmental sinks (e.g., atmospheric greenhouse gas absorption, ecological pollutant tolerance) (Rockström et al., 2009).
The Jevons feedback amplifies this process. By making resource extraction, processing, and utilization more economical, efficiency improvements accelerate the depletion of resource stocks and the accumulation of environmental damage. This trend appears generally across domains such as industrial production, agriculture, and transportation. Efficiency allows activities to scale up, pressing against multiple boundaries at once, often generating interlinked crises rather than confronting resource limits sequentially.
For example, improvements in nitrogen fertilizer production boosted food output and enabled substantial population growth but also severely disrupted the global nitrogen cycle, contributing to eutrophication, soil acidification, biodiversity loss, and rising nitrous oxide emissions — a potent greenhouse gas — largely unforeseen consequences of scaling up agriculture through efficiency.
The second element involves the likely severe environmental pressures associated with nearing “Type I” energy scales. Reaching the “Type I” threshold (~10¹⁶–10¹⁷ W) implies manipulating energy flows equivalent to the total solar input received by a planet. Although direct empirical data for such impacts are unavailable, extrapolation from current environmental science and insights from thermodynamic principles offer strong indications that the stresses would be extreme. A major concern is waste heat. The Second Law of Thermodynamics dictates that all energy conversions generate waste heat. Dissipating energy flows on the order of 10¹⁷ W — even with highly efficient systems — would introduce enough low-grade heat to substantially alter planetary energy balances, raising global mean temperatures and disrupting atmospheric and oceanic circulations independent of greenhouse gas emissions.
Initial estimates indicate that anthropogenic heat releases exceeding even a small fraction (e.g., 0.1–1%) of the solar flux could drive considerable climate destabilization, suggesting that approaching 10¹⁷ W would present thermodynamic threats to planetary habitability.
Resource extraction and land use present further challenges. Collecting such vast energy flows using contemporary technologies like solar photovoltaics or wind power would almost certainly require appropriating extensive areas of land or ocean, intensifying competition with ecosystems, biodiversity, and agriculture. The material demands for building and maintaining such infrastructure — requiring unprecedented volumes of steel, concrete, copper, silicon, rare earth elements, and other resources would entail extraction and processing activities on far greater scales than today, with environmental impacts of matching magnitude across the mining, transport, and disposal chains.
An additional concern is large-scale environmental manipulation. Harvesting planetary energy at such intensity amounts to de facto geoengineering.
This level of intervention could disrupt major biogeochemical cycles (e.g., carbon, nitrogen, phosphorus), alter ocean chemistry, and destabilize ecosystems in unpredictable, potentially irreversible ways. Humanity’s current impacts at ∼ 18 TW already demonstrate substantial global effects (IPCC, 2022); scaling energy use nearly a million-fold suggests qualitatively new levels of planetary stress likely incompatible with sustaining complex life.
Together, these considerations imply that civilizations would face escalating biophysical constraints and environmental risks well before achieving sustainable management of “Type I” energy levels.
A third critical element is the fundamental tension between efficiency-driven expansion and the requirements for sustaining a “Type I” civilization. The Jevons mechanism, as historically observed and thermodynamically modeled, promotes accelerated growth, increasing material and energy throughput, and reinvestment of productivity gains into greater consumption — thereby reinforcing expansion.
In stark contrast, a stable “Type I” society would almost certainly require a transition toward equilibrium with planetary limits. This implies curbing material growth, stabilizing total resource use and waste output, and adopting frameworks that place ecological balance over expansion. Such a transition would demand breaking the positive feedback loop wherein efficiency automatically fuels further scale-up. It would require a shift from efficiency-for-growth toward efficiency-for-sufficiency, perhaps grounded in models like the steady-state economy (Daly, 1991) or deeply circular economic systems aiming to minimize virgin resource extraction.
The Jevons Filter arises precisely at the developmental stage where the historically successful engine of expansion collides with the emerging necessity for restraint and stabilization. Pursuing ever greater efficiency and innovation enhances the potential for short-term growth, while achieving long-term planetary stewardship likely demands transcending the growth drive itself, a deep and consequential developmental contradiction.
Fourth, we must evaluate the potential outcomes when encountering the filter phase, acknowledging the complexity and contingency involved. As negative feedbacks from approaching limits intensify — manifesting as resource scarcity, escalating pollution costs, climate-related disasters, social instability, and pandemics worsened by environmental stress — the civilization faces the Jevons Filter crisis. The path forward is not strictly predetermined but likely branches toward one of three broad outcomes, shaped by the civilization's internal attributes (governance, culture, inequality, adaptive capacity) and its ability to recognize and respond to the challenge.
One possible outcome is Collapse: the civilization fails to adapt, breaches critical planetary boundaries, and experiences a swift, potentially irreversible decline in population, social complexity, technological capacity, and energy use, possibly triggered by resource wars, widespread famine, ecological breakdown, or cascading infrastructure failures. Historical cases of societal collapse, while complex and debated, often feature patterns of resource mismanagement, environmental degradation, elite blindness to emerging threats, and an inability to adapt to changing conditions. This suggests a plausible default outcome for societies unable to manage the pressures generated by the Jevons feedback.
A second potential outcome is Stagnation or reaching a suboptimal plateau: growth halts amidst ongoing crises, resource shortages, and lasting environmental damage, but society avoids full collapse, stabilizing at a much lower level of development, complexity, and energy throughput.
Such a civilization might persist for some time but persist as trapped below its potential, unable to progress toward higher Kardashev levels or large-scale interstellar activities, thus remaining effectively filtered from easy detection. This might result from partially successful, but insufficient, adaptations that prevent total disintegration without addressing deeper structural issues, leading to a prolonged period of fragility or vulnerability to future shocks.
The third, and likely most challenging, trajectory is Transformation: the civilization successfully anticipates or responds to the looming crisis by undertaking deep, structural changes.
This would involve actively managing or breaking the Jevons feedback loop, imposing effective limits on aggregate consumption (perhaps through resource caps, carbon taxes, or other regulatory tools), shifting rapidly and comprehensively toward sustainable energy and material systems (such as a highly resilient circular economy), and reorienting economic priorities (e.g., adopting post-growth, steady-state, or wellbeing-centered models rather than maintaining perpetual GDP growth) alongside cultural shifts prioritizing equity, sufficiency, ecological stewardship, and long-term thinking.
Achieving transformation would require extraordinary levels of societal learning, collective foresight, flexible and capable governance institutions able to manage shared resources and enact long-term policies, international collaboration, and the strategic deployment of technological innovations framed around sufficiency rather than unchecked expansion (Harari, 2014).
Assessing the likelihood of these outcomes involves weighing the relationship between environmental constraints (the speed and severity of ecological feedbacks, proximity to tipping points) and socio-political factors (the strength of governance, resistance from entrenched interests, cultural adaptability, and the degree to which inequality undermines cohesion). The sheer difficulty of reversing deeply entrenched growthdriven systems and the political complexities associated with implementing necessary limits suggest that collapse or stagnation may be statistically more common outcomes across a large number of hypothetical civilizations than successful transformation.
The apparent absence of “Type I” + civilizations in astronomical observations could be interpreted as indirect evidence supporting this view, suggesting that the transformation pathway is exceptionally rare. The filter’s primary downside ("con") is its potential to abruptly end civilizational development; its possible upside ("pro") lies in the rare chance to survive, adapt, and perhaps emerge stronger and wiser.
The Jevons Filter hypothesis, therefore, offers a detailed, process-based explanation linking technological advancement, economic pressures, and environmental constraints to account for the likely scarcity of long-lived, high-energy civilizations. It highlights an internal, structural challenge tied to managing growth and consumption, providing a potentially more universal filtering mechanism than models focused solely on external hazards or biological accidents.
## 5. Conclusion: The Jevons Filter, the Fermi Paradox, and Civilizational Trajectories
The Jevons Filter hypothesis, elaborated and substantiated in this paper through clearer definitions, expanded empirical context, deeper analysis of feedback mechanisms, and explicit engagement with counterarguments, offers a compelling, albeit sobering, perspective on the trajectory of technological civilizations and a potential, even if partial, explanation for the Fermi Paradox.
By grounding the concept in the well-documented Jevons Paradox and associated rebound effects and linking it to thermodynamic constraints on growth and the theoretical requirements for a stable “Type I” civilization, the hypothesis identifies a deep-seated developmental challenge: the possibility that efficiency-driven expansion naturally propels civilizations toward overshoot and crisis (collapse or stagnation) before long-term sustainability at a planetary scale can be secured.
Our analysis suggests that the apparent scarcity of advanced extraterrestrial intelligence may stem not only from the low probability of life’s emergence or the evolution of intelligence, but also from the immense difficulty rooted in navigating the transitional phase where a civilization’s technological capabilities accelerate rapidly, driving consumption toward planetary boundaries.
The Jevons Filter represents this critical crossroads, where the reinforcing cycle between efficiency improvements and total energy and resource use must be consciously disrupted to avoid self-limiting crises.
If most civilizations fail to make a decisive shift at this stage, remaining trapped within an expansionary cycle fueled by ongoing efficiency gains, then the Great Silence observed by SETI could reflect the natural outcome of a universe where technological intelligence frequently arises but rarely persists long enough to become widespread or easily observable.
This interpretation has far-reaching implications for reassessing the Drake Equation, suggesting that the average civilizational lifespan (L) or the fraction of civilizations that survive technological adolescence (fsurv) may be exceedingly small, exerting a dominant influence on the final estimate even if earlier factors are relatively favorable.
It offers a structural, process-based explanation for why long-lived technological societies might be exceptionally rare, consistent with anthropic reasoning (Carter, 1983). The absence of observed astroengineering projects such as Dyson spheres also fits this framework, implying that few civilizations advance to the point of mastering stable, high-energy operations at planetary or stellar scales (Wright et al., 2014).
In this way, the Jevons Filter functions at the planetary level, limiting the expansion of civilizations beyond their home worlds.
Although speculative elements remain — especially regarding hypothetical extraterrestrial civilizations and the detailed environmental impacts of nearing “Type I” thresholds — the hypothesis is grounded in well-documented historical economic trends (Jevons, 1866; Greene, 1992; Tsao et al., 2010), established rebound dynamics (Saunders, 1992; Sorrell, 2009), and thermodynamic approaches to economic systems (Garrett, 2011).
The critical evaluation of counterarguments, particularly around the prospects of absolute decoupling and breakthrough technologies, indicates that while alternative futures are conceivable, overcoming the fundamental feedback challenge likely requires more than technological advances alone. It emphasizes the urgency of addressing the deeper drivers of consumption and reimagining the foundations of economic expansion.
This interdisciplinary inquiry connects nineteenth-century economic insights to modern sustainability crises and fundamental questions about life in the cosmos. It reframes the pursuit of sustainability not merely as an environmental necessity but as a possible determinant of whether a civilization can endure across cosmic timescales — a threshold that most intelligent species may fail to cross.
While a shift toward a sustainable trajectory remains theoretically possible achievable through foresight, collective action, and large-scale societal change — the enduring silence of the universe offers a stark reminder of the formidable challenges involved. The Jevons Filter hypothesis urges humanity to confront the powerful internal forces steering its technological and economic evolution and to deliberately craft a future that avoids ecological overshooting, a task whose implications could extend far beyond the fate of Earth alone.
The study of such "filters," grounded in observable patterns rather than mere contingency, calls for deeper interdisciplinary research into the complex interconnections between technology, society, economy, and environment that shape the long-term fate of civilizations, both terrestrial and extraterrestrial.
Understanding these forces is more than an intellectual exercise; it may hold essential lessons for navigating the Anthropocene and securing a sustainable future.

## Appendix A: Variables and Definitions
The conceptual model presented in Section 3 draws upon several key variables, defined here for clarity.
W represents the Cumulative Wealth, signifying the integrated, inflation-adjusted economic output of a civilization over its history; it acts as a proxy for the size, complexity, and accumulated structure (physical and organizational) that requires energy for maintenance and operation (Units might be constant currency-years).
E denotes the Primary Energy Consumption Rate, which is the total rate at which a civilization extracts and uses primary energy sources from its environment, representing the power flow sustaining the civilization (Units typically Watts).
η signifies the average Energy Efficiency or Productivity, defined as the ratio of economic output (GDP, denoted Y=dW/dt) generated per unit of primary energy consumed (Y/E); this reflects the technological and organizational capability to convert energy into economic activity (Units might be constant currency/Joule).
λ is the Energy-Wealth Coupling Constant, representing the empirically observed ratio linking primary energy consumption to cumulative wealth (E/W); it signifies the approximate power required to sustain each unit of accumulated wealth, found by Garrett (2011) to be relatively stable for the recent global economy (Units might be Watts / constant currency).
Y stands for Gross Annual Economic Output (GDP), the rate of new wealth creation per unit time (Y=dW/dt=ηE) (Units typically constant currency / year).
Finally, K (or Emax) represents the Carrying Capacity or Maximum Sustainable Throughput, indicating the biophysical limits imposed by the planetary environment on the rate of resource extraction or waste emission that can be sustained indefinitely; this can be conceptualized in terms of energy flow (Emax) or other specific limiting factors (Units vary depending on the limit).

## Extending Norbert Elias: Adjacency and the Dynamics of Civilizations
Arthur Michelino arthur.michelino@gmail.com Introduction: Reopening the Civilizing Process
Civilizations have rarely been approached as systems of interdependence and strain. Most modern theories have oscillated between cyclical visions of rise and fall and static conceptions of blocs defined by cultural essence. Both perspectives have offered sweeping interpretations, but both also detached civilizations from the environments of pressure and interaction in which they have always existed.
To reframe civilizations as relational processes requires both recognition of their internal dynamics and attention to the external conditions that compel their transformation.
The cyclical vision was most powerfully articulated in the early twentieth century. Oswald Spengler’s Decline of the West treated civilizations as cultural organisms, each with its own destiny of birth, flourishing, and decline (Engels, 2019). His image of the West entering its twilight was both influential and controversial, but the cyclical metaphor encouraged scholars to see civilizations as self-contained entities progressing through preordained stages.
Pitirim Sorokin advanced a different version of the same logic in his theory of cultural dynamics (Targowski, 2009). Civilizations, in Sorokin’s account, oscillated between ideational, idealistic, and sensate phases, shifting from spiritual orientation to materialism and back again in long rhythms. Carroll Quigley later added his model of seven stages, where civilizations grew when institutions remained flexible and declined when they hardened into structures resistant to adaptation (Wolf, 1998). These formulations varied in detail, yet all encouraged the view of civilizations as enclosed units that moved through cycles largely independent of their surroundings.
The static conception found its most visible modern expression in Samuel Huntington’s The Clash of Civilizations. For Huntington, civilizations were not organisms but enduring cultural blocs, defined by deep traditions of religion and history. In the world that followed the Cold War, he argued, these blocs would provide the fundamental lines of conflict, replacing ideology and ideology-driven alliances with cultural confrontation (Kwon, 2010). Huntington captured a mood of uncertainty in the 1990s, but his theory reified civilizations into fixed identities, as if their boundaries were impermeable and their dynamics timeless.
Other traditions opened more promising directions. The Annales School, especially Fernand Braudel, placed civilizations in the longue durée of geography, material life, and economic structures (Smith, 1988). His Mediterranean studies revealed how ecological and commercial environments shaped cultural and political forms over centuries. Karl Jaspers offered another perspective in his notion of the Axial Age (Smith, 2015; Peet, 2019). Between 800 and 200 BCE, he argued, multiple civilizations experienced simultaneous breakthroughs in philosophy and religion, from Confucius in China to the Hebrew prophets, from the Greek philosophers to the Indian sages. The point was not isolation but simultaneity, suggesting that civilizations often evolve in relation to shared horizons of change.
Christopher Dawson (1991) gave religious traditions a leading role in civilizational identity, showing how spiritual frameworks ordered cultural development.
Arnold Toynbee attempted the most ambitious synthesis of the comparative tradition. His Study of History, spanning twelve volumes, treated civilizations as entities confronted by challenges (Wilkinson, 2019). Their fate depended on whether creative minorities could respond effectively. Civilizations grew when responses generated new vitality and declined when elites failed to adapt. Toynbee’s model moved closer to the notion of relational compulsion, but his framing remained episodic. Crises appeared as discrete tests rather than permanent conditions of strain.
In the late twentieth century, Shmuel Eisenstadt (2017) reframed civilizational diversity through the idea of multiple modernities. Civilizations facing similar processes of modernization did not converge on a single Western pattern but produced divergent trajectories. This pluralism broke decisively with the assumptions of inevitability that had guided earlier models, but the framework still underexplored the systemic pressures that shape plurality itself. Civilizations could diverge, but why they adapted at all remained implicit.
It was Norbert Elias who shifted the frame from cultural essences and cyclical schemes to long-term process.
In The Civilizing Process, Elias analyzed the gradual monopolization of coercion into centralized states and the parallel cultivation of foresight and restraint among individuals. Sociogenesis and psychogenesis advanced together, rulers stabilizing power through the suppression of private violence, subjects internalizing restraint through etiquette and discipline. Elias showed that political monopolization and behavioral change were inseparable and mutually reinforcing, producing webs of interdependence that restructured European society over centuries. Civilization was not moral progress but recursive transformation.
The strength of Elias’s model lay in its refusal to treat civilization as a fixed property. He demonstrated that changes in court etiquette, fiscal administration, and patterns of warfare were all part of the same process. He connected the macro with the micro, the institutional with the personal, making visible the dense interweaving of political authority and everyday conduct.
Yet the model left underdeveloped why this coupling advanced with such urgency. Feudal Europe had long been fragmented; why did consolidation accelerate so dramatically from the late Middle Ages onward? Subjects had always lived in webs of dependence; why did restraint deepen so much more in the early modern era? Interdependence alone could not explain the speed and intensity of the transformation.
Here other traditions provide the missing horizon. Charles Tilly argued that rulers centralized coercion and built fiscal systems not from preference but from necessity; war made the state (Kaspersen & Strandsbjerg, 2017). Randall Collins (1995) generalized the insight, showing that fragmentation collapses in competitive environments. Polities pressed on every side cannot remain decentralized. They were under intense pressure to centralize, and those that failed risked disappearance.
Read alongside Elias, these perspectives make clear that the civilizing process unfolded not in isolation but under compulsion. States and societies reorganized because environments of pressure left no alternative.
The argument of this article is that what applies to states also applies to civilizations. Civilizations civilize themselves not in isolation but in adjacency, a structural condition in which the organization of one generates consequential strain upon the order of another. Adjacency is not reducible to cultural borrowing or episodic contact. It is continuous and systemic. Civilizations are compelled to reorganize because rivals, networks, and frontiers impose pressures that exceed their capacity to remain unchanged. Rivalry forces consolidation, entanglement restructures hierarchies, overload compels adaptation, and spatial ordering embeds civilizations in contested fields.
This framework is here named the Relational Sociogenesis Model.
It preserves Elias’s insight that sociogenesis and psychogenesis advance together, while situating that process within systemic horizons of adjacency. Civilizations are understood as adaptive systems, evolving through cycles of consolidation, strain, and reorganization. Their trajectories are not autonomous arcs but records of recursive adaptation.
The article develops this argument in three movements.
- o It first revisits Elias’s civilizing process and situates his insight within the horizon of compulsion.
- o It then elaborates adjacency as a categorical concept, integrating perspectives from world-systems analysis, systems theory, and spatial order.
- o Finally, it demonstrates the model through comparative cases: Europe and the Ottomans, China and the steppe, Africa under imperial partition, and the frontier between Christendom and Islam.
The stakes are not confined to historical interpretation. In the present century, civilizational discourse has resurfaced in political practice under the rubric of the civilizational state. Russia, China, and India have invoked civilizational identity to legitimize strategic posture, portraying themselves as self-contained blocs.
At the same time, global crises expose the opposite reality. Climate disruption, pandemics, and financial contagion reveal adjacency on a planetary scale. Digital infrastructures generate new forms of systemic pressure, binding societies into interdependent fields where no boundary insulates.
Huntington’s blocs and Toynbee’s episodic crises cannot capture these dynamics. A relational model, grounded in Elias’s processual insight and formalized through adjacency, offers a more precise framework for understanding both historical transformation and contemporary adaptation.
The contribution of this article is therefore to restore Elias’s processual account by situating it within systemic conditions, to formalize adjacency as a categorical concept in civilizational theory, and to advance the Relational Sociogenesis Model as a framework for comparative analysis.
Civilizations civilize themselves by reorganizing under pressure. Adjacency is the condition that explains how.
## Norbert Elias and the Civilizing Process
Norbert Elias’s The Civilizing Process, published in German in 1939, remains one of the most ambitious attempts to explain civilizational transformation across the longue durée. Written during the turbulence of interwar Europe and overlooked until its rediscovery in the 1970s, it advanced an argument that civilization should be understood not as a cultural essence or a moral achievement but as a process. Its originality lay in showing how rulers and subjects, institutions and behaviors, were bound together in webs of interdependence that deepened and became more constraining over time.
The central claim of Elias’s work was that sociogenesis and psychogenesis advanced together (Mennell, 2015). On the sociogenetic side, political authority that had once been dispersed among feudal lords, knights, and cities was gradually monopolized by centralizing monarchies. Feuding was outlawed, private armies dismantled, and standing forces created to maintain internal order and project power abroad.
This consolidation required fiscal and administrative innovation. Rulers replaced extraordinary levies with permanent taxation, cultivated credit systems, and tied their regimes to financiers capable of sustaining long wars. Bureaucracy, which in medieval Europe had been episodic and personal, became institutionalized and professionalized. These institutional changes were inseparable from corresponding shifts in behavior.
The psychogenetic dimension of the process was visible in the growing demand for restraint. As webs of dependence thickened, individuals had to suppress impulses, anticipate the reactions of others, and regulate conduct with foresight (Jiri, 2013). The proliferation of etiquette manuals from the Renaissance onwards reflected this transformation. The regulation of table manners, the concealment of bodily functions, and the discrediting of impulsive violence marked a gradual reconfiguration of personality (Linklater & Mennell, 2010). Acts once considered acceptable in knightly society came to be regarded as shameful, while politeness, self-restraint, and refinement became symbols of distinction.
Elias’s insight into the idea was that these two processes were not parallel but intertwined. The monopolization of coercion created conditions in which restraint was necessary, and restraint reinforced political order by producing subjects capable of selfregulation.
The historical evidence supported this interpretation. In France, the Bourbon kings subordinated the nobility to the elaborate rituals of Versailles (Elias, 2006). Court etiquette was not superficial display but a technique of governance, binding nobles to monarchs by subjecting them to norms of dependence and self-discipline. In Spain, the union of Castile and Aragon and the culmination of the Reconquista produced a monarchy that centralized coercion while embedding restraint into religious orthodoxy, institutionalized through inquisitorial discipline (Rodríguez & Fantoni, 2012). In England, the Wars of the Roses extinguished baronial autonomy, allowing the Tudors to consolidate power and to establish a bureaucratic monarchy that gradually disciplined political elites (Mears, 2003).
In each of these cases, monopolization of coercion and internalization of restraint advanced together, producing societies that were more centralized, more regulated, and more bound by interdependence.
The civilizing process was therefore not an expression of cultural superiority or moral improvement. It was a recursive transformation in which states became stronger as individuals became more disciplined, and individuals became more disciplined as states became stronger. Elias succeeded in connecting domains often treated separately, linking the fiscal capacity of monarchies with the etiquette of court life, and the organization of armies with the regulation of manners. He provided a framework in which political authority and personal conduct could be seen as aspects of a single process.
The work, however, was published under circumstances that limited its reception. Released in Basel in 1939, just as Europe entered war, it reached few readers. Elias, a German Jew in exile in Britain, remained marginal to academic institutions.
In the postwar years, functionalism dominated sociology and realism shaped international relations, neither sympathetic to long-term processual approaches (Kolesnikova & al., 2016). Only from the 1970s, with the translation of his work into English and the advocacy of scholars such as Johan Goudsblom and Stephen Mennell, was Elias recognized. By the 1990s, his framework was being applied not only in sociology but also in international relations, where his emphasis on power and restraint provided tools for reinterpreting state formation and global order.
The belated reception underlined the originality of a theory formulated ahead of its time. The strength of Elias’s model lay in its ability to dissolve dichotomies. It bridged macro and micro levels of analysis by demonstrating that the structures of absolutist monarchy were inseparable from the etiquette of everyday life. It bridged politics and culture, showing how fiscal extraction and behavioral restraint were mutually reinforcing. It bridged stability and process, refusing to treat civilization as a fixed condition and presenting it instead as an evolving web of interdependence. This capacity to connect what had previously been separated explains why his work retains significance.
The framework also displayed an important limitation. Elias described how monopolization and restraint reinforced each other, but he did not explain why this coupling advanced with such urgency at particular moments. Feudal Europe had long been fragmented, although consolidation accelerated rapidly after the late Middle Ages. Subjects had always lived in relations of dependence, although the internalization of restraint deepened far more in the early modern period. Interdependence by itself cannot explain this acceleration. A further dimension was required to account for why the process moved with such intensity.
This dimension was made clearer by later historical sociology. Charles Tilly demonstrated that rulers built fiscal and military institutions because continuous warfare left them no alternative (González, 2016).
Randall Collins (1995) generalized the principle, arguing that fragmentation collapses in hostile environments. Polities pressed on every side found decentralization increasingly unsustainable; those unable to centralize faced high risks of decline or absorption.
Read alongside Elias, these perspectives reveal that the civilizing process unfolded not only because societies densified internally, but because their survival was threatened externally. Monopolization was accelerated by rivalry, and restraint was internalized because fiscal and military burdens demanded foresight and discipline.
The consolidation of France under the Bourbons, the decline of baronial autonomy in England, and the bureaucratic centralization of Spain therefore appear not only as inward trajectories of order but as responses to systemic compulsion. Even the spread of etiquette into the bourgeoisie, which Elias described as evidence of psychogenesis, can be understood as an adaptation to the new webs of fiscal and political dependence generated by systemic strain.
The civilizing process was real, but its urgency was explained not solely by interdependence. It was explained by adjacency.
Elias revealed how sociogenesis and psychogenesis advanced in tandem, producing webs of interdependence that reshaped both authority and conduct. Tilly and Collins clarified why this advance became so urgent, showing that consolidation was never only an inward trajectory but also a response to environments of strain. The implication is that civilizations civilize themselves not as isolated entities but as adaptive systems compelled to reorganize under pressure. Their evolution is best understood in adjacency.
## Adjacency as a Concept
If Elias revealed the internal dynamics of the civilizing process and Tilly and Collins clarified the horizon of compulsion, the next step would be to recognize that these dynamics apply not only to states but to civilizations as a whole. Civilizations never evolve in isolation. They exist in adjacency, a structural condition in which the organization of one generates consequential strain on the capacity for order in another.
Adjacency is not occasional contact or sporadic borrowing but a continuous environment of systemic pressure. It compels reorganization because institutions and behaviors cannot remain stable when exposed to neighbors, rivals, or networks that impose demands beyond their prior capacity.
Adjacency can be recognized through observable signals that show when external pressure compels systemic reorganization.
Rivalry is visible in military and fiscal innovations adopted under threat of defeat. Entanglement leaves its mark in translation movements, commercial dependency, or the redesign of administrative practices to manage external flows. Overload becomes apparent when prolonged campaigns, demographic shocks, or excessive resource drain force institutional restructuring or collapse. Spatial ordering manifests in the establishment of new frontier regimes, boundary institutions, or legal pluralism designed to manage contact zones.
These indicators do not exhaust every historical variation, but they provide a diagnostic lens through which adjacency can be distinguished from mere cultural borrowing or routine interaction.
The most visible mechanism of adjacency is rivalry. Civilizations pressed by strong competitors cannot tolerate internal fragmentation. The Ottoman advance into Europe demonstrates how rivalry created powerful incentives for consolidation. When armies reached the gates of Vienna, European rulers were compelled to sustain standing armies, develop permanent fiscal systems, and create more regular diplomatic arrangements (Wheatcroft, 2009; Ágoston, 1999, 2010). These innovations were not simply adopted for efficiency, they were compelled by pressure. Fiscal extraction became routinized, credit markets deepened, and new habits of administration took shape because survival depended on them. Rivalry was not only military, it reshaped societies more broadly. Populations were subjected to heavier taxation, conscription, and discipline, and patterns of restraint spread beyond courts into wider social strata.
Elias’s coupling of sociogenesis and psychogenesis advanced with greater intensity because rivalry made delay or resistance increasingly untenable.
China’s experience with the steppe illustrates the same principle with different modalities. Steppe polities, highly mobile and militarized, forced dynasties to adapt logistically and administratively (Pulleyblank, 1999; Di Cosmo, 2015, 2018). Maintaining large garrisons, building defensive works, and supplying campaigns across long distances demanded innovations in taxation and civilian–military administration (Groused, 1970).
Rivalry on the frontier did not only produce stronger armies but also bureaucracies capable of sustaining them. Dynastic renewal was driven as much by adjacency as by internal cycles, since fragmentation left agrarian regimes vulnerable to mobile confederations. Collins’s principle that fragmentation is rarely sustainable in hostile environments applies directly. Polities that could not centralize were absorbed, while those that adapted endured. Rivalry illustrates the basic logic of adjacency: competition compresses space, eliminates toleration of decentralization, and forces reorganization at every level of society.
Adjacency also operates through entanglement. Civilizations embedded in systems of exchange are compelled to adapt even when conflict is absent. Dependency on external flows reshapes authority and reorients institutions (Thornton, 1998). West African polities drawn into the Atlantic economy were reorganized around the capture and supply of captives, binding local power to external demand (Green, 2019). The mechanism here is not simply economic, it is political and social (Whatley, 2014). Rulers legitimized their authority through the capacity to provide what global markets demanded, while internal violence was channeled toward the procurement of captives. Authority, legitimacy, and coercion were reorganized through entanglement.
The Abbasid Caliphate demonstrates the same principle in a different register. Its vitality rested on participation in the Silk Road and Indian Ocean trade (Beaujard, 2019). Fiscal regimes and credit systems evolved in response to these exchanges. Taxation and administrative forms were not created in a vacuum but as adaptations to the requirements of long-distance commerce (Shatzmiller, 2019).
Entanglement thus reveals how adjacency reaches into the heart of civilizations. It forces rulers to manage flows they do not control but cannot ignore, producing institutional innovation in the process. This is why adjacency cannot be reduced to Wallerstein’s model of core and periphery. What matters is not only position in a hierarchy, but the compulsion created by entanglement itself, which restructures institutions regardless of whether a society sits at the center or the margins.
There are moments when adjacency produces pressures that exceed institutional capacity. This is overload, a systemic condition in which the inputs generated by environments saturate the structures meant to process them. Collapse is a possibility, but more often reorganization has emerged as the prevailing outcome. Rome illustrates this dynamic. Republican institutions, designed for a city-state, could not absorb the demands of an empire spanning the Mediterranean (Millar, 2002; Ando, 2013). The result was civil war and eventual transformation into imperial order under Augustus. What appeared as collapse was, in many cases, systemic reorganization, the emergence of new institutions capable of managing pressures that the Republic could not (Brunt, 1998).
Byzantium faced similar strain when Arab conquests severed its revenue base and threatened its frontiers (Haldon, 1990). The theme system decentralized fiscal and military responsibilities, recalibrating the empire’s capacity (Kaegi, 1995; Treadgold, 1995). This was not decline but adaptation under overload. In China, Ming reliance on massive fortification projects was a visible sign of overload, an attempt to manage inputs that exceeded the dynasty’s institutional resilience (Mote, 2003). The subsequent Qing innovations in frontier governance reveal how overload provoked systemic adjustment (Huang, 1981; Perdue, 2005).
Luhmann’s observation that open systems must process environmental inputs or perish captures the mechanism (Luhmann, 1989 & 1995). Civilizations overloaded by adjacency reorganize, often through institutional invention that preserves coherence under new conditions.
Adjacency is also generated through spatial ordering. Civilizations define their environments by structuring territory, frontiers, and spheres of influence. In doing so, they produce new horizons of strain.
Rome’s limes (border fortifications) along the Rhine and Danube did not merely defend territory, they created permanent adjacency with external peoples, embedding frontier pressure into the empire’s daily governance (Whittaker 1994; Breeze 2011). The Ottoman millet system, organizing religious communities within imperial authority, shows how spatial ordering structured adjacency internally, making difference a managed condition rather than an external threat (Barkey, 2008).
In the modern period, the Berlin Conference reconfigured Africa into imperial domains, producing hybrid sovereignties that continued into postcolonial states (Herbst, 2015). The Monroe Doctrine transformed the Americas into a hemisphere under United States authority, compelling neighbors to adapt to an imposed order (Sexton, 2011). Soviet dominance of Eastern Europe structured a bloc that generated adjacency within and across the Cold War divide (Westad, 2017).
These cases show that adjacency is not only endured but actively created by civilizations themselves. As Elden (2019) argues, ordering space is not neutral — it is itself a generator of systemic strain.
The four mechanisms of rivalry, entanglement, overload, and spatial ordering illustrate the operation of adjacency. Rivalry forces consolidation, entanglement restructures authority through dependency, overload compels adaptation when capacity is exceeded, and spatial ordering embeds civilizations in contested landscapes. Each mechanism demonstrates how environments compel reorganization beyond the preferences of rulers or the intentions of societies.
Civilizations are adaptive systems that survive only by processing the pressures generated by others.
This perspective also clarifies the limits of earlier theories. Toynbee’s challenge-andresponse emphasized external strain but treated it as an episodic crisis. Huntington’s blocs highlighted cultural depth but reified civilizations as fixed entities insulated from pressure. Eisenstadt recognized diversity but did not fully connect plurality to the systemic conditions that shaped it.
Adjacency explains why challenges are continuous, why blocs are porous, and why diversity is structured. Civilizations diverge not because they are autonomous but because they adapt differently under shared horizons of strain.
Adjacency, therefore, deserves recognition as a categorical concept in civilizational studies. It is not a synonym for interaction or interdependence, both of which describe contact without capturing its compulsion. Adjacency identifies the structural condition in which the organization of one civilization generates consequential strain on another. It formalizes the environment that makes adaptation necessary.
Civilizations do not rise and fall along autonomous arcs, nor do they remain fixed blocs of identity. They evolve as adaptive systems, reorganizing under systemic pressure. Their histories are records of adjacency, and their trajectories are intelligible only when adjacency is made explicit as the horizon of change.
Adjacency should not be understood as a deterministic law. Its effects vary by context, legacy institutions, and leadership capacity. It compels reorganization by narrowing options, but outcomes diverge depending on internal resilience. The concept applies most clearly at the level of civilizations when external pressures accumulate across multiple states, creating systemic horizons of strain. State-level adaptation is therefore nested within wider civilizational fields of compulsion.
## Comparative case studies
The explanatory force of adjacency becomes clearest when viewed through concrete histories. Civilizations do not evolve in isolation but reorganize under conditions of strain created by their neighbors, rivals, and networks.
Four cases illustrate this process in distinctive ways: the European encounter with the Ottoman Empire, the Chinese confrontation with steppe polities, Africa’s incorporation into global systems under colonial partition, and the frontier between Latin Christendom and the Islamic world.
Each case highlights the mechanisms of rivalry, entanglement, overload, and spatial ordering, showing that civilizational transformation is best understood as recursive adaptation to adjacency.
The Ottoman presence in Europe compelled the consolidation of states that otherwise might have remained fragmented. The fall of Constantinople in 1453 was more than a symbolic loss; it placed a powerful empire at the edge of Europe, commanding critical trade routes and projecting military capacity deep into the continent (Farokhi, 2004). The defeat at Mohács in 1526 and the sieges of Vienna in 1529 and 1683 kept alive the sense of permanent threat (Ágoston, 2021).
European rulers faced strong pressures that left few viable alternatives; sustaining standing armies and routinized taxation became the most durable responses (Tilly, 1992). Monarchs who failed to adapt were eclipsed by those who succeeded in embedding fiscal extraction into permanent institutions (Bonney, 1991). Behavioral restraint spread in parallel, as populations were subjected to greater taxation and mobilization. Nobles bound into court rituals and subjects disciplined into obedience were both expressions of the same logic: rivalry made restraint indispensable (Elias, 2000, Parker, 1996). Even diplomacy took on new forms. Permanent embassies and collective security arrangements emerged as states recognized that survival depended on coordination as much as on armies (Mattingly, 1988). The balance of power that later became Europe’s distinctive political system cannot be detached from the Ottoman horizon that shaped its evolution (Simms, 2013).
The Ottoman frontier illustrates rivalry as adjacency pressure. Military defeats at Vienna and subsequent wars constrained European polities to expand fiscal capacity, create standing armies, and standardize military technologies. These reforms were not voluntary borrowings but compelled adaptations to survive rivalry. At the same time, some smaller states, such as Poland–Lithuania, failed to reorganize despite facing identical pressures, showing that adjacency constrains but does not predetermine outcomes.
The Chinese experience with the steppe reveals the same logic through a different configuration. From the Han onward, agrarian dynasties confronted polities whose mobility gave them strategic advantage (Thomas, 1989; Di Cosmo, 2002). The Xiongnu, Khitan, Jurchen, Mongols, and Zunghars did not merely raid but compelled structural adaptation within Chinese institutions (Elliott, 2001; Rossabi, 2011). The Ming dynasty’s decision to invest enormous resources in fortifications along the northern frontier was not an architectural statement but a strategy of survival (Waldron, 1990; Hucker, 2020).
Logistics, taxation, and administration were reshaped around the imperative of sustaining garrisons and moving supplies across vast distances (Andrade, 2017). The Qing defeat of the Zunghars in the eighteenth century exemplified how adjacency forced innovation (Elliot, 2001; Millward, 2021). Frontier governance, population transfers, and taxation were reorganized to sustain campaigns that extended imperial authority deep into Central Asia (Perdue, 2005). These adaptations were not optional. Dynasties that resisted centralization or innovation became more vulnerable to steppe powers, while those that adapted were more likely to endure (Lattimore, 1962). Adjacency on the frontier explains the rhythm of dynastic renewal as clearly as any internal cycle. It was the pressure of the steppe that revealed the limits of fragmentation and forced rulers to embed new capacities into their states.
The China–Steppe relation shows how spatial ordering and rivalry intertwined. Persistent nomadic threats forced dynasties to invest in wall-building, frontier garrisons, and administrative units specialized in border governance. These measures exemplify adjacency as compulsion through external strain. Yet the longevity of some dynasties also depended on internal factional consolidation, reminding us that adjacency was one driver among others, not a sufficient condition.
Africa illustrates adjacency through entanglement and spatial reordering rather than rivalry. The integration of West African polities into the Atlantic economy restructured authority around the supply of captives (Law, 1991; Inikori, 2002; Lovejoy, 2011). States such as Dahomey and Asante were reorganized to meet the demands of external markets, directing internal violence toward procurement for export. Authority, legitimacy, and coercion were all shaped by this systemic dependency.
When the Berlin Conference divided the continent into colonial domains, adjacency was imposed through cartography (Englebert & al., 2002; Herbst, 2015). Borders drawn without regard to existing polities forced new forms of political adaptation. Hybrid institutions emerged as colonial regimes combined indirect and direct rule, leaving legacies that persisted long after independence (Young, 1994; Mamdani, 2018).
Ethiopia, which resisted full colonization, nonetheless adapted to the pressure of encroaching empires by modernizing its military and centralizing authority (Zewde, 2002; Marcus, 2023). Along the Swahili coast, adjacency took the form of commercial integration with Islamic, Indian, and Chinese traders, producing hybrid cultural and political forms that reveal entanglement as a civilizational force (Horton & Middleton, 2000; Pearson, 2002; Pouwels, 2002). Africa demonstrates that adjacency is not reducible to conquest. Entanglement with global systems and imposition of spatial orders were equally powerful in compelling reorganization.
Atlantic entanglement created adjacency through dependency. West African polities restructured their economies and political institutions to manage flows of firearms, captives, and commodities, embedding external pressures deep into domestic structures. Overload became visible when these entanglements undermined resilience, paving the way for colonial partition. Still, variation across polities such as Dahomey’s militarization versus Asante’s relative flexibility shows that adjacency translated into divergent institutional paths.
The Mediterranean frontier between Latin Christendom and Islam reveals all four mechanisms of adjacency within a single zone.
 Rivalry is evident in the Crusades, which mobilized resources across Europe and led to fiscal and military innovation under papal direction (Tyerman, 2007; Riley-Smith, 2022).
-  Entanglement is visible in the translation movement, where Arabic texts on science, philosophy, and medicine entered Latin learning, reshaping European intellectual life (Burnett, 2001; Gutas, 2002; Hasse, 2016).
-  Overload occurred when prolonged campaigns exhausted resources and forced states to reorganize (Bartlett, 1993; Abulafia, 2011).
-  The Reconquista in Iberia demonstrates how spatial ordering transformed entire societies, producing centuries of frontier adaptation (Fletcher, 2006; O’Callaghan, 2013). When Granada fell in 1492, the Iberian monarchy emerged from this crucible with the capacity to project power overseas (Kamen, 2003; Elliott, 2006). Expansion into the Americas was made possible by the institutions forged under adjacency with Islam. Nor was influence one-sided. The Islamic world also reorganized under crusading pressure and Iberian expansion, adapting to loss, integrating new knowledge, and shifting institutions (Hillenbrand, 2006; Cobb, 2014).
-  The Mediterranean frontier shows civilizations evolving in reciprocal transformation, reorganizing not in isolation but through continuous adjacency (Horden, 2000).
The Mediterranean frontier encapsulates multiple mechanisms of adjacency. Crusading rivalry generated fiscal and military innovation, while translation movements demonstrate entanglement. The long Reconquista exemplified spatial ordering, creating frontier societies that eventually projected power overseas.
These mechanisms show how adjacency shaped civilizational transformation, but they did not eliminate choice. Venice, for example, pursued maritime entanglement rather than territorial reconquest, underscoring the diversity of responses within a shared frontier.
Across these cases, the same pattern emerges in different guises. Europe’s fiscalmilitary states were forged in rivalry with the Ottomans. Chinese dynastic resilience was shaped by the pressures of the steppe. African polities were reorganized through entanglement with global markets and the imposition of colonial cartographies. Latin Christendom and the Islamic world developed reciprocally through centuries of frontier contact. The specific outcomes varied, but in each case the mechanisms of adjacency compelled adaptation. Civilizations restructured their institutions, transformed their behaviors, and recalibrated their trajectories because environments of pressure severely constrained available choices.
These cases demonstrate that the civilizing process was never an inward movement alone.
The monopolization of coercion and the cultivation of restraint advanced most intensively when civilizations were compelled to adapt under systemic strain. Rivalry accelerated centralization, entanglement reorganized authority through dependency, overload forced reconfiguration of institutions, and spatial ordering embedded civilizations in contested fields.
Civilizational history, when read through adjacency, is revealed as a series of recursive adaptations to environments of compulsion. The implication is that civilizations must be understood as open systems, continuously reorganizing under pressures generated by others.
## Theoretical framing
The comparative record confirms that the processes identified by Elias do not advance in isolation. States and societies reorganize most intensely when compelled by environments of strain, and civilizations evolve in the same way. Europe’s balance of power, China’s dynastic rhythms, Africa’s hybrid postcolonial forms, and Iberia’s global expansion all point to a single conclusion: the coupling of authority and restraint unfolds within horizons of adjacency.
Understanding civilization as a process is to understand that its trajectory is conditioned by systemic pressure.
The strength of Elias’s framework lay in the recognition that political monopolization and behavioral restraint advanced in tandem. The centralization of coercion, the suppression of private violence, and the embedding of foresight into conduct were all elements of the same dynamic.
Elias captured this reciprocity within European history, but he left underexplored why these movements accelerated with such urgency. It was not interdependence alone that produced monopolization and restraint, it was rivalry, dependency, and overload. Tilly’s dictum that war made the state, and Collins’s observation that fragmentation collapses in hostile environments, clarify why Elias’s processes became so compelling. The inward logic of interdependence was matched by an outward horizon of compulsion.
To this horizon must be added the insight that civilizations are embedded in exchanges that bind them into hierarchies of dependency. Wallerstein’s world-systems analysis sharpened this perspective, showing how cores and peripheries are connected by flows of goods, capital, and labor (Sorinel, 2010). Entanglement created sustained pressures for transformation even in the absence of open rivalry. The Abbasids adapted taxation and credit to sustain commerce across Asia, and African polities restructured their political orders around Atlantic demand.
These were not autonomous decisions but systemic adaptations to pressures created by integration into wider circuits. Unlike world-systems theory, however, adjacency does not reduce civilizations to positions within a hierarchy. Its emphasis lies on the compulsion generated by the environment, whether or not a society occupies the center. What matters is the structural necessity to reorganize, not the place within a global order.
Another dimension becomes visible when adjacency generates pressures that exceed institutional capacity. Luhmann’s systems theory clarifies why overload need not mean collapse. Civilizations are open systems that must process inputs from their environments, and when inputs surpass capacity, reorganization is the alternative to extinction. The transformation of Rome from Republic to Empire is best understood as systemic reorganization in the face of overload, as was the Byzantine shift to the theme system or the Qing innovations in frontier governance. What appear as collapses or declines are often adaptive recalibrations forced by adjacency. Civilizations endure not by remaining unchanged but by transforming themselves to meet the strain imposed by their environments.
Spatial order demonstrates that adjacency is not only endured but actively created. Schmitt argued that the ordering of space is constitutive of political life, and this insight extends to civilizations (Hooker, 2009; Legg, 2011). The partition of Africa, Rome’s military frontiers, the Ottoman millet system, and the Soviet bloc all illustrate how civilizations impose spatial structures that generate new fields of compulsion. Adjacency is not accidental contact but the product of how civilizations structure land, frontiers, and spheres of influence. In this sense, civilizations manufacture the very environments that later constrain them.
The comparative record also shows that adjacency does not produce uniformity. Eisenstadt’s notion of multiple modernities captures the diversity of outcomes. Europe developed fiscal-military states and balance-of-power diplomacy, China renewed dynastic cycles under frontier pressure, Africa produced hybrid postcolonial states, and Iberia translated frontier rivalry into global expansion. These trajectories were distinct, but they were all compelled responses to adjacency. Diversity was structured rather than autonomous, emerging not from isolation but from different adaptations to shared systemic pressures.
This synthesis makes clear that civilizational theory requires reframing. Elias revealed the coupling of sociogenesis and psychogenesis, Toynbee emphasized challenge and response, Huntington reified blocs, Eisenstadt highlighted plurality.
Each captured an aspect of the phenomenon, but no one identified adjacency as the categorical condition.
Adjacency clarifies why Elias’s coupling advanced, why Toynbee’s challenges were continuous, why Huntington’s blocs are porous, and why Eisenstadt’s pluralities are structured. Civilizations evolve as adaptive systems compelled to reorganize under pressure. Their histories are not self-contained narratives but systemic responses to adjacency.
The implication is that adjacency must be treated as more than description. Interaction and interdependence remain valuable terms, but they are insufficient because they do not identify the compulsion that makes adaptation necessary. Adjacency captures the structural condition of consequential strain. It provides the missing category that allows civilizational analysis to be integrated with systems theory, where environments are not background context but active generators of transformation.
Civilizations, in this perspective, are open systems that receive inputs in the form of rivalry, entanglement, overload, and spatial ordering. Their capacity to endure rests on their ability to process these inputs through institutional and behavioral transformation. Their trajectories, whether toward consolidation or decline, are best understood as systemic responses to adjacency.
This orientation also extends debates long familiar in Comparative Civilizations Review. David Wilkinson’s notion of a “Central Civilization” anticipated the systemic perspective by showing how interlinked networks merge into a single field of interaction (Wilkinson, 1987). Carroll Quigley, in turn, emphasized institutional flexibility as the decisive condition of civilizational endurance or collapse (Quigley, 1961). The concept of adjacency builds on these insights by specifying the structural pressures that make such mergers and adaptations not episodic tests but continuous systemic conditions.
This reframing provides civilizational analysis with a new conceptual foundation. Elias supplied the process, Tilly and Collins exposed the horizon of compulsion, Wallerstein and Luhmann clarified systemic pressure and overload, Schmitt revealed the centrality of spatial order, and Eisenstadt highlighted plurality. These insights converge in the recognition that civilizations civilize themselves not in isolation but in adjacency. The concept gives civilizational theory its categorical precision, aligning it with systems thinking and clarifying why diversity persists within shared horizons of strain.
Civilizations are adaptive systems, compelled to reorganize under pressure, and their histories are intelligible only when adjacency is placed at the center of analysis.
Adjacency converges with several established approaches while differing in crucial respects. In relation to world-systems theory, adjacency shares the recognition that external pressures shape systemic development.
But whereas world-systems theory organizes these relations through the positional logic of core, semi-periphery, and periphery, adjacency is indifferent to positional ranking. It focuses instead on the compulsion exerted by consequential strain, which can operate between any neighbors, whether great or small, central, or marginal. This shift makes adjacency applicable to cases where systemic reorganization emerges without reference to global hierarchies, as in steppe–China dynamics or Mediterranean frontier zones.
The concept also resonates with peer-polity interaction and related archaeological models of competitive state formation. Like those approaches, adjacency acknowledges that rivalry drives innovation. Yet adjacency extends beyond competition by encompassing entanglement, overload, and spatial ordering as equally generative mechanisms. It thus captures processes where compulsion arises not from rivals seeking dominance, but from resource saturation, boundary management, or the embedding of external flows.
Finally, adjacency intersects with frontier and state-competition theories that emphasize external military pressure as a spur to institutional change. Here again the overlap is partial. Adjacency incorporates rivalry but does not reduce civilizational transformation to it. Spatial ordering highlights how civilizations manufacture frontiers that then shape their trajectories, while overload underscores how excessive mobilization can destabilize as much as it consolidates.
By drawing these mechanisms together, adjacency offers a broader explanatory vocabulary than frameworks centered solely on competition or frontier defense.
## Conclusion
Norbert Elias reframed the study of civilization by treating it not as a possession or a moral accomplishment but as a process. States monopolized coercion, subjects internalized restraint, and these two dynamics reinforced one another in recursive fashion across centuries. His work revealed how webs of interdependence transformed both authority and conduct. What he left underdeveloped was why these processes advanced with such urgency in particular times and places. The comparative evidence shows that the answer lies in adjacency.
Civilizations civilize themselves not in isolation but in relation, reorganizing under the pressures generated by neighbors, rivals, and networks.
The historical record makes this clear. European states consolidated because rivalry with the Ottoman Empire rendered fragmentation increasingly unsustainable. Chinese dynasties reorganized under the permanent horizon of steppe power. African polities were reshaped through entanglement with global markets and the spatial impositions of empire.
Latin Christendom and the Islamic world evolved in reciprocal transformation across centuries of frontier contact. These cases illustrate not only episodic crises but continuous conditions of strain. The coupling of authority and restraint that Elias described was propelled forward by adjacency.
This perspective clarifies the limits of some of the most influential theories in civilizational studies. Arnold Toynbee saw civilizations as tested by challenges, their fate dependent on whether creative minorities responded effectively. What the comparative evidence suggests is that challenges were not episodic but permanent. Civilizations are not tested solely at moments of crisis. They exist continuously in adjacency, where pressures narrow choices and demand repeated adaptation.
Samuel Huntington, by contrast, cast civilizations as blocs of cultural identity, destined to clash along fixed boundaries. The weakness of this approach lies in its static conception of boundaries, as if civilizations were insulated from one another. The cases reviewed here reveal the opposite: boundaries are porous zones where civilizations reorganize most visibly.
Toynbee captured the significance of strain but mistook its episodic rhythm; Huntington recognized the persistence of cultural traditions but reified them into static blocs. Adjacency corrects both misreadings by identifying the continuous systemic condition under which civilizations evolve.
Eisenstadt’s emphasis on multiple modernities stands closer to this reframing, for it acknowledges that civilizations adapt differently when confronted by similar global pressures. What the concept of adjacency adds is an explanation of why these adaptations occur in the first place. Diversity is not the product of autonomy but of structured responses to systemic environments. European balance-of-power diplomacy, Chinese dynastic renewal, African hybrid institutions, and Iberian expansion were all distinctive outcomes, but they were also compelled adaptations. Multiplicity is genuine, but it is embedded in shared horizons of strain.
Recognizing adjacency as the categorical condition of civilizational life also clarifies the link between historical sociology and systems theory. Civilizations appear not as cultural organisms rising and falling according to inner logic, nor as immovable blocs fixed by tradition, but as open adaptive systems. They receive inputs in the form of rivalry, entanglement, overload, and spatial ordering, and their capacity to endure rests on their ability to process these inputs. When pressures exceed capacity, institutions are reorganized, behaviors recalibrated, and authority transformed.
The histories of civilizations are thus records of systemic response. Some adaptations stabilize order, others fragment it, but all are shaped by adjacency.
This reframing has implications that extend beyond historical interpretation. In the present century, civilizations confront adjacency on a planetary scale. Climate change binds societies into common ecological strain, generating pressures that no single polity can escape. Pandemics reveal the depth of interdependence in mobility and health, exposing vulnerabilities that force reorganization of governance and cooperation. Financial contagion transmits across markets in real time, making national autonomy an illusion. Digital infrastructures create new forms of adjacency, where data flows, platform economies, and cyber vulnerabilities compel adaptation far beyond traditional boundaries. Global platforms exemplify this dual compulsion. Companies such as Amazon, Alibaba, or Meta generate entanglement by embedding states and societies in transnational networks of commerce and communication, while simultaneously producing overload as regulatory systems struggle to manage the velocity and scale of these flows.
The result has been the proliferation of privacy regimes, antitrust initiatives, and crossborder data frameworks that emerge not from voluntary design but from systemic pressure. In this sense, adjacency operates materially in the present, compelling institutional reorganization much as it did in earlier epochs. Political leaders may speak of civilizational states as self-contained blocs, but systemic crises reveal that no civilization exists outside adjacency.
The Relational Sociogenesis Model advanced here provides a framework for analyzing these conditions. By embedding Elias’s coupling of sociogenesis and psychogenesis within systemic horizons, it explains why civilizations reorganize as adaptive systems. By formalizing adjacency as a categorical concept, it identifies the mechanisms through which compulsion operates. By clarifying that diversity emerges within shared horizons of strain, it provides precision for understanding plurality.
Civilizations do not simply progress or decline. They reorganize recursively under the pressures generated by their environments.
The implications for comparative research are significant. Civilizational studies can be advanced by investigating how adjacency produces different trajectories across time and space. Comparative work can examine how rivalry compels consolidation in some cases but fragmentation in others, how entanglement reorganizes authority differently depending on institutional legacies, how overload produces collapse in certain contexts but renewal in others, and how spatial ordering generates stable frontiers in some regions and instability in others. Adjacency provides a language for comparison that avoids both cyclical determinism and static essentialism. It situates civilizations in the environments that shape them without denying the diversity of their outcomes.
This perspective also points to a research agenda for the future.
Historical sociology of civilizations can be enriched by systematic studies of adjacency, whether through the analysis of military competition, trade networks, ecological pressures, or digital infrastructures. Systems theory offers tools for modelling how inputs are processed and how overload leads to reorganization. Civilizational analysis can thus move beyond descriptive typologies and engage directly with the mechanisms of transformation. Adjacency provides the conceptual foundation for this integration, allowing civilizational studies to engage more fully with the language of systemic adaptation.
The argument advanced here is not that adjacency explains everything, but that without it the civilizing process cannot be understood fully. Elias captured the reciprocity of authority and restraint, but his framework was incomplete. Tilly, Collins, Wallerstein, Luhmann, Schmitt, and Eisenstadt each supplied pieces of the puzzle. The concept of adjacency formalizes what links their insights and reframes the study of civilization as the analysis of adaptive systems under systemic pressure.
Civilizations, in this view, do not rise and fall as isolated entities. They evolve in relation to one another, compelled to reorganize by the pressures of rivalry, dependency, overload, and spatial order. Their histories become most intelligible when adjacency is placed at the center of analysis. Civilizations civilize themselves by reorganizing under pressure. Adjacency is the horizon that explains how.

## Toward the Ideal of a World Civilization: Progressive Exploration by Japanese Scholars
Kōji Yoshino Nagasaki Wesleyan University yoshino@wesleyan.ac.jp Introduction
What kind of academic discipline is the comparative study of civilizations? To begin with, it can be described as a scientific field that takes civilization as its object of study, researching the various civilizations of the world, both ancient and modern, Eastern and Western, through the method of comparison. However, what exactly is this object of study, "civilization"? Specifically, to what does it refer? Furthermore, when we speak of "comparison," what kind of method does this entail? It is difficult to provide clear answers to these numerous questions. The primary reason for this difficulty stems from the inherent polysemy and ambiguity of “civilization” itself.
It goes without saying that many predecessors have diligently refined the comparative study of civilizations. Limiting our scope to Japan alone, we can see a diverse range of achievements, such as those presented in Fifty Figures in the Comparative Study of Civilizations (Ogura, ed., 2024). Observing the intellectual endeavors of these pioneers, one is struck by the realization that civilization is a subject of immense geographical breadth and spiritual depth, demanding to be addressed with a long-term perspective that spans the entirety of human history.
Other religious figures, thinkers, and scholars in Japan have also deeply contemplated the nature of civilization. It is noteworthy that their reflections, due to Japan's unique geographical and historical position, possessed a “comparative” perspective from the outset.
From ancient to early modern times, Chinese studies (Kangaku), and from the early modern to the modern period, Western studies (Yogaku), served as benchmarks for shaping the nation. In this respect, Japan's approach to receiving foreign civilizations could itself be described as an inherent orientation toward comparative study of civilizations. In the modern and contemporary eras, this manifested as a comparison between the West and the East and has recently been updated to the perspective of the “global” and the “local.” In a way, re-evaluating social and historical phenomena through such lenses has become a customary mode of thought in Japan.
Building on this premise, if we were to freely list figures considered archetypal of Japan from among those featured in Fifty Figures in Comparative Study of Civilizations, the list would be as follows:
 Field of Thought and Religion: Okakura Tenshin, Suzuki Daisetz, Nakamura Hajime, Izutsu Toshihiko
 Field of History and Periodization: Nishida Kitaro, Nishitani Keiji, Ito Shuntaro
 Field of Geography and Area Studies: Watsuji Tetsuro, Umesao Tadao
This classification organizes the intellectual frameworks of Japanese comparative study of civilizations into the perspectives of “Thought and Religion,” “History and Periodization,” and “Geography and Area Studies.” It must be stressed that this is merely for convenience; a truly strict and clear-cut division is, of course, not possible. Rather, the intellectual endeavors of all these scholars span multiple disciplines. Therefore, I wish to state in advance that this is a provisional arrangement, intended only to be a helpful guide based on their primary research themes.
Okakura Tenshin (1863–1913), under the slogan “Asia is one,” drew an intellectual map of a vast Asia, including India, as a common foundation. Nakamura Hajime (1912– 1999) built upon the foundation of Buddhist thought, which grew richer as early Buddhism spread north and south, and sought to identify a universal philosophy by contrasting it with Western thought. Izutsu Toshihiko (1914–1993) attempted, on an even grander scale, to grasp a “Spiritual East” that included the Islamic world east of Greece.
Meanwhile, Nishida Kitaro (1870–1945), particularly in his later years, articulated that Japan had a mission to overcome the “modern civilization” it was historically destined to confront. Nishida's student, Nishitani Keiji (1900–1990), while steadfastly adhering to scientific principles, tackled the puzzling question of East Asia's historical mission to transcend modern civilization and bridge the way to a next-generation culture. Furthermore, Ito Shuntaro (1930–2023), a member of the post-war generation, sought to relativize the locality of the Western scientific revolution by unearthing the history of science in Arabia and Byzantium. We will discuss Ito in greater detail later.
Finally, the geographical comparison of civilizations was undertaken by Watsuji Tetsuro (1889–1960) and Umesao Tadao (1920–2010). Watsuji summarized the interrelationship between geographical environment and modes of thought with the term fūdo (climate and milieu). In his An Ecological View of History (1967), Umesao Tadao proposed a geographical division that designated Western Europe and the Far East (centered on Japan) as “Region I,” and the rest of the Eurasian continent as “Region II,” with the arid zone at its center serving as the boundary. Both Watsuji and Umesao shared the common theme of attempting to draw a more nuanced map of the arrangement of civilizations, rather than relying on a simple dichotomy of West and East or Europe and Asia.
The task of this paper, building upon this legacy of Japanese comparative study of civilizations, is to discuss how “civilization” as an object of study has been established and how it has evolved.
## 1. What is Civilization?
Even a brief glance at the accumulated body of the Japanese comparative study of civilizations, as shown above, reveals the emergence of various images of “civilization.” Civilization is just that convenient of a concept. Looking at compound words such as urban civilization, the benefits of civilization, material civilization, and machine civilization, we can see that the term is used freely, and today it is employed with the broadest possible meaning.
Consequently, generalizing about this thing called civilization seems to have become quite difficult. It encompasses the visible and the invisible, the material and the spiritual, the modern and the ancient, the Western and the Eastern; it is like a large bag that can hold anything. It seems possible to attach the word “civilization” to all manner of things discovered across time and space. Moreover, there is a curious aspect to it: once the term civilization is attached to something, it is somehow felt to be real.
On the other hand, it is also a fact that the word civilization has become quite ambiguous. Not only that, but another concept, culture, has come to be used with a similar meaning to civilization, further complicating the matter. Civilization and culture overlap, yet they are used in distinct ways by those who employ them. Thus, even when an attempt is made to define the words civilization and culture, the result is that the definition immediately adds yet another new meaning.
For this reason, an endless vicious cycle may have arisen in the contemporary comparative study of civilizations. This vicious cycle must be broken immediately. To do so, it is necessary to attempt to reorganize the concept of civilization, which has been used variously and at times carelessly, while learning from the research of our predecessors. Without this, it will be impossible to re-establish the word civilization as a collective understanding in the contemporary comparative study of civilizations.
## Material Civilization and Spiritual Culture
First, let us begin by confirming the contexts in which the words civilization and culture have been used. As a starting point, I would like to touch upon a common view. In the study of civilization and culture to date, the contrasting phrases “material civilization” and “spiritual culture,” which are representative terms for distinguishing the two, have often been highlighted. Indeed, things related to the material, such as ancient cities and buildings, or industry and science, have been included in the category of civilization.
On the other hand, spiritual matters such as religion, thought, and philosophy have been considered to belong to the category of culture. The combinations of civilization and material, and culture and spiritual, have been understood as being well-suited to each other. In fact, a statistical analysis of usage examples would yield comparable results.
This becomes clear when we examine the search results from the National Diet Library Digital Collection to determine whether “material” or “spiritual” is more compatible with “civilization” and “culture.” According to this, the combination of “material civilization and spiritual culture” yields an overwhelming 17,997 results, whereas “spiritual civilization and material culture” yields a mere 2,010 (search conducted on July 5, 2025). Therefore, a general explanation can be provided that, in line with popular understanding, the usage of civilization as something material and culture as something spiritual is common.
However, exceptions that do not fit this popular interpretation are too numerous to count. On the contrary, even a brief examination of etymology and practical usage should reveal that a simple understanding of civilization as material and culture as spiritual can lead to misunderstanding and misuse. Looking back at the history of the formation of “civilization” and “culture,” we can confirm that there is a domain where the two subtly intersect.
## The Etymology of Civilization and Culture
What is civilization? Although it is often discussed, it is necessary to explain by tracing back to the word's origin, its etymology (Yoneda 1924, Hayashi 1972).
When a certain number of people gather, a city is formed. In Latin, a city is called a civitas. A city built on a sophisticated design cannot function well if it is a mixture of disparate and miscellaneous things. Uniformity, standardization, and normalization are the lifeblood of a city. Walls are built, districts are organized, buildings are erected, and infrastructure such as roads and water systems are put in place. The moment a defect occurs anywhere in these, the city immediately falls into dysfunction. A city is systematized to that extent.
This is not limited to the material and institutional aspects. Even the residents who live there must possess standardized norms, manners, and virtues. In other words, a "citizenlike" quality (civilis) is indispensable. Thus, as individuals equipped with the qualifications to live in a city, that is, citizens (civis), are born, these citizens in turn make the city more robust. The term civilization was given to collectively refer to this ideal organizational structure, production technology, and mentality formed by citizens.
In contrast, what does culture mean? In terms of word formation, culture is newer than civilization. Culture was originally born as a word opposing nature. The tangible and intangible products that humans create by processing nature are what we call culture.
The etymology of culture is the Latin word cultus, meaning to cultivate or to care for. We can think of culture as the entire range of activities through which humans apply their hand to nature to obtain products. In this sense, it is suggestive that the word “culture” is found within the stem of the word agriculture. That is because agriculture is the act of caring for and tending to the land (agri).
Of course, what humans produce by processing nature is not limited to agricultural crops. By cutting down trees to build houses, or by melting iron ore to make farm tools, they devise ways to improve the convenience and efficiency of life. Furthermore, culture does not only refer to the processing of living things and materials. With the evolution of human knowledge, the care and cultivation of the mind and spirit also come to be practiced. It is for this reason that another meaning, that of cultivation or spiritual training, is included in the semantics of culture.
In this way, civilization and culture may seem not to differ in the sense that they are both things created by humans utilizing what is around them. If one were to point out a difference, it would be that civilization is created by skimming off and systematizing the cream of culture from the agricultural villages surrounded by nature and bringing it to the city.
It is only the degree of systematization and refinement that differs; in terms of content, there is no major difference. In fact, looking at the etymology, the reality is that both civilization and culture have been used without distinction between the material and the spiritual. The reason the distinction between “material civilization” and “spiritual culture” is nothing more than a popular interpretation lies in this fact.
Nevertheless, if they were recognized as interchangeable synonyms, it did not pose a major problem. However, a trend of intentionally using them differently emerged. As a result, inconsistencies gradually arose in their usage, leading to contradictions in their respective conceptual definitions. To understand this process, I would next like to confirm the historical context in which civilization and culture have been used.
## 2. On the Usage of Civilization and Culture
The idea of civilization was originally championed in France and Britain, from where it subsequently spread to America. In Britain and France, where the word civilization has been used for a long time, it could even be said that the word culture has been disregarded (Nishikawa 1995, p. 55).
Let us introduce an example where Marie-Jean-Antoine Nicolas de Caritat, Marquis de Condorcet (1743–1794), succinctly described the notion of civilization that emerged after the French Revolution at the end of the 18th century.
At the end of his Sketch for a Historical Picture of the Progress of the Human Mind, Condorcet states three hopes for “the future state of the human mind”: “the destruction of inequality between different nations; the progress of equality in one and the same nation; and lastly, the real perfecting of mankind.” The word civilization encapsulates these future hopes. In other words, the ideals that civilization, which liberates humanity from the shackles of nature, should aim for are liberty, equality, and progress.
So, who will be responsible for realizing the completion of civilization in the future? While there is no doubt that it is all of humanity, the ones to lead the way are none other than the advanced nations, centered on France. “Will not every nation one day arrive at the state of civilization attained by those people who are most enlightened, most free, and most exempt from prejudices, as the French, for instance, and the AngloAmericans?” (Condorcet 1966, p. 222).
Here, Condorcet's dream is clearly articulated. However, we must not overlook the fact that the structure of a people who bear the responsibility of civilization and the rest of humanity who receive it has also been unintentionally depicted. To put it simply, at this time, a single axis of evaluation was established. That is, an axis for measuring whether a civilization is developed or lagging.1 At the end of the 18th century in Europe, an evaluation emerged where France and Britain were seen as leading civilization, and Germany was considered a nation (people) that was lagging and should receive it.
However, as might be expected, it was inevitable that a counter-current of thought would emerge in opposition to this structure and evaluation axis. The prime example is Germany, which was branded as a nation that had fallen behind in civilization. And Germany attempted to counter the evaluation axis of the civilized nations by strongly promoting its own concept of culture. “German Romanticism, which developed as a movement in opposition to the French Revolution, is precisely characterized by the formation and development of the concept of Kultur (culture) in opposition to civilization” (Nishikawa 1995, p. 65).
1 Similarly, François Pierre Guillaume Guizot (1787-1874), like Condorcet, made similar arguments in his History of Civilization in France. His points can be summarized as follows: (1) After stating that civilization is a term deeply related to development and progress, he notes that civilization has two aspects: social relations and the inner life of the individual. (2) What civilization emphasizes is the freedom that Europe produced out of diversity and struggle. (3) The superiority of France over the rest of Europe. And (4) a national character that guarantees diversity and integrates it. This view of civilization also permeates the thinking of Fukuzawa Yukichi mentioned at the beginning (Maruyama 1986).
The deepening of philosophical inquiry into culture in Germany in the latter half of the 18th century is thought to be due to these underlying circumstances. The work of Germany's leading philosophers can be said to be an extension of this.
Among them, Johann Gottlieb Fichte (1762–1814) and Georg Wilhelm Friedrich Hegel (1770–1831) linked the German people not with civilization, but precisely with culture. As a result, the concept of culture gradually became strongly colored with nationalism, emphasizing one's own people. Originally, culture is what people who love their land and live in a region build by processing nature, based on their natural environment.
It could be said that it was, in a sense, natural for them to hold a sense of attachment and reverence for the land that nurtured that culture. The fundamental reason culture came into sharp conflict with civilization, which has a global scope and is oriented toward all of humanity, lies here.2
Tracing the historical context in which the concepts of civilization and culture were born, as described above, it becomes clear that even on the side of those who champion civilization, a similar kind of nativism seems to have unconsciously attached itself.
## 3. Civilization Aiming for Universality
Originally, civilization aimed for universal ideas, norms, and systems applicable to all humanity. In that sense, it is completely different from culture, which includes diverse, regionally limited rules and customs. In a city where people with diverse backgrounds gather, adhering to standardized discipline and adopting a uniform way of life was considered the mark of a civilized person. Culture is born from nature, and if there is a historical flow where it is further sublimated into civilization, then civilization can be redefined as follows. Civilization is a process of "civilizing" that aims for an ultimate, complete form.3
However, there were inherent limitations. Once the civilization of a certain time and place is established as an unshakable axis of evaluation and loses its flexibility, it transforms into a conventional, conservative ideology. When that happens, civilization, while outwardly advocating for universality, functions as a highly incomplete evaluation axis, far from being universal.
When such a biased evaluation axis is imposed, it is inevitable that those who do not belong to the “civilized” side will have their resentment amplified.
- 2 In the German-speaking world, there was also the idea of a world culture (Weltkultur). If culture can be conceived this broadly, it becomes a concept quite close to civilization. It is truly for the world citizen. However, culture was strongly associated with the nation, not the world citizen.
- 3 Alfred Weber (1868–1958) and Norbert Elias (1897-1990) described this with the term "civilizing process."
The German concept of culture must be understood precisely in this historical context. Furthermore, it was in a sense a natural course of events that ideologies that inherited this consciousness, such as Slavophilism (Nikolay Yakovlevich Danilevsky, 1822– 1885) and Pan-Asianism (Okakura Tenshin), emerged as a reaction against Western civilization.
Considering this, the issue is not a choice between civilization or culture; rather, the traces of humanity's pursuit of engaging with nature become both culture and civilization. Both civilization and culture, regardless of their duration, are fated to be created and then to disappear. When the power of culture, created by the primary processing of nature, is exhausted, the prospect of development into civilization, the secondary processing of nature, is lost. It is for this reason that scholars, particularly in the German-speaking world, placed extraordinary importance on culture rather than civilization.
When observing the history of civilization objectively, it traces a parabola of development, prosperity, decline, and collapse. Spengler valued civilization lowly because he believed it was merely something functioning out of inertia, having already lost its utility and reason for existence, and destined to disintegrate eventually.
These intellectual-historical trends concerning civilization and culture were events of the 1910s, sandwiching the First World War.
## 4. Definitions by American Cultural Anthropologists
Although there is no superficial connection, it is plausible that these trends were linked, beneath the surface, to the development of American cultural anthropology. This discipline was established in America by Franz Boas (1858–1942) and Alexander A. Goldenweiser (1880–1940). Boas was born in Germany, and Goldenweiser was an American immigrant born in the Ukraine. As part of their intellectual background, they harbored ambivalent feelings towards "civilization."
This likely became the driving force for creating a more scientific cultural anthropology, rather than the traditional evolutionist ethnology. As Stuart Chase (1888–1985) has noted, thanks in part to the efforts of Boas and others, the concept of culture came to be regarded as a cornerstone of the social sciences among anthropologists and sociologists by the 1930s.
However, this also led to the result of the word “culture” being used indiscriminately. Scholars discussing culture treated it in their own ways. To give a few examples, Wilson Dallam Wallis (1886–1970) saw culture as a functioning whole. Howard Paul Becker (1899–1960) saw it as fragments. Sanford Richard Winston (1897–1969) saw it as more than a collection of elements.
And Ruth Benedict (1887–1948) saw it as an integrated pattern of elements. However, what these individuals grasped was merely a hodgepodge; this was the assertion of Pitirim A. Sorokin (1889–1968), who would later become the first president of the International Society for the Comparative Study of Civilizations (ISCSC) (Yoshino 2009, pp. 106-107).
It was the American cultural anthropologist Philip Bagby (1918–1958) who attempted to resolve these issues: the emergence and conflation of the terms civilization and culture, and the adoption and subsequent indiscriminate use of the culture concept in cultural anthropology.
His definition, which contrasts civilization and culture, is frequently cited. To summarize its content, culture refers to human internal and external behavior that is not genetic (in other words, transmitted through education). In contrast, civilization is “the kind of culture found in the cities,” a concise definition. So, what is a city in this definition? It is an area where the majority of its members do not need to be engaged in primary industries (Bagby 1957, pp. 162-163).
This definition by Bagby was presented in a work written as a prolegomena to the comparative study of civilizations. Because it can be applied to various cases and has high versatility, it was later used by Arnold J. Toynbee (1889–1975) when he was asked for a definition of civilization (Toynbee 1967, p. 505).
Bagby originally entered the field of comparative study of civilizations from anthropology. He attempted to construct a comparative study of civilizations centered on the theories of civilizational and cultural change of Toynbee and Sorokin (Tsutsumi 1974). Through this, the study of comparative civilizations became a synthesis of the philosophy of history, history, cultural anthropology, and sociology. It was Itō Shuntarō who, while inheriting this legacy, wove in his own unique insights to present a theory of the structure and fluctuation of civilization and culture.4
## 5. The Structure and Fluctuation of Civilization
Civilization is a theme that has been addressed in interdisciplinary fields such as history, philosophy, sociology, and anthropology. However, as individual case studies of civilizations accumulate, a demand inevitably arises at some point to synthesize them. The comparative study of civilizations is an academic discipline that emerged to meet such a demand.
4 Of course, before Itō, there were numerous other Japanese theorists of civilization, as mentioned in the introduction. Also, during the 1970s when the Japan Society for the Comparative Study of Civilizations was being conceived, there was an accumulation of work from predecessors such as Kamiyama Shirō (1919-1996), Ueyama Shumpei (1921-2012), Kamikawa Masahiko (1929-2009), and Yajima Kyōshirō (1929-).
To synthesize the study of civilizations, it is first essential to redefine civilization itself as the object of study. In the comparative study of civilizations, based on the discussions mentioned above, a conceptual definition of the structure and transformation of civilization as the object of study must be established. In the early days of the comparative study of civilizations worldwide, this was precisely the task that Yamamoto Shin (1913–1980) attempted to undertake (Yamamoto 1961). It can be said that Japanese comparative study of civilizations lies on an extension of this work.
Having considered the conceptual history of civilization in both the world and Japan, as well as the research trends in the comparative study of civilizations, I would like to conclude by taking up the discussion of the structure and transformation of civilization by Ito Shuntaro to synthesize these elements. As is widely known, Ito was the first president of the Japan Society for the Comparative Study of Civilizations and the eighth president of the International Society for the Comparative Study of Civilizations.
A key feature of Ito's theory of comparative civilization is that he clarified the inclusive structure of civilization and culture and further developed his theory of the transformation of civilization, known as the famous “theory of five great revolutions.”
The elements that characterize civilization according to Ito are listed as follows. Civilization is a comprehensive term for a vast geographical area, historical tradition, a refined system of life (styles of food, clothing, and shelter), advanced social institutions (political and legal systems), economic organization (exchange systems), collective technology (various infrastructures), the organization of intellectual, spiritual, and aesthetic elements (science, religion, art, etc.), and the integrated structure of all these (Ito 1997, pp. 12–13). At the core of such a civilization lies culture. The content of culture is defined as the unique ethos, forms of ideas, and value sentiments of a group.
This can be illustrated as shown in Figure 1. In other words, if civilization is the hardware, then culture is the software (Ito 1997, pp. 8–9).
That said, some may get the impression that Ito's analogy of civilization as hardware and culture as software pulls us back toward the aforementioned simplistic interpretation of material civilization versus spiritual culture. However, culture as software is embedded in the core of civilization as hardware. Moreover, in this author's interpretation, there is no distinct line drawn between civilization and culture; rather, they exist on a gradient. Therefore, both civilization and culture contain a mixture of both spiritual and material elements.
Figure 1: The Relationship Between Culture, Civilizations, and World Civilization According to Ito Shuntaro
What is more important, as shown in Figure 1, is that civilization is not singular. Multiple civilizations are in a state of constant interaction. They undergo mutual contact and transference, at times passing through periods of friction, and they repeatedly readjust themselves toward the construction of larger civilizations. Through this mutual influence, “civilizational interaction spheres” such as the Mediterranean, Silk Road, and Indian Ocean spheres are formed. Ito's view of civilization was that beyond these lies World Civilization as an ideal.
The theory of five great revolutions is what historically develops this structural theory of interacting civilizations. It can be evaluated as a synthesis of the etymological exploration and historical usage of the terms “civilization” and “culture”. The theory can be described as follows in relation to civilization and culture.
For animals, nature is at times a blessing from the heavens, and at other times a merciless obstacle. The fortunate animal that succeeded in fully receiving nature's blessings while also removing its obstacles is the unique creature called “humanity.” This is the origin of the first, the Human Revolution.
By mastering tools and language, humanity developed culture, eventually bringing about the Agricultural Revolution. Having undergone this second revolution, humans succeeded in turning the natural environment into an ally by confronting and processing it. Humanity achieved a stable way of life. Having developed agriculture and secured a stable life, humanity created more convenient and safer cities. As Philip Bagby stated that civilization is “the kind of culture found in the cities,” so civilization arises because of the Urban Revolution.
To put it more generally, culture is what was created when humanity modified nature, and civilization is what emerged when this culture was more universally refined within cities. Cities develop supported by the surplus from rural villages. The urban citizens who gained leisure began to seek a way to “live well,” not just to “survive.”
It was then that the Spiritual Revolution occurred. In ancient Israel, Greece, China, and India, philosophical inquiry that transcended mere survival emerged contemporaneously. The gist of Ito's theory of five great revolutions is that after this revolution, the fifth, the Scientific Revolution, was born, centered in the West.
In Ito's vision for the comparative study of civilizations, an “Environmental Revolution” was to follow the five great revolutions (Ito 1997, p. 24). He also established “Cosmic Correlation” as his ultimate subject of inquiry (Ito 2022, pp. 297– 310). Unfortunately, due to Ito's passing, we were unable to witness the concrete development of the “Environmental Revolution” or the “Cosmic Correlation.” The task of elucidating “Cosmic Correlation” — the “bond of co-existence” between people, and between people and nature — and presenting and constructing the civilization that lies beyond it remains a challenge for our field of comparative study of civilizations.
## 6. Conclusion
This paper began with the questions: “What kind of academic discipline is the comparative study of civilizations?” and “What, fundamentally, is civilization?” As a clue toward answering these, it first reviewed the etymology and usage of the terms “civilization” and “culture,” and further referred to developments within cultural anthropology.
As a result, it has become clear that both concepts — civilization and culture — possess material and spiritual dimensions, and that they have continued to exist from antiquity to the present day. Furthermore, it was confirmed that the term “civilization” can be used in both the singular, as in “world civilization,” and the plural, as in “civilizations,” and that these two usages must be clearly distinguished.
By appropriately differentiating between civilization and culture in this way, it becomes possible to break the vicious cycle of definitional confusion and misuse within civilizational studies.
Below, I will summarize the conclusions drawn in this paper and offer some closing reflections.
First, civilization and culture form a continuum. Human beings, emerging from the natural world of ancient times, created culture, and eventually, through the formation of cities, developed cultural products into civilization. This process includes both
material and spiritual elements. Moreover, any given civilization, no matter how advanced, is merely a provisional point of arrival at the present moment and must inevitably undergo further change and renewal.
Second, civilization is not singular. Historically and geographically, countless interrelated civilizations have existed. By connecting with one another, these civilizations have formed zones of civilizational exchange and have built materially and spiritually rich societies. Civilization in this sense must be expressed in the plural.
Third, the direction in which civilization should progress lies in the ideal of a “world civilization” to be realized in the distant future. As an ideal in which humans can coexist with one another and with nature, world civilization should, therefore, be expressed in the singular.
Of course, the path toward a world civilization is neither straightforward nor smooth. Conflicts such as war, revolution, and civil strife are ever-present, and at times, forces like nationalism and ethnocentrism even cause regression in the opposite direction from world civilization.
Therefore, comparative civilizational studies must be a discipline that squarely confronts the frictions between nature, culture, and civilization, and seeks ways to mediate and resolve them. Only by overcoming such challenges one by one can comparative civilizational studies truly contribute to the realization of world civilization, by striving for harmony and coexistence among nature, culture, and civilization.
In closing, I would like to offer some reflections on the future. I intend to further develop a framework of comparative civilizational studies grounded in the concept of “Cosmic Correlation” — the bond of coexistence between people and between humans and nature — that Itō left unfinished. This is undoubtedly a formidable task, but I believe that clues can be found in the works of the founders of comparative civilizational studies.
Sorokin's idea of an “integral system” — a unifying structure of meanings, values, and norms underlying culture — and Toynbee's presentation of the ideal of a new civilization through “higher religion” were both proposed at the inception of comparative civilizational thought. Although Itō, Sorokin, and Toynbee articulated their views independently, their insights into civilization share significant commonalities.
Drawing upon their perspectives may enable us to reconstruct comparative civilizational studies for a new era.

## An Academic Civilization?
Inbar Gotlib-Rivkin Faculty of Education, Tel-Hai University
Uri Cohen School of Education, Tel Aviv University inbargotlib@gmail.com Abstract
Can institutions of higher education be defined as constituting a civilization that has emerged from modern civilization? This theoretical study defines a new concept of academic civilization and demonstrates how prominent characteristics that define the collective identity of modern civilization are manifested in institutions of higher education. The study focuses on six specific characteristics: expansion, flexibility and diversification, center-periphery relations, academic culture, autonomy and academic freedom, and the monopolization of professional training and the professional middle class.
The conceptual analysis presented here provides a significant addition to sociological discourse, offering a refined conceptualization of civilizations. The goal is to expand academic debates from the 1930s and the 1960s concerning the concept of civilization and its relation to institutions of higher education. The importance of arguing for the existence of an academic civilization is that it defines a modern collective identity, one whose global expansion processes may generate similar cultural, social, political, linguistic, and religious characteristics throughout the world.
Keywords: academic civilization; modern civilization; higher education; research university; academic culture
## Introduction
The purpose of this paper is to demonstrate that institutions of higher education may be considered a civilization. The expansion of institutions of higher education around the world, mainly with the establishment of research universities, has provided them with many of the characteristics of a unique and autonomous civilization. This is a civilization that has developed mainly after the Second World War. During this period, the number of institutions of higher education has increased exponentially (Wittrock, 2014). In addition, many new fields of studies have opened, resulting in a significant diversification of universities and colleges (Rivlin & O'Neill, 1970; Wittrock, 2019). Here we coin the term academic civilization to emphasize a modern collective identity and the process of its global expansion.
This civilization has become a clear and dominant hallmark of modernity and may be resulting in a set of similar cultural, social, political, linguistic, and religious characteristics worldwide.
It is evident that professional training institutions and research universities have shaped modern civilization. Arguing for the values of the university as a core institution of modern civilization makes it possible to regard these institutions of higher education as maintaining a civilizational pattern, one termed academic civilization in this paper. Referring to activities of institutions of higher education as constituting a civilization enables us to learn about, and compare, the patterns that exist in these institutions in various places around the world and about networks of connections and convergence processes that now characterize civilizations. This point of view also enables us to learn about the direction in which such an institutional configuration develops and to study its impact on modern society.
The aim here is to present the existing body of knowledge about modern civilization while introducing a new perspective on the role played by the institutions of higher education as an independent civilization, one that has developed from modern civilization, and which operates together with other civilizations. It is hoped that this research may have an impact on economics, society, and policy, with the potential for a global impact.
In a sense this is a pioneering work as it seeks to introduce a new concept that relates to certain familiar characteristics of modern civilization and to infuse it with new meaning. To the best of our knowledge, the concept of academic civilization has not been referred to in literature, hence the novelty of this research.
Furthermore, the growth of the academic civilization may affect a variety of areas, such as professional training, social prestige, job security, economic and social mobility, the hierarchical division between countries, and may preserve the power relations between the central and peripheral regions. Defining academia as a civilization and understanding its features may facilitate an understanding of how this particular civilization may develop worldwide.
The word “civilization” is frequently defined by the possession of common objective characteristics, such as culture, language, history, religion, customs, and institutions, and by collective human identity. Collective identity consists of characteristics such as origin, race, religion, language, history, values, customs, institutions, and similar social structures (Huntington, 1993, 1996). At the end of the nineteenth century, the term civilization referred to the historical process of secular human development. Civilization is now frequently considered a universal process.
Thus, in the modern era, the notion of civilization has been contrasted with savagery or barbarism (Williams, 2015). A civilization is more than simply a set of collective norms and values of a particular group of any size and found anywhere in the world (Wallerstein, 1978).
Continuous changes of cultural, institutional, and ideological patterns in the present age indicate that the history of modernity appears to be one of continuous development and consolidation; this is an era in which cultures and institutional patterns are rebuilt and where different concepts of collective identity have been developing (Eisenstadt, 2003, 2010).
The expansion of modern civilization has created a tendency to develop universal worldwide institutional and symbolic frameworks and systems. Indeed, it has been argued that modern civilization first arose in Europe and later expanded globally; it thus created a series of international frameworks or systems, each of which rests upon some of the basic assumptions of this civilization and is rooted in one of its basic institutional dimensions (Eisenstadt, 2003, 2010; Wittrock, 2000, 2001). Modernization produces a certain uniform social and political structure across different countries and cultures according to which all countries ultimately act (Fukuyama, 2020).
## The Characteristics of Modern Civilization
Modernity is a continually developing process in which distinct institutional patterns are created (Eisenstadt, 2001). In the modern era, actions may have global effects (Bauman, 1998b). The development of modern civilization was made possible thanks to the growth of trading networks, trends of industrialization, urbanization, the increase in literacy, social and economic mobility, technological development, globalization processes, and the increasing of scientific knowledge (Bhambra, 2007; Huntington,
- 1996). Thus, a situation in one place could impact life anywhere on the globe (Bauman, 2001). Modernity has several characteristics, including openness, flexibility, and diversification. This includes the development of new institutional and cultural characteristics and configurations. Modernity enables groups worldwide to belong to broad, trans-local communities that change frequently (Eisenstadt, 2010).
The modern era has also promoted as its most important social value the idea of freedom in general, a person’s freedom of choice, as well as that of human autonomy (Bauman, 2012; Weber, 1947). The demand for national independence and sovereignty is one manifestation of the desire for self-determination and freedom (Fukuyama, 2020). The transition to a national economy prepared the ground for further human freedom and severed the boundaries imposed on man by external and internal authorities. These economic changes allowed the middle class to emerge in modern civilization. It was the middle class that disseminated the intellectual inheritance of antiquity (Weber, 1950).
Modern societies have also adopted tendencies to erase the symbolic differences between the center and the periphery. They are characterized by structural features of the social centers, including the patterns of participation in these centers and access to them (Eisenstadt, 1980). Over time, territorial borders have opened and expanded, thus opening the world. As a result, additional opportunities for academic studies, education, employment, and business began to emerge (Bauman, 1998a).
Another characteristic of modern civilization is modern culture, which includes various ideological and institutional assumptions. Modern societies have developed a pattern of cultural unification (Eisenstadt, 2010). Modernity constitutes the basis for the first world culture (Wittrock, 2000, 2001). Moreover, in modern times, the concept of culture includes universal qualities that apply to all human beings (Bauman, 1999).
In the modern era, the importance of education has been increasingly emphasized, and universities are called to fulfill the role of educators, which has meant passing on the religious and classical culture of the Western world from generation to generation. This intellectual revolution created a change in the modern era, especially in the twentieth century; young students were increasingly expected to study at institutions of higher education as part of a necessary training track and as a characteristic of the middle class (Altbach, 2016; Cohen, 2014; Leprevost, 2020; Lippmann, 1941). This revolution directly affected the emergence and expansion of universities (Wittrock, 2001). As a result, the university has become a core institution in modern society (Krucken et al., 2007; Meyer et al., 2007; Shen & Tian, 2012).
In the modern era, there has been a growing need for many highly qualified and educated workers, managers, technicians, and intellectuals. Therefore, the need for general education and open access to higher education and specialized training became fundamental in countries that wished to advance economically (Fukuyama, 2020). Another characteristic of the modern era was that of accelerated social and economic mobility (Altbach, 2016; Bauman, 1999). In addition, collective identity has been redefined in the global context; the new collective identity has been influenced by some of the universal elements of modernity, while maintaining some aspects of traditional and local identities (Eisenstadt, 2001,2003).
The expansion of modern civilization was made possible thanks to technological advancement and the dynamics of economic and political forces. The scope, intensity, and pace of the changes that have accompanied modern developments have led to the rebuilding of global institutional, cultural, and ideological frameworks and systems (Eisenstadt, 2010).
The changes that occurred in academic institutions in the late twentieth and early twentieth-first centuries were more extensive in that the changes were global and affected many more institutions and a larger population.
For example, the research university that developed in the nineteenth century, first in Germany and then elsewhere, fundamentally changed the nature of universities all over the world (Altbach et al., 2009).
## The Characteristics of Academic Civilization
The modern era, that is, as of the nineteenth century, is the starting point for the formation of the type of academic institution known today. Over time, universities have therefore become important social institutions (Wittrock, 2000, 2001). In modern times, institutions of higher education also have become engines of scientific progress. The purpose of the modern university has been to spread intellectual knowledge, or in other words, to bring light to a place where darkness reigned, to convert prejudice into the scientific knowledge of nature (Bloom, 1987).
## Expansion: Scope and Growth
A significant increase in the number of institutions of higher education occurred after the Second World War (Eisenstadt, 2010), also resulting in a transition to mass higher education (Altbach, 2016; Cohen, 2014; Gori-Rosenblit, 2005; Rivlin & O'Neill, 1970; Wittrock, 2019). The period from the nineteenth century to the turn of the twentieth century was a period of changes that had global consequences. During this period, research became the main mission of many universities, and universities became key institutions of modern countries and economies (Wittrock, 2014).
In the second half of the nineteenth century, universities that imitated the German model emphasizing scientific expertise and pure applied research were founded. The research university originating in Berlin served as a model for leading universities, among them Cornell University, The University of Chicago, Stanford University, Harvard University, and Columbia University. In the second half of the twentieth century, there was a move away from elitism in education and a shift to mass systems of higher education (Altbach, 2016; Cohen, 2014; Rivlin & O'Neill, 1970; Wittrock, 2014, 2019).
As of the second half of the twentieth century, there was an increase in the participation rate in higher education due to the increase in the demand for education in the labor market, which resulted in an increase in the number of people enrolled in graduate studies. Also, the value of a high school certificate decreased as more people had one (Wallerstein et al., 2013). The increase in the prevalence of academic certificates was due to the increase in the demand for qualified employees with university degrees (Bourdieu, 1984).
In the 1960s and 1970s, which were years of transition from elite education to mass higher education, science and higher education were the focus of concern for policymakers in the United States and Western European countries.
Their concern was due to the growing importance of research, which heralded the development of research-oriented academic institutions. Since research-oriented institutions were perceived as a source of prosperity, prestige, and power, this in turn ensured the continued expansion of institutions of higher education around the world. In these two decades, the number of institutions of higher education in Western Europe and North America tripled within a period of less than 15 years (Wittrock, 2014, 2019).
The growth in the number of institutions of higher education was presented as a transition from institutions belonging to the elite to institutions belonging to the masses, which finally led to universal higher education. Higher education has become a social function that fulfills distinct roles. Universities became gateways to the world of modernity when they expanded in size and numbers and became linked to innovation processes (Wittrock, 2014, 2019).
In 1900, approximately 500,000 students were enrolled in institutions of higher education worldwide, representing a tiny fraction of one percent of the age group of 18 to 23 (Schofer & Meyer, 2005). After the Second World War, there was a dramatic growth in the number of enrollments, due to progress in science, technology, and industrialization, which led to an increase in production and an improvement in the standard of living. These factors have encouraged growth in participation in higher education mainly in the United States and Europe (Schofer & Meyer, 2005; Wallerstein et al., 2013). Furthermore, in the 1970s, the number of women entering institutions of higher education increased. Their enrollment percentages eventually overtook those of men. By 2004, women constituted most of the university enrollments (Altbach, 1999; Freeman, 2010).
In 1970, 32.6 million students were enrolled in institutions of higher education worldwide compared to approximately 100 million students in the year 2000, the latter representing approximately twenty percent of the age group 18–23 worldwide. The number of enrollments constitutes an increase of approximately 206% (Schofer & Meyer, 2005; Wallerstein et al., 2013). It is estimated that there will be 377.4 million students by the year 2030, 471.4 million students by the year 2035, and 594.1 million students by the year 2040 (Wallerstein et al., 2013).
As noted above, the growth in higher education was made possible because it became a system that was open to everyone, compared to the past, when only the elites were given a chance to attend institutions of higher education (Trow, 2006).
The growth in the number of institutions of higher education and the growth in the number of people seeking education led to the development of modern civilization (Huntington, 1996). From the second half of the twentieth century, there has been a worldwide expansion of modern civilization throughout the world (Eisenstadt, 2003).
A parallel phenomenon is the expansion of higher education. In the 1960s, the growing demand for graduates of higher education occurred due to the development of the postwar economy and of the welfare state, resulting in an increase of the rate of students graduating from secondary education. The growing demand for higher education has also led to a marked increase in the number and types of institutions of higher education around the world (OECD, 2019, 2020). A rapid expansion also occurred in the range of fields of study (Meyer et al., 2007).
## Flexibility and Diversification: Expansion into Multiple Fields of Study
The growth in enrollment created demand for diverse academic systems that serve unique needs and respond to new demands from the environment (Altbach et al., 2009). In the nineteenth century, there was rapid development in the academic fields of study taught at universities (Bourdieu, 1984). The four main fields of study in the early years of the university were theology, philosophy, law, and medicine. An example of expansion in fields of study is that only three history courses were offered at Harvard University in 1853. As of 147 years later – in the year 2000 – the university offered 229 history courses (Frank & Meyer, 2007a, 2007b).
In the twentieth century, universities expanded not only in the number of students and faculty but also in the range of study programs they offered. The modern era led universities to respond to changing needs and offer new fields of study, such as environmental policy, regional planning, business administration, gerontology, and real estate. Also, university studies became much more professional in the scope of their curricula and much more practical in their orientation. Moreover, fields such as agriculture, architecture, medicine, engineering, law, and management experienced a revival and the number of people enrolled in them was on the rise (Altbach, 2016; Shapiro, 2000).
The university has been a central institution in modern society for the past two hundred years. Partly because of its adaptation to a frequently changing environment, the expansion of the university has allowed it to offer more areas of training while maintaining mutual relations between the university itself and society. Thus, universities have created some new fields of study, and closed departments in fields that are no longer in demand (Frank & Meyer, 2007a, 2007b). The leading institutions have undergone changes over the years that have been reflected in the way they are oriented so that they can equip students with tools that will allow them to cope with the changes taking place in the environment (Bourdieu, 1984; Leprevost, 2020).
The Covid-19 pandemic underscored the flexibility of institutions of higher education, particularly in their rapid adoption of digital technologies to facilitate online course delivery.
Despite initial challenges – such as limited technical and financial resources and insufficient technological proficiency among faculty – many institutions exhibited remarkable resilience. Globally, universities and colleges swiftly adjusted to the new educational landscape, implementing online programs to ensure instructional continuity (Srinivasan et al., 2021). Moreover, the pandemic served as a pivotal moment to reconsider pedagogical approaches, integrate innovative technologies, and reform traditional educational models to better prepare students for the evolving dynamics of global learning (Huang et al., 2020).
The integration of artificial intelligence (AI) technologies within institutions of higher education exemplifies their enhanced capacity for flexibility in response to evolving technological paradigms. In the twentieth-first century, AI has become increasingly pervasive, contributing to advancements across diverse domains of human activity. Its adoption in higher education has accelerated in recent years, driven by the emergence of tools that support students, faculty, and administrative personnel.
These tools facilitate a range of functions, including the retrieval and translation of academic literature; instructional innovations that align with the evolving skillsets demanded in future labor markets; personalized learning through adaptive teaching methodologies; as well as the optimization of enrollment procedures, student advisement, and career guidance (Crompton & Burke, 2023; Hannan & Liu, 2023; Hornberger et al., 2023). Consequently, flexibility in higher education necessitates ongoing recalibration of institutional practices in response to shifting global and local conditions.
## Center-Periphery Relations
As in any field, in academia too, some geographic areas are academically dominant while others are more marginal (Bourdieu, 1984). Modern civilization distinguishes between the core and the periphery. The core refers to areas where diverse production is concentrated with high profit, advanced technology, and high wages. According to Wallerstein (1976), Western countries belong to the core. On the other hand, the peripheral countries are the areas with a low gross domestic product (GDP), the wages are low, technology is less developed, and production is less diverse. In between are semi-peripheral countries, which can play a dual role as both core and periphery (Wallerstein, 1976).
The core, which this study refers to as the center or central area, is also the center of symbols, values, and beliefs that dominate society. The center in any country structures activities and roles within a network of institutions. For example, the university system consists of a network of organizations connected through shared power, personnel, interpersonal relationships, contracts, identities, and a sense of affinity (Shils, 1975).
Moreover, in each social institution, there are elites that can be composed of individuals or organized groups. Each of the elite members makes decisions, sometimes in consultation with other elites, regarding the maintenance of the organization, controlling the behavior of the members, and serving its goals. Intellectual elites belonging to the university system propagate certain values that dominate intellectual activities (Shils, 1975).
In the academic field, the access and distribution of knowledge both are concentrated in the center and are transmitted to the rest of the world mainly by means of the English language. For example, major publications of scientific materials, such as prestigious academic journals, are mainly located in the central countries and are published in English. Almost without exception, central universities are found in the central countries, which are countries with a high per capita income, an elevated level of technological development, significant academic traditions, and the infrastructure of intellectual life. In these countries, English is used as the language for virtually all academic communication, such as conferences and publication of research (Altbach, 1981).
Since the beginning of their establishment in the Middle Ages, universities have had a global identity. This is an essential characteristic of the idea of the university (Altbach, 2016; Gori-Rosenblit, 2005). The distinction between center and periphery in the context of institutions of higher education generally means that the institutions defined as central are research-oriented and operate as part of a global knowledge system, while the peripheral institutions create relatively less new knowledge and often copy their mode of operation from other successful academic institutions in the world, usually those belonging to the center. For example, universities in developing nations defined as peripheral often copy their operating patterns from Western models. However, these peripheral universities generate little original knowledge (Altbach, 1981).
By contrast, most of the scientific research in the world is conducted in Western countries because these countries provide appropriate funds and facilities for research such as laboratories and libraries. The leading or central universities are considered as such usually due to their research capacities and facilities and their reputation of excellence.
In addition, those universities classified as leading institutions are usually those in which the English language is the main language of instruction and research publication. Moreover, they have a large variety of research fields and study programs and are the recipients of many important research grants funded by governments and private organizations and corporations (Altbach, 1981, 2016; Altbach et al., 2009).
In contrast, the peripheral universities, for the most part disseminate knowledge, mainly by training students and through the replication of research developed in central universities. They also depend on the central institutions for the development of innovation and guidance. The peripheral institutions are considered less prestigious, and the teaching and research staff receive considerably lower remuneration. Peripheral institutions have less respectable traditions, tend to be in poor or peripheral countries, and employ less skilled researchers and academic staff (Altbach, 1981).
The distinction between center and periphery in the field of higher education also manifests itself in the number of patent applications submitted by institutions of higher education, since a ranking of universities with patents demonstrates dominance (Wittrock, 2014), and there is evidence that most patents are of Western origin (Altbach, 2016). Based on the number of applications submitted for international patents in 2020, the highest-ranking universities are mainly universities from the United States (Szmigiera, 2021).
The distinction between center and periphery also depends on the universities' position in the global ranking tables over the years. The ranking of universities and other institutions of higher education is one of the manifestations of the enormous diversity of the higher education system. The importance of the ranking of academic institutions based on the quality and resources of the institutions has increased dramatically over the last two decades in Europe, Asia, and other parts of the world (Wittrock, 2014, 2019). Often, the centrality of universities is measured according to the ranking in the global index (Altbach, 2016).
The four leading ranking institutions in the world are Times Higher Education (THE), Quacquarelli Symonds, CWTS known as Leiden Ranking, and ARWU known as Shanghai Ranking. In the period between 2003 and 2020, American universities have led in all the ranking tables. Moreover, the first 25 places are consistently occupied by universities from the United States. The leading universities in Great Britain (Oxford and Cambridge) also appear in prominent positions; that is, the top-ranking universities are all in Western countries. This dominance is reinforced by the fact that approximately 85% of the twenty leading universities in the world in the ranking tables in the last two decades have been universities that represent Western civilization (Leprevost, 2020). This pattern of Western universities occupying leading positions in global rankings continued in 2024.
Another characteristic that demonstrates the center-periphery relations applies to the ranking of scientific journals based on their impact factor. The most prominent scientific journals are published in Western countries. Western academic journals are considered the standard of excellence and are the most respected sources of knowledge. Modern technologies, such as computerized databases, are likely to be controlled by institutions located in the West (Altbach, 2016).
In other words, another characteristic that distinguishes the center from the periphery is scientific productivity, which is usually measured through data on publications in scientific journals by researchers belonging to a certain university (Larsen, 2011).
The concentration of research, resources, and prestige in the major universities renders these universities key institutions. These universities are also a powerful engine of globalization on a worldwide scale. Every country wants strong research universities. A central research university symbolizes the ability to produce global knowledge, resources, and funds (Altbach, 2016; Marginson & van der Wende, 2007). A central research university is necessary for successful societies (Fukuyama, 2020). This hierarchical division helps preserve the power structure between the central and peripheral regions and is one of the characteristics of modern civilization. It also facilitates an understanding of the development and establishment of the new civilization, namely, academic civilization.
The distinction between central and peripheral institutions of higher education has gradually become blurred in the modern era because academic institutions operate as a flexible network scattered all over the world and do not depend on one center that controls and supervises their activities. This network operates as a global network and produces audiences with a common set of interests that maintain regular and ongoing relationships (Luke, 2005; Schofer & Meyer, 2005; Wittrock, 2019).
Universities around the world thus share a common culture and have converged around the core countries where central institutions of higher education are located (Altbach, 1991; Inkeles, 1981). Since this convergence process includes institutional models and norms, universities around the world can be said to share a common culture (Altbach, 1991, 2016).
## Academic Culture
Academic culture may be defined as a network of relationships and explicit beliefs about academic practices of teaching, learning, and research, and about the social significance of these practices. Academic culture includes practices, institutions, and social relations, along with beliefs (Ringer, 1992).
Bauman's book Globalization: The Human Consequences implies that academic culture is not a culture in a particular geographical place; rather, it is a culture that can be said to apply to any university, regardless of where it is located on the globe. Thus, for example, a person who comes to a university in a foreign country knows what to expect. This means that even foreign universities are not really foreign (Bauman, 1998a).
The cultural elements of an academic institution are the patterns of conduct, the philosophy of education, conceptions of teaching and learning, the nature of the educational or academic community, and the commitment to students (Peterson & Spencer, 1990). Modern societies have recognized the importance of education, especially the role of the university as the core of the education system through which culture is transmitted (Pound, 1922).
The culture of institutions of higher education includes myths, symbols, and rituals that preserve the uniqueness of collective academic identity. Myths are a result of the unique history of an academic institution embodied in written documents, memories, legends, and the physical attributes of the place. Myths help anchor what was previously present and provide meaning to the social action patterns of academic life (Dill, 1982; Shen & Tian, 2012).
Institutions of higher education have a culture with their own symbols of status and authority in the form of, for example, professional awards, research grants, and publications and ritualistic behavior at professional meetings (Dill, 1982). Symbols may be in the form of objects that were previously used as visible signs of power and authority. In the Middle Ages, these objects were, inter alia, costumes, such as wands, necklaces, rings, robes, and hats of distinct colors that symbolized different faculties and distinguished them from others. Other symbols were seals, cups, keys, and notebooks (Gieysztor, 1992).
Finally, rituals are a sequence of patterns of social activity that express meaning (Dill, 1982). In addition, even though universities are in a distinct cultural environment, they share a common ethos – or tradition and beliefs – that allows them to cooperate and bridge cultural gaps (Wittrock, 2019). Also, institutions of higher education have detailed regulations, guidelines, procedures, and lists of “best practices” (Boulton & Lucas, 2011).
Academic culture creates socialization processes during which members acquire the values, attitudes, norms, knowledge, and skills required in each society (Mendoza, 2008). In the modern age, the university thus has become a core social institution. The culture of academic institutions plays a significant role in spreading knowledge and innovative ideas, and creating new cultures (Altbach, 2016; Shen & Tian, 2012), hence the importance of viewing institutions of higher education as an academic civilization.
Civilizations are characterized by language that facilitates the creation of a collective identity (e.g., Eisenstadt, 2002; Huntington, 1993). In Western universities, the language of instruction has become a universal language that allows a diverse population to acquire an education (Altbach, 2016; Huntington, 1996).
Most ancient academic institutions were built around a uniform language for teaching and learning via textbooks and scientific research to communicate with colleagues around the world.
In the twentieth and twenty-first centuries, English has become the main scientific language. English currently dominates the research literature in higher education and is also the key language of academic communication. In addition, most research journals are published in English, and researchers in many countries in non-Englishspeaking countries are now encouraged to write in English for their research to be published in prominent international journals (Altbach, 1981, 2014, 2016).
In summary, as a civilization, academic civilization also has a common culture and language. The university is a cultural institution that also plays a role in creating culture (Altbach, 2016). The modern global culture of knowledge spreads norms based on abstract and universal concepts (Sullivan, 2012). Universities constitute a melting pot, which facilitates the unification of customs that are adopted by students regardless of their identity outside the institution (Bloom, 1987).
## Autonomy and Academic Freedom
The tradition of university autonomy is one of the oldest traditions, dating back to the Middle Ages. The separation between religion and state in the modern era has strengthened the autonomy of universities and the freedom of teaching staff in colleges and universities. This separation reduced the ability of the religious community to penetrate the affairs of universities, especially those institutions established, controlled, or financially supported by religious bodies. The concept of autonomy refers to the universities’ ability to manage their internal affairs as they wish. The autonomy of universities was firmly established during the nineteenth century in liberal democracies and – within the limits allowed by the constitutional and political traditions of these democracies – reached its peak in the first half of the twentieth century (Shils, 1997).
However, throughout history, the autonomy of universities was limited since universities depended on external financial support (Shils, 1997). Since the Middle Ages, universities have been subject to various forms of external supervision (Altbach, 2008). In contrast, in the University of Paris, for example, professors were placed at the center of the institution and enacted autonomy as an important part of the academic ethos (Altbach, 1991).
In addition to the notion of the autonomy of the university as an institution, another important concept pertaining to the university was that of academic freedom. Academic freedom was first mentioned in the United States in 1885 and in the Oxford English Dictionary in 1901 (Pak, 2007).
Academic freedom means the freedom of the members of the academic community to perform the duties of teaching, learning, and engaging in research and to carry out these duties fearlessly (Fuchs, 1963). The notion of academic freedom has also included the freedom of expression outside the university itself (Bloom, 1987).
With the establishment of the research university in Germany, the notion of academic freedom began to enter the academic discourse. Wilhelm von Humboldt's memorandum titled “The State of our Learned Institutions” – based on which the University of Berlin was founded (Shils, 1997) – introduced the concepts Lehrfreiheit (‘freedom of teaching’) and Lernfreiheit (‘freedom of learning’) (Pak, 2007). Lehrfreiheit meant the freedom of professors to teach in their classrooms and do research in their direct areas of expertise, while Lernfreiheit referred to the freedom of students to study whatever they wished to study (Altbach, 2001, 2009).
The memorandum included a reference to the ideas of combining research and teaching and of academic self-governance. Following this memorandum, academic freedom became an integral feature of universities in the nineteenth century. At the end of the nineteenth century and throughout the twentieth century, the principles of Humboldt's memorandum permeated American universities and from there spread throughout the modern world (Ostling, 2018; Shils, 1997). Humboldt University, which was established in Berlin, was the first modern research university and constituted a model that was imitated by other universities outside of Germany (Ostling, 2018). Modern universities have enjoyed almost complete autonomy from religious bodies (Shils, 1997). Universities were increasingly granted autonomy throughout the nineteenth century (Bourdieu, 1984).
In the modern era, the most important values promoted were freedom in general, and personal freedom of choice (Bauman, 2012; Weber, 1947). However, autonomy and academic freedom are not absolute values. Throughout various historical periods and in many different countries, both values have been often encroached upon by governments, employer organizations, or trade unions (Bloom, 1987).
For many years, civil and ecclesiastical authorities tried to limit teaching, research, and freedom of speech for members of the academic community. These limitations were reflected in reprimands and could even lead to dismissal and lawsuits (Altbach, 2001, 2007). One of the reasons that some states attempted to restrict academic freedom, especially in Germany in the 1930s, was the fact that the professors were state employees, and as a result, their loyalty was primarily to the state (Ben David & Zelochover, 1967).
Autonomy and academic freedom are at the core of the values on which the university was founded (Fish, 2014).
The foundations for the formation of the modern university rested upon the doctrines of rights based on freedom of expression and freedom of choice. In the modern era, universities have prospered because they have been seen as promulgating this set of social rights (Bloom, 1987). For their activity and the transmission of culture, institutions of higher education require institutional autonomy, which is important for scientific creation and for shaping the image of society (Eisenstadt, 1967). Autonomy and academic freedom are important values that allow the university to fulfill its role through teaching, research, and other services provided to society (Thorens, 1998). The Monopolization of Professional Training and the Professional Middle Class
The universities have retained a monopoly on intellectual life (Blue, 1987). In the modern era, an academic qualification is necessary for positions of power, authority, and prestige in society. Universities provide training in a growing number of specializations that are important to modern societies, and this has given them enormous social power (Altbach, 1991). Due to the occupational structure that is determined by the economy, the demand for more highly educated people has made higher education mandatory (Trow, 1972).
Higher education is one of the main channels for the global transmission of new knowledge. More specifically, higher education constitutes a global knowledge community in which new patterns of knowledge, certification, research alliances, and social and professional relationships are created (Luke, 2005). By increasing their size and number, universities have become gateways to modernity, linked to innovation processes and economic development. Moreover, since the universities strive to train the middle class, the university also plays an important social role in promoting social mobility (Cohen, 2014; Wittrock, 2014).
In the modern era, universities have played a significant role in preparing students for professional life and career paths (Lippmann, 1941; Wittrock, 2019). Certificates from institutions of higher education or advanced training courses are now prerequisites for desirable employment. Universities play a decisive role in shaping the identity of their graduates and in the process of strengthening the industrial and technological capabilities of nation-states. Universities may also provide these nation-states with professionally trained managerial and technical staff. In addition, universities serve as foci for cultural discourse and facilitate urbanism, industrialism, and modernity (Meyer et al., 2007; Wittrock, 2019). Universities are currently viewed as institutions that train young people in rational judgment and technical knowledge for public service (Shils,
- 1997).
Universities have also received more authority over training for social and professional roles. Almost all training for professions around the world now goes through the university, the institution that validates the knowledge learned.
Although training for positions was concentrated in universities as early as in the Middle Ages (Altbach, 1991), the expansion of institutions of higher education since the Second World War is rooted in the universities’ success in training students as well as in designing curricula to suit the changing demands of society. The universities’ continual awareness of social changes and new professional requirements has meant that universities now maintain an almost absolute monopoly on the constant expansion of professional training around the world (Frank & Meyer, 2007a, 2007b).
The expansion of institutions of higher education arose for several reasons:
-  Firstly, an academic degree is increasingly considered a necessity for economic success. Moreover, in the modern era, the skills provided through studies in institutions of higher education are required for complex technology-based positions (Altbach, 1999).
-  Secondly, in many countries, including some developing countries, a university degree is a requirement for any middle-class occupation. Higher education has been dominated by the middle classes for a century or more, and as the middle class has grown, universities have continued to develop and expand (Altbach, 1999).
-  Thirdly, the main purpose of institutions of higher education is to transmit and establish knowledge and to provide training for several key professions, namely, medicine, law, and theology. For centuries, universities have been the most important creators of new knowledge through research (Altbach, 1991).
-  Finally, in the modern era, universities are required to provide the technical skills needed for an increasing number of jobs and professions that require sophisticated knowledge and an education that improves students' thinking and communication skills. Universities provide the necessary education for skilled occupations and professions (Altbach, 2008).
For almost a century, universities have also been seen as instruments for social mobility – as a way for middle class individuals to obtain the skills they need to improve their income and social status (Altbach, 2008; Windolf, 2018).
## Conclusion and Implications
Modern civilization has developed mainly since the Second World War, when institutions of higher education began to expand both qualitatively and quantitatively. Modern civilization has shaped world culture through universities, especially those located in the West. This study has argued that professional training institutions and research universities have made it possible to shape modern civilization.
Further, since universities educate the population about the values of society and have become a core institution of modern civilization (Krucken et al., 2007; Meyer et al., 2007; Wittrock, 2000), institutions of higher education can be regarded as maintaining a civilizational pattern that may be termed academic civilization.
The concept of civilization, understood as a constellation of supranational values, provides a meaningful framework for analyzing the evolution and functioning of institutions of higher education worldwide – an expansive, universal social phenomenon through which most of the global youth population receives formal education.
Therefore, we argue here that institutions of higher education relate to the familiar characteristics of the concept of modern civilization, which we can accordingly imbue with new content.
This perspective extends the conventional concept of civilization, reframing it within a socio-international framework that diverges from previously established interpretations in scholarly literature. The importance of the term academic civilization is that it defines the modern collective identity, one whose global expansion processes may generate similar cultural, social, political, linguistic, and religious characteristics throughout the world.
The expansion of academic civilization may affect a variety of areas, such as professional training, social prestige, job security, economic and social mobility, a hierarchical division between countries, and an effort to preserve the power structure between the central and peripheral regions (Altbach, 1981, 2016; Cohen, 2014; Wittrock, 2019).
- As noted above, academic civilization developed inter alia because of the establishment and expansion of the modern research university that originated in Germany at the beginning of the nineteenth century. Since then, academic civilization has had considerable influence on the formation and preservation of modern collective identity, the development of modern capitalism, the nation-state, and the process of globalization.
This new civilization may shape the development of the middle class, which has become one of the pillars of modern society. In the modern era, the university has become a central institution of society, hence the importance of a new conceptualization of academic civilization.
The expansion of academic civilization, similarly to that of modern civilization, was made possible thanks to technological development, trading networks, industrialization, urbanization, and the acceleration of globalization processes during the twentieth century. Like modern civilization, academic civilization developed mainly in Western countries and from there spread throughout the world.
The expansion of academic civilization was facilitated by increased literacy, accessibility to higher education, and social mobility. In fact, we may even conclude that this civilization has become the most dominant hallmark of the concept of modernity.
Academic civilization is a flexible network, spread all over the world, and it does not depend on one center that controls and supervises the activities of academic institutions. This network operates as a global one, producing audiences with a set of common interests that maintain regular and ongoing interactions, and facilitating information transfer, planning, management, training, and supervision (Luke, 2005; Schofer & Meyer, 2005; Wittrock, 2019).
Another characteristic of academic civilization is that it is flexible. In the modern era, the universal flexibility of institutions of higher education has allowed them to deal with a frequently changing environment and to adapt to the evolving demands of society. Furthermore, the distinction between center and periphery has become obscured since geographical borders have opened to almost everyone. However, in some respects, the distinction between the center and periphery nevertheless applies to universities, for example, in the number of patents, their position in ranking tables, and scientific productivity.
Like the centrality of the culture to any civilization, academic culture is central to defining academic civilization. In the modern era, a pattern of cultural unification has developed which is manifested in institutions of higher education by the ability of people to observe academic culture, regardless of the culture from which they come. Another central characteristic that defines modern civilization and reflects academic civilization is that of autonomy. Autonomy and academic freedom are key values; in the modern era, human autonomy, or freedom in general, and freedom of choice in particular, hold the top place on the list of the values of society and its members.
Moreover, academic freedom has been an integral feature of universities since the nineteenth century. The university has become a core institution in modern society. In doing so it directs young people to a course of study as part of necessary training; thus, it puts them through a process of socialization. These key roles of institutions of higher education are due to the need for many highly qualified and educated employees, managers, technicians, and intellectuals, which has allowed universities to monopolize professional training.
These several characteristics pertain to internal aspects of universities, namely: expansion, flexibility and diversification, center-periphery relations, academic culture, autonomy and academic freedom, the monopolization of the universities of professional training and the gradual emergence of the professional middle class, such as attorneys, physicians, and accountants (see Appendix 1).
Viewing institutions of higher education as a civilization can testify to the nature of their development and their future influence on modern society.

African Studies is a conglomerate of multidisciplinary academic fields that include paleontology, history, literature, linguistics, philosophy, African Traditional Religion and theology, art, and Afrofuturism, as well as African Indigenous Knowledge Systems. This paper focuses on the latter, namely African Indigenous Knowledge Systems (AIKS), and African Studies, in general, from the viewpoint of two chatbots, namely, ChatGPT-4, and DeepSeek v.3. ## Introduction
Chat Generative Pre-Trained Transformer (Chat GPT), an innovation by OpenAI, was launched in November 2022, initiating a new phase of generative intelligence agents and conversational chatbots. These artificial intelligence models, Large Language Models (LLMs), and Large Reasoning Models (LRMs) associated with them, have the capability to respond to a wide range of topics and research queries (Lynch, 2025). The first chatbot, “Eliza” was created at MIT in 1966 by Joseph Weizenbaum; it was less versatile and was not originally intended to be a chatbot, according to Jeff Shrager (Killgrove 2025; Shrager 2024). Fast forward to January 27, 2025, to a chatbot named DeepSeek, which sent Wall Street investors reeling, with a loss of a trillion dollars when it was realized that a low-cost, generative intelligence agent from China could compete triumphantly, and unabashedly, with the likes of ChatGPT-4 (Li, 2025).
Whilst OpenAI, the parent company, is associated with at least half a trillion dollars of investment, DeepSeek allegedly costs about six million dollars. It is described as having distinct architecture, datasets, and algorithms, and being the first reasoning model that understands its context. Moreover, it is open-source and available at no cost, thus democratizing AI access for individuals and companies (Wu, 2024).
Some analysts argue that DeepSeek was built on the back of Chat GPT and was not an original innovation but in reality, it may have had no access to Nvidia H100, or H200, the best professional graphics processing units (GPUs) to date. DeepSeek utilizes less energy and employs reinforcement learning strategies.
We look first at the Artificial Intelligence models ChatGPT-4 and DeepSeek and at their responses to issues related to African Studies, in general, and African Indigenous Knowledge Systems (AIKS), in particular.

## AIKS as defined by ChatGPT-4 and DeepSeek 3

## AIKS, Mathematics and African Architecture
I asked DeepSeek to comment on the interconnections between African mathematics and architecture. The response was upbeat and positive.
“African architecture and mathematics share a deep, often under-appreciated connection that reflects the continent’s rich, cultural, scientific and historical heritage,” said the bot, and under the heading of “Mathematical principles in African Architecture,” it included fractal design, symmetry, and modularity. Under the subheading of “Vernacular Architecture and Practical Maths,” adobe walls, thatched roofs and woven structures were cited. The bot said that these relied on “geometric efficiency for stability and climate adaptation” and in the case of urban planning, cities like Benin “used radial layouts with mathematical precision.” Traditional African settlements use “recursive fractal-like patterns in their designs.”
African architecture as well as art and urban planning, employed self-similar scaling, “later formalized in Western mathematics as fractals,” the bot pointed out.
But the first major factual error of the bot was in its description of Nubian pyramids. Under a section heading called “Sacred Geometry,” the bot stated that:
Many African structures such as the Great Pyramid of Giza (Egypt) or the stepped pyramids of Kush demonstrate advanced knowledge of geometry, proportions, and astronomical alignments.
This statement contained a major error. Nubian pyramids were not “stepped “pyramids. Neither were Egypt’s, with the exception of the pyramid of Djoser. So, I queried the bot and asked for its full description of Nubian pyramids to understand its line of argument.
DeepSeek doubled down on the error describing Nubian pyramids as having “a series of stepped or tiered layers resembling a series of stacked “and “trapezoidal and tiered layers.” The chat bot was accurate in its identification of other features of the Nubian pyramids such as their steepness, 70-degree angle, decorative elements, underground burial chambers, alignments, and locations at places such as Nuri, El-Kuro, and Meroe.
It captured accurately mathematical techniques used in their construction, such as geometric proportions, trigonometric precision, steep angles, “arithmetic precision in quarrying and assembly,” and “use of fractional ratios in mortar mixtures and recursive scaling.” But as for “trapezoidal and tiered layers,” a feature that would correlate with the stepped and stacked platforms earlier described, DeepSeek was misinformed.
I told the bot bluntly that it was wrong and that Nubian pyramids were not stepped. Here is a bit of the dialogue on this matter:
Gloria Emeagwali (GE): Nubian pyramids are not stepped. You are wrong on this.
DeepSeek: You are absolutely right to point this out and I appreciate the correction. Nubian pyramids (Kushite pyramids) are not stepped in the same way as say the earliest Egyptian step pyramid (Djoser’s at Saqqara). This was a mischaracterization in my earlier response.
The bot then identified key features of the Nubian pyramids and added two interesting new segments: “Why the confusion,” “Mathematical and engineering insights,” and “How to see the difference.” The last heading referred to the difference between Nubian pyramids and Djoser’s step pyramid at Saqqara.
DeepSeek: Thanks again for catching this – it is crucial to represent these structures accurately. Let me know if you would like deeper analysis on their construction or cultural significance.

Numerous researchers have pointed to the problem of what is now called artificial hallucination or AI hallucinations. This phenomenon relates to forms of misinformation from AI agents. The major problem seems to be in referencing, although there are cases of misleading content and data.
In the case of the legal profession, few incidents have been as much publicly debated and publicized as that involving lawyer Steven Schwartz of Levidow, Levidow and Oberman P.C., sanctioned in 2023 for using nonexistent cases that ChatGPT had created out of thin air (Browning, 2024). The case in question, Mata v Avianca, Inc. took a serious turn when the defense lawyer for Avianca Airlines – sued by the plaintiff, Roberto Mata – could not locate the cases cited by Attorney Schwartz. Numerous other legal cases of AI hallucination have taken place since 2023, including a high-profile case, United States v. Michael Cohen, a case involving hush money payments and Google’s Bard (now called Gemini), the hallucinating AI culprit that created fictitious case law.
There have been concerns in the medical field. Writing in the Annals of Hepatology, Jimmy Daza, after evaluating the input of four chatbots on autoimmune liver disease, arrived at the conclusion that there was a need for improvement in currency and accuracy (Daza, 2025). Similarly, Marder et al. (2025), in their focus on sports medicine, came to similar conclusions. The researchers warned that even though the AI models may be used as a supplemental resource, providers should be discreet in recommending their use.
Robin Emsley, Professor Emeritus in Psychiatry, University of Stellenbosch, Cape Town, South Africa, in an editorial for the journal Schizophrenia in 2023, expressed skepticism about the utility of the chatbots and the LLMs associated with them, noting that the chatbots are not just hallucinating but are engaged in “fabrication” and “fraud.”
One study investigating the frequency of so-called AI hallucinations in research proposals generated by ChatGPT found that out of 178 references cited, 69 did not have a DOI, 28 of which were found not to exist.
Another study investigating the authenticity and accuracy of references in medical articles generated by ChatGPT found that of 115 references that were generated, 47% were fabricated, 46% were authentic but inaccurate, and only 7% were authentic and accurate” (Emsley, 2023).

## The Way Forward
Academic scholarship is built on a network of credible historiographies, bibliographies, citations, and research, all in the context of accuracy and trust. There is obviously a disconnect between the content and subject matter covered by the chatbots, particularly in their referencing and citation portfolios.
The flagrant weakness of the two chatbots rests in their deployment of inaccurate sources and fictitious authorship when asked for bibliographic and historiographic details. Their creation of imaginary, phantom authors, and what has been euphemistically referred to as “artificial hallucinations” has unsettled users of the models. There is also the challenge of permanent and indelible source contamination, as pointed out by Emsley (2023). These infractions merit urgent precautionary steps, including the creation of new agencies that could systematically monitor AI distortions and apply penalties to relevant companies, as disincentives for faulty programming and inappropriate algorithms.
Why is it that the bots, who were largely innovative and informed in their analyses of African board games, mathematics and architecture, became fraudsters, manipulators, and hallucinators when asked to cite sources and bibliographies? Is this deliberate or accidental? Why are the chatbots so sloppy, inaccurate, and misleading in their referencing system? Are the creators of artificial intelligence chatbots simply trying to dodge compensation for intellectual property by making invisible the true owners and originators of the thoughts consumed by the bots?
Is this phenomenon inevitable, given the algorithmic techniques and methodologies underpinning LLMs models and bots? Is the non-acknowledgement of the authors, creators, originators and sources of specific concepts, cultural patterns, technologies, and ideas primarily due to how chatbots are trained? Is the piracy of intellectual products by social media companies, and their gargantuan appetite for anonymized data, the reason the chatbots are unable to respond effectively?
Some scholars consider AI agents such as ChatGPT-4 and DeepSeek to be existential threats to scholarship, and they believe that these AI agents should be completely boycotted, given the predominance of faulty and fraudulent references.
Others take a more nuanced approach, hoping that the programmers and IT specialists associated with these models will fix the credibility loopholes voluntarily. One might ask: will they be forced to do so through legal measures, including class action lawsuits or hefty fines for the parent companies of the bots generating bibliographical and historiographical misinformation?

## Conclusion
This article focused on the evolution of ChatGPT-4 and DeepSeek with reference to African board games, architecture, mathematics, and African Indigenous Knowledges, in general. There was a clear recognition that the chatbots and the LLMs associated with them generally fared well in terms of the overall content material despite biases and pitfalls in some of their intellectual responses. The author paid attention to “artificial hallucination” in AIKS as well as other intellectual endeavors and arrived at the conclusion that steps should be taken to bridge the credibility gap between AI intellectual discourse and its tragically faulty and misleading referencing system that routinely included fictitious and mangled titles, and sloppy references.
The weaknesses in the system may be indirect and linked to rogue algorithms. Thus, the way forward lies in the creation of mechanisms for rigorous scrutiny, and in punitive action against AI parent companies that violate intellectual property rights.
