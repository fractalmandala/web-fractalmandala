• Publisher’s Book Description:
This edited book examines the growing worldwide phenomenon of civilizational populism in democratic nation-states and brings together research that explores this in a wide variety of religious, political, and geographic contexts. In doing so, the book shows how, from Europe to India and Pakistan, and from Indonesia to the Americas, populists increasingly define national belonging through civilizational identity, claiming that the world can be divided into several religion-defined civilizations with incompatible values.
The volume also discusses the complex relationship between civilizational populism, democracy, and nationalism and shows how nationalists often use civilizational identity to help define ingroups and outgroups within their society.
With this, the book investigates the salience of the concept, its widespread and influential nature, and explains how populists construct civilizational identities, and the factors behind the rise of civilizational populism. --Springer Nature
Breton, Jean-Francois. (2009). Arabia Felix: From the Time of the Queen of Sheba (Eighth Century B.C. to the First Century A. D.). University of Notre Dame Press, Notre Dame, Indiana
Reviewed by Tseggai Isaac
Arabia Felix is a book about Sheba, “modern Yemen” (p.6) with added highlights on the broader region of Arabia. The author gives an in-depth analysis on how Arabia captured the attention of ancient empires. Arabia also conducted rich trade with her contemporary civilizations such as the Egyptians, Assyrians, and Persians who “encountered caravans of aromatic products coming from South Arabia” (p.5).
Early in the book, the author mentions widespread skepticism about the location of Sheba as a mythical place or geographical propinquity. He resolves the uncertainty of location by stating: “the precise location of Sheba remains uncertain, but its frequent mention of in association with frankincense lends credence its having been in South Arabia” (p. 2).
The book’s focus is on Saba, the “Roof of Arabia,” with its graceful and well-placed capital, Marib, reigning supreme in the book’s chapters. In general, Arabia is rich in the remains of ancient civilization. It hosts the material contents of the Sabean civilization as imagined by ancient Greeks and Romans.
The introductory chapter describes how ancient civilizations romanticized this mysterious region of South Arabia now known as “modern Yemen” (p. 6). Strategically situated at the southern belly of the Arabian Peninsula, abutting on the Red Sea and open to the limitless expanse of the Southern Arabian Gulf on to the Indian Ocean, Yemen was once a powerful empire. Its topography and general landscape are formidable, because it is land of mountainous ranges, and monsoon rains.
“Dawn the Wadi Dhan” — the people of Arabia Felix — specifically the “Sabean tribes,” figure prominently in the book. They are described as “The richest of these fabled tribes were the Sabeans, who possessed all the fruits of the earth and the riches of Asia and Europe” (p. 10). They mastered the art of irrigation, enabling them to flourish and prosper in riches from fertile lands producing bounties of fruits, condiments, myrrh, cinnamon, frankincense, and grains (Chapter 5). Bounties of highly prized products, such as fragrances of every kind were available, but frankincense, gumlabdanum, incense, myrrh, saffron and storax nascent wild trees and bushes exuded sweet-smelling harvest of aroma.
The pleasant smell intermingled with the breeze and blow of the winds from the confluence of the Arabian Gulf and the Red Sea engulfing Saba or “Green Yemen.”
Travelers impressed with pleasant habitat appreciated the landscape, but they were more impressed with the blend of historical echoes of past civilization and the void left after the expiration of ancient heritages.
The Queen of Sheba figures prominently in the first chapter. She is described from the Arabian and Judaic perspectives and, as such, the pillar of Arabia Felix history. Her visit to King Solomon of Jerusalem is described as a diplomatic entourage “bringing with her an imposing retinue including camels loaded with spices, gold, and precious gems” (p. 3). She is described by the author in the way she has been perceived by Jewish, Christian, and Islamic senses of religious tradition.
Jewish and Islamic descriptions cast more of a mythical fiction. A hoopoe bird warns the Queen of the calamities that may await her if she fails to fulfill her obligation to travel and appreciate the awe-inspiring greatness and wisdom of King Solomon. She obliged, visited the King who appreciated her facial appearance, but was repulsed by her hairy legs, resulting in royal contempt for the Queen.
The Queen’s legacy is glorified by the Ethiopians who also claim her as their own, the foundational root of their Solomonic Dynasty. The author scantly mentions the Sabeans of Ethiopia (p. 121), but the biblical tone with which the Ethiopians describe the Queen as one of their ancient rulers also is sidelined by the author.
“The Garden of Saba” or the “roof of Arabia” is grounded in rich ancient civilization, as can be witnessed from the vast ancient remains. The geographical terrain is characterized by high mountain ranges, and rich plains with lush valleys suitable for producing fruits, staple crops, and fragrances. Monsoon rains and climactic seasons condition societies to make fruitful adaptation through irrigation and dam building.
The ancient world imagined “Arabia Felix” as an idealized mythical earthly garden. The book explains how Saba gained the attention of ancient peoples, explorers, and empires, such as Egypt, Mesopotamia, Persia, Greece, and Rome. From its distant past in the eighth century BCE., the region hosted a sophisticated civilization boasting of a literary system, irrigation networks, and religious beliefs adorning deities such as Ashtar, Amlaq, Hawbas, and various deities of lesser import. Ancient writers and explorers described Arabia as a mythical land of blissfulness and happiness.
Herodotus gave sustained attention to South Arabia as early as 450 BCE. and narrated about the abundance of Arabian frankincense in Babylonian markets. Explorers, merchants, and invading kingdoms frequented the region. Explorers and merchants were in eager pursuit of aromatic products, seeking voluminous and valuable qualities and quantities of spices and perfume processing products (Ch. 4).
The idealized version of Arabia Felix’s history as described by Herodotus is contradicted by Pliny the Younger.
He described the rough and steep features of the topography, and the monsoon rains, fog, and mists as horrifying difficulties for Roman soldiers to navigate. He recounted how the inhospitable terrain, and the wet and foggy climate, was difficult and deadly for ancient Roman soldiers.
Chapters 6, and 7 are about the sacred and religious culture expressed in ancient temples, cemeteries, and sarcophagi. These footprints of Arabia’s ancient histories reveal the high religious culture of Arabia Felix before Christianity or Islam. For instance, evidence from fragmentary ruins indicate that the temples and sanctuaries found in Marib, Shabwa, Jawaf, and Hadramawt, may be hints of ancient pre-Sabean civilization serving as testimonials of high arts and refined skills in construction, designs, calligraphy, and high-level engineering feats. There are also Greek and Roman inspired ruins of private and public dwellings.
The necropolis of Tamae, a vast burial complex comprising tombs, a temple, and a well, was built on the peak of Hyad Ibn ‘Aqil just north of the city. The mausoleums begin at the base of the hill and continue up the slope for nearly forty-five meters. The architecture is terraced, with entrance at a steep grade. Set side by side, they formed a great suite which was divided by a central hallway with steps. The mausoleums were all rectangular in shape and consisted of a central hallway with a series of four to eight connecting rooms along the sides. . .. These were family tombs, not unlike those found in numerous regions of the Near East. (p. 147-48).
The Sabean influence reached the region of al Hurayda on the shores of the Red Sea, where they introduced their writing to the local population. The presence of Sabeans across the sea in Ethiopia, attested to as early as the reign of Karib’ll Watar, indicates Sabean settlers in Ethiopia who worshiped the Sabean divinities dhat-Himyam (p.39) and Hawbas (p. 121).
The book pays attention to the ancient legacies of Yemen. How ancient societies with great civilizations deteriorate to utter social, political, and economic ruin, with no hope of recovery and redemption, is shown in this book.
The Yemeni affinity to their ancient, well-developed civilization is too distant. Like many Islamic societies who have jettisoned their ancient civilizations to embrace Islam, the Yemeni share the same mindset. They yearn for the Islamic dogma of reading history. Instead of approaching Yemeni civilization equipped with rationality, as a national treasure in which they could find wisdom and high-mindedness, they would rather be forgetful of their ancient glory.
Arabia Felix is written with meticulous sourcing, sound erudition, and skillful analysis. The author weaves various historical and civilizational heritages of Saba and brings them into a sharper focus to highlight various angles of historical details into succinct conclusion. The author’s sources are vast, but reading the book gives the impression that the author was a long-distance observer and cataloguer of sources. There is only one sketch illustration of Marib and none of the monuments, castles, temples, or human figures that the author presents in such scholarly erudition are depicted by the addition of illustrations.
Meticulous description of the region’s riches, social formations, and economic and political beliefs are in the book blended with religious practices and complementary administrative differentiation and bureaucratization. Thus, these riches are analyzed as reflections of structured governmental institutions, efficient and effective bureaucratic agencies, and enlightened civil societies rooted in ancient Arabian cultural foundations (pp. 95, 101).
The book’s range of focus starts in the eighth century BCE, up to the first century CE. Ancient international trade flourished with exports heading to Persia, Greece, Rome, and India. Cities were functional and of high aesthetic and artistic refinement. “Benefiting from its fertile coasts, a city harbored all sorts of riches within its houses and sanctuaries” (p. 76). Marib was the “ancient capital of the Sabean kingdom, but also the greatest city in all of Arabia” (p. 22). The city embodied the finest elements of Sabean civilization ranging from ancient religious symbols, synagogues “and a church built after the Abyssinian conquest” that can be “inferred from surviving inscriptions” (p. 24).
The civilizational evidence is summarized lucidly with clear analysis of representative themes of various sources. The overall societal, religious, and political elements give credence to the existence of a robust and stellar civilization in “Sheba,” the geographical realm of the Queen of Sheba.
There is a curious description of family life among Arabian society that indicates ancient practices of normalizing incest and borderless sexual practices among ancient “South Arabia” women. The author, half-heartedly, doubts the ancient account about South Arabian women as wives and as accessible to travelling men as a matter of hospitality. This observation is attributed to Strabo who catalogued family life in ancient Arabia (p. 98-99). Nonetheless, the author of this book relishes in describing South Arabian women’s “personal autonomy” as characterized by polyandrous sexual flagrancy and normalized incestuous relationship of women with their sons.
Looking at Arabia from today’s perspective, the divorce of modern Yemen from her ancient civilization appears complete and irredeemable.
Nearly all the civilizational material and heritages, irrespective of their glorious standards of civilization, do not seem to have captured the Yemeni people’s attention due to the intensity of Islamic monopoly of faith. Sabean religion, Judaism, and Christianity have vast footprints in Yemen.
The concluding chapter indicates that Sabean civilization persisted until the fourth century CE. Whatever innovations they may have attempted to introduce, the Sabean alphabets, civilization, and ancient heritages gave way to Islam (p. 178). Today Yemen is in deep civilizational decay. The effects of Islamic inspirations exert a firm grip on the consciousness of Yemeni society.
Arabia Felix, far astray from her ancient glorious and stellar civilization, yearns for a loadstar to lead her back to her senses of ancient honor and glory.
Boorstin, Daniel J. 1973. The Americans: The Democratic Experience. 1st ed. New York: Random House. 717 pages. ISBN 978-0-394-48724-3 Boorstin, Daniel J. 1992. The Creators. 1st ed. New York: Random House. 811 pages, ISBN 978-0-394-54395-6
Reviewed by Robert Bedeski
Two classics by American historian Daniel Boorstin should be central in the analysis of civilization.
In The Americans, he traces the progress of material innovation, starting with cattle raising in the West. A special breed adapted to the semi-arid grasslands, creating legends, and feeding a beef-eating population back East. He looks at the romance of the old West and sees lawyers and sheriffs bringing civilization to the vanishing frontier. Almost every step of the way, the government intervened with reforms and experiments to create nineteenth century America. Crime, whether organized or by free agents, added to the complexion of this emerging country.
Growth of the clothing industry and changing fashions were expedited by Elias Howe’s invention of the sewing machine and the development of factories to produce for the growing public. Other inventors such as Isaac Singer made improvements to the machine, and these were then soon overshadowed by further innovations. The cluster of inventor ingenuity, market competition, patents, and court suits against infringements, marked the progress of inventive genius to the present. But the net result has been remarkable benefits to the American consumer and the growth of huge fortunes.
The author describes the emergence of “Consumption Communities” as one result of industrialization. Entrepreneurs such as Aaron Montgomery Ward, who started as a traveling salesman, invented the mail order business, and eliminated the middleman. His success was only possible because of rural mail delivery, set up by the government. Another mail order competitor was Richard Warren Sears, who started selling watches more cheaply than local jewelers. American capitalism was born in inventors’ workshops and salesmen’s inspirations and blossomed into modern corporations.
Food processing was another field of innovation. Canning was improved and it nearly abolished the seasonal harvest cycle, with most foods available any time of the year albeit losing freshness and flavor. The author looks at the technology of architecture. Plate glass came into favor and brought sunlight into homes and offices. Steel and concrete built skyscrapers when Otis invented the elevator.
Boorstin’s tale of invention, application and prospering describes the sources of American power and wealth. Other countries had creativity but the rapid spread and openness to innovation seems to be an American trait. While the book is more than a half century old, it describes a spirit of inventiveness that has continued to the present.
The United States is still the world leader in science and technology and its universities continue to train foreign students in our latest discoveries. Science and technology know no boundaries, but we can at least acknowledge much of its modern source in the United States.
The Americans covers a critical dimension of civilization — the material improvements that have extended life expectancy and made it more pleasurable. By contrast, The Creators is more about what Karl Jaspers called “Transcendence” — the ideas, visions, and imaginations beyond the material world.
The author does address some of the material world in this book but largely in terms of how architecture in particular carries philosophical meaning and reflects the spirit of its civilization. The Japanese preference for buildings of wood, for example, reveals an attitude towards nature. Each piece is unique, whether in a temple door or in a miniature carving. Moreover, the texture is constantly in flux, depending on humidity, aging, and temperature. The spirit of wood reflects life and is expressed in architecture, gardens, poetry, Shinto, and painting.
The volume, at 747 pages and longer than The Americans, opens with discourses on the great religions and how they saw the world as without beginning. The idea of a Creator came with Judaism and was expanded with Christianity and its theology. The God of Islam never rests and creates by decree. (p.67) Confucianism consists of searching for truth, for it is never revealed. More human-centered than the other faiths, this ancient Chinese philosophy was more concerned with living in the present world than with what might exist beyond.
The history of religion is also related to iconography — whether in words, music or art. Purists considered any attempt to express the divine in sensible form blasphemous. Icons and stained-glass windows and statues made the divine more accessible to the masses but are also distortions of holy existence. Islam has been the most successful in repressing representation of God’s saints.
Much of the book addresses literature, art, and music, with religion remaining in the early Axial Age (except for later Islam). Chapter after chapter addresses Chaucer, Dante, Cervantes and numerous other authors in biographies and their works. Music of early composers, such as Bach and Beethoven, are considered in their form, impact, and biography. Literature is a major component of a civilization, with the invention of writing the key development in the transformation from barbarism.
In sum, both volumes, together, constitute a tour de force in addressing the two key components of civilization — the material and the transcendent. While The Americans tells the tale of American ingenuity and invention, that has been repeated globally and often fails. It bespeaks the benefits of a free and open society where diligent individuals see opportunity, apply their intellect and labors, risk time and capital, and may fail or succeed beyond their expectations. The Creators, on the other hand, looks at products of the imagination, expressed in architecture, literature, music, religion, and art. These creations make us human and accumulate in our civilizations.
