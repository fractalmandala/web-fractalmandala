The late Andrew Linklater (1949-2023) has been considered one of the most influential scholars within the field of International Relations. This was the result of his innovative synthesis into the field, with others, of the historical sociology of Norbert Elias (18971990), work that addressed the long-term figuration processes of societies. This synthesis was enriched with the insights of the English School of International Relations, as devised by Martin Wight (1913-1972) and Hedley Bull (1932-1985), scholars who analyzed the many and differing interlocking networks and connections amongst geopolitical actors on the world stage.
These efforts led Linklater to his final trilogy of works that addressed the different ways that violence has played in the creation and conceptions of world order: The Problem of Harm in World Politics: Theoretical Investigations (Cambridge University Press, 2011), Violence and Civilization in the Western States-Systems (Cambridge University Press, 2017) and, finally, The Idea of Civilization and the Making of the Global Order (Policy Press, 2020).
In light of the focus of the last two works of the trilogy, it was hoped that Andrew Linklater would be able to share his intellectual insights with the International Society for the Comparative Study of Civilizations (ISCSC). Unfortunately, his untimely passing in 2023 prevented such an occurrence. Nevertheless, this book review will be part of an ongoing tribute to the significant work of Andrew Linklater from the Comparative Civilizations Forum, the new name of the ISCSC.
Violence and Civilization in the Western States-Systems will be the focus of this specific book review, but the other volumes will be the subject of future reviews. Direct invitations to former colleagues of Linklater to further advance this ongoing tribute have been made.
Andrew Linklater explained in the Preface that the main goal of this volume was to build a synthesis between Martin Wight’s comparative studies into different statesystems with Norbert Elias’s research into the process of conceptions of “civilization,” to determine how the latter impacted the former. (pg. xi)
As Linklater himself explains: “The central question in the following discussion is whether modern ethical attitudes to the use of force are significantly different from the prevalent assumptions about violence and suffering in earlier Western states-systems.” (Pg. 1)
Linklater’s conception of “civilization” as used in the book is quite distinct from the common use of the term to designate a macro-scale socio-cultural unit. Rather he is referring to “civilization” as a higher stage or state of advanced development as opposed to “barbarism.”
Linklater addresses two related, but distinct characteristics of “civilization” in the book:
- (1) European conceptions of cultural superiority to other cultures and to the European past, and
- (2) Conceptions of restraint on the use of violence in any given society (European and non-European alike). (pg. 8n1)
This is a direct influence from Norbert Elias and his famous work The Civilizing Process that examined how these concepts developed over centuries within the European mindset.
While the work marks a more distinct understanding of the concept of “civilization” when compared to its usual uses within the civilizational field, it should be noted that the normal understanding is not completely neglected in Linklater’s work. This is so when Linklater further introduces the concepts of “we-feeling” or “we-identity” that “refers to solidarity between people that is most apparent in collective attachments” in varying contexts. (pg. 6)
Linklater then extends Elias’s concept of the “we-I balance” as well to explain how particular states relate to wider international societies and associations (pg.7.) This line of argument is built upon the insight of Martin Wight that “all societies of states emerged within cultural zones where pre-existing we-identity supported agreements on principles of coexistence.” (pg. 24)
It is then that these two concepts (“we-identity” and “we-I balance”) interact with a third concept of “self-restraint” that is concerned with what could legitimately be conducted in the use of violence and coercion. The balance of the three concepts created an on-going paradox that “modern ‘civilized’ peoples became both more and less dangerous to each other as part of the same process of development. In the course of European state formation, societies became less tolerant of ‘cruelty’ and unnecessary suffering within their borders, but they were also increasingly exposed to the danger of physical destruction through interstate warfare.” (pg.11.)
To do full justice to the extensive historical sociological work that Linklater accomplished in this one volume, tracing out the long evolution of this dynamic from the ancient Hellenic city-states to the present day (well until 2017 when it was published), is quite impossible in a short book review.
Suffice it to say that Linklater does provide much material and analysis to preoccupy scholars on the historical record for some time, which has also paved the way for further historical studies in International Relations inspired by the English School, such as Kevin Blachford’s more recent work World Order in Late Antiquity: The 'Two Eyes' Rivalry of Byzantium and Sasanian Persia (Oxford University Press, 2025).
To return to the contemporary era for a moment, the on-going paradox of war and violence becoming both less and more dangerous at the same time is still very much witnessed on the world stage.
Noted intellectual historian Samuel Moyn observed in his recent book Humane: How the United States Abandoned Peace and Reinvented War (Farrar, Straus, and Giroux, 2021) how NATO militaries (the United States, specifically) have attempted to wage
- war in a more “humane” manner through the use of “smart weapons” like drones that supposedly reduce casualties. The actual end result was that it did not eliminate violence but made it more invisible to public scrutiny, thus allowing “political elites steeped in liberal orthodoxies to enshroud war in secrecy designed to marginalize questions about its purpose and justness,” as Priya Satia explained in her review of Moyn’s book.1
It was in this environment that the phenomena of so-called “forever wars” that lasted for several decades were able to exist, whereas a more traditional style war would usually be concluded within the span of several years. As Rayan Fakhoury further summarized Moyn’s argument: “Might the project of humanizing war have the perverse effect of legitimating and sustaining it? Might we find ourselves waging a
- war so humane that it need never end?” 2
The same basic issue can also be discerned in recent discussions of the nature of “hybrid warfare” and “grey-zone operations” where conflict need not involve direct violence at all but still has violent implications.3

One vital significance of this dynamic is the almost complete abolition of the distinction between combatants and non-combatants.4 This dynamic has been demonstrated in the on-going conflicts in Ukraine and elsewhere. The growing interconnected and globalized world over the past few decades since the end of the Cold War was supposed to abolish war, as was proposed in the 1990s “McDonald’s Peace Theory” as it has become to be called pejoratively since.5
Now it has become clearer that no conflict operates in isolation but today all conflicts have wider global impact. The war in Ukraine, for example, has disrupted both grain and energy supplies on the global market.
This is further demonstration of the overall argument put forth by Linklater, in continuation of Norbert Elias, that the process of restraint in the use of violence is not a linear one and it often depends upon the changing dynamics of the “we-identity” as well as the “I-we balance.” With the further shifting configurations of world order underway in our age, it is important to keep this critical point in mind.
Andrew Linklater has provided for scholars quite a tour de force in documenting the historical evolution of this dynamic, one that has lain the groundwork for what we are witnessing in the present day.
