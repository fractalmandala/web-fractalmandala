Niv Horesh niv.horesh@gmail.com Abstract:
Among historians, it has become axiomatic that coinage over three millennia served three purposes: as a medium of exchange, as a store of value, and as a unit of account. The communicative aspects of coinage, i.e. the imagery it carries often by way of issuer propaganda, have been of less interest even if it remains the mainstay of numismatists. But in the world of numismatics, globally framed studies of imagery or iconography are rare. This article is aimed at correcting this imbalance, at least provisionally. It will explore the main archetypes of coinage imitation by way of inferring commonalities or dissimilarities through the ages, with particular emphasis on the Islamic world. It is hoped that this effort will encourage larger-scale efforts to map out coin imagery comparatively, so as to gain a better understanding of its propagandistic properties.
Keywords: coinage, money, imitation, metal Introduction
One of the tenets of Westphalian modernity is state sovereignty, and with it comes the notion of monetary sovereignty, namely, only the ruler’s coinage can circulate in his or her realm. In other words, modernity holds that a nation’s currency would be distinct.1 But, as we shall explore below, in pre-modernity native coinage often imitated foreign iconography so as to partake of its aura and gain a foothold in the currency market.
In focusing on imitations, we understand coinage to be round or near-round, and we thus leave the study of famous non-round coinage – such as cowrie, square punchmarks, larins, manillas, tin-hats, rhombus coinage, dolphins or even holy dollars – until later. In turn, the main round coinage specimens imitated throughout history include the Macedonian gold stater, the Roman silver antoninianus, the Roman silver denier, the gros tournois, the gold florin and a host of silver trade dollars.2 Here, we shall foreground in particular the lesser-studied coinage of the Islamic world.
What is the purpose of coin imitations? Unlike copies or forgeries, they do not aim to defraud. Rather, imitations deliberately and openly fashion themselves after other wellknown coins.

Imitations have often been issued by people for whom the concept of coinage itself was fairly recent. The weight and metallic purity of the imitative coin had to approximate the imitated coin, at least at first.
Coin imitations are contemporaneous with the coin imitated and therefore do not include Paduans. The latter is a type of coin imitation, specifically referring to 16thcentury imitations of ancient Roman coins, often created with great skill and artistic merit by Giovanni Cavino and others in Padua, Italy. These fakes, while not genuine ancient coins, are highly sought after by collectors for their craftsmanship and historical interest.3
Rather, coin imitation is in essence the process by which one ruler copies imagery fully or partially from another ruler’s coinage, either as a part of debasement attempts or through political or religious inspiration. Typically, the obverse side of a coin reflects imperial or national emblems, or indeed the ruler’s portraiture, while the reverse is often reserved for less important zoomorphic or floral elements, alongside the stated value. Imitation can involve both heraldic motifs, anthropomorphic, zoomorphic or floral motifs, and thus can occur on either obverse or reverse or both at once.4
Historically, such transmission of motifs from one coin to another was rife insofar as precious-metal coinage was concerned, but it stretched, of course, to billon (alloy) on occasion. Currencies viewed as strong in a metallic age carried an agio which profited the ruler, but on the other hand, invited intrinsically lesser-value imitations that proved financially destabilizing. To wit, here we do not deal with imitations from within one polity but focus on transmission from one polity to another.5
There are many famous examples of coin imitation around the world, right from the birth of coinage in Lydia in the 7th century BCE. However, the mechanization of coinage in modern times, as conceived by Boulton and Watt, as well as the rise of fiat money in modern times, has largely put paid to these imitations. Here we shall therefore confine ourselves to the pre-modern era, noting that the arrangements for minting/casting coinage varied enormously across time and space. Suffice it to point out that moneyers inscribed their names on some coins but not others, and that coin issuance was private-order still in some areas; religious or provincial authorities issued coinage in some places but not others. In Safavid Iran and Mughal India, foreign coins had to be surrendered at the border.

The quality of Mughal coinage was well renowned: it largely adhered to Muslim image prohibitions except during Jahangir’s reign, when a pictorial coin was issued.6 And of course, gold was scarcely issued in China.
While there was no blanket ban on all foreign coins during the Stuart period in England, there were instances where certain foreign coins were banned or their usage restricted due to concerns about their value and potential disruption to the English currency. For example, the Coinage Act of 1708 addressed the encouragement of bringing foreign coins to be coined and prosecuting offenses concerning the coin. Additionally, in the past, foreign coins were sometimes assigned specific values in relation to English currency, as seen in the reign of Henry VI.
The opposite of coin imitation is coin dissimilarity, as carried out, for example, by the many Greek city-states in antiquity. Here, polities aimed to set themselves apart from other polities through unique imagery, often animals. The famous owl coin of Athena (Tetradrachm) and turtle coin of Aegina come to mind. By contrast, Alexander the Great’s stater, issued three centuries afterwards, was not only a turning point in terms of portraiture, but also in terms of the millions of stater imitations issued by non-Greek polities around the Aegean and Black Sea. From then on, portraiture on Western coinage became rife.7
Because post-Song copper coin output levels were lower in China, Chinese emperors tolerated commoner use of standard-size coins genuinely or supposedly issued by previous dynasties. Except for Emperor Qianlong’s (r. 1735-1796) vigorous efforts to recast deficient or forged “cash” and increase coin output, Chinese late-imperial largescale recasting efforts were relatively rare. By contrast, late-medieval European debasements were often facilitated by the recall and remintage of older coinage (renovatio monata), and thus coins from previous centuries were much harder to come by there, even if coeval foreign coinage was rife. As mentioned, by the early eighteenth century, Western European polities had started discouraging the use of “older” or “foreign” coinage so as to enhance seigniorage revenue and prop up their nascent public debt.8
What is more, China’s coinage standard was bimetallic (copper and silver), whilst in Europe and the Islamic World it was trimetallic (copper, silver, and gold). This meant that polities like Iran suffered more due to a lack of exploitable precious-metal mines. In turn, precious metal (bullion or species) had to be imported from overseas, and a reliance on base metal coinage developed.

Also, in China, ruler portraiture was shunned in order to augment his mysteriousness, whereas since Alexander the Great’s staters, such portraiture became standard, if not omnipresent, on Western coinage. The tendency for portraiture was reinforced with the onset of denarii during the Roman civil war.9
In that sense, Chinese and Islamic coinage, which shunned human imagery altogether, share an important trait. But here we need to remember that European ruler portraiture on coins turned considerably more realistic (“big-headed” depictions) beginning in the 15th century.10 Curiously, the US dollar coin adhered to portraiture avoidance to distinguish itself from European autocracies until around 1909.11
## Notable Imitations
Dissimilarity notwithstanding, early Greek city-state coinage and later staters had been widely copied too by surrounding peoples. A good example is the coinage of Massalia (Marseille), which was copied by Gallic tribes in the region, Massalia being the oldest Greek settlement in Gaul.12 More generally, Celtic tribes in Europe imitated Greek coins, especially those from Macedonia, to create their own currency from the 3rd Century BCE. These Celtic imitations were often stylized versions of the Greek prototypes, incorporating unique Celtic designs and imagery. The Celtic coins, while initially mimicking Greek and Roman styles, eventually developed into distinct designs reflecting their own tribal and cultural identities. While Celtic coins did not always form a trade currency, they were nevertheless important for local trade.13
Conceptually, when it comes to dissimilarity, Rome is a good example too, because it pragmatically allowed the new provinces it conquered to continue minting their coins.14

A case in point was ancient Britain, where native designs copied Roman coins, particularly of bronze, with tribe leader names inscribed in Latin.16
The Huns imitated Sassanian coinage in India in the 5th and 6th centuries AD – see, for example, the Gadhya paisa. Yet South Indian coinage was of gold by way of dissimilarity.17 There was, however, also an imitation of Roman motifs on the obverse.18
The case for independent Jewish coinage early on under the Achaemenids is out. There was coinage carrying the YHD (Jewish or Judea) inscription with Greek motifs, mostly struck in Jerusalem. Later, Hasmonaean motifs incorporated Jewish motifs somewhat more conspicuously, but the coinage retained Greek script for the most part. 19 Interestingly, much later on in Sweden, the script JEHOVAH was inscribed on a coin minted by Karl IX.20
Herod the Great, despite maintaining a semi-autonomous status from Rome, did not issue precious-metal coins of his own imagery but relied instead on the Tyrian silver shekel (obverse Heracles head, reverse eagle on a ship’s prow). It was only during the first Great Jewish Revolt, 66-70 CE, against Rome that precious-metal as well as basemetal coins were issued with distinctly Jewish Hasmonaean motifs like the pomegranate. These were accompanied by paleo-Hebrew script, meaning “Shekel Israel” or “Yerushalaim Kadosha” (Holy Jerusalem). During the Bar Kochba War of 132-135 CE, coinage mostly consisted of overstriking of Jewish motifs like the palm branch and pomegranates. The façade of the destroyed Temple also appeared therein.21
Following the Roman era, the gold and silver coins issued by the Ostrogoths were imitations of Byzantine coinage.22 Then, King Offa of Mercia minted a gold coin in the late 8th century that was a copy of an Abbasid dinar, an Islamic gold coin.

This coin, known as the Offa Dinar, is significant because it features an upside-down Latin inscription of "Offa Rex" (King Offa) alongside a copied Arabic inscription of the Islamic declaration of faith. The purpose of this coin is debated, but it is thought to have been used for trade in areas where Islamic dinars were common, or perhaps as a gift for the Pope.23 Interestingly, Viking coinage from that era also borrowed Arab (and Byzantine) motifs.
During the Almohad period (1145-1232 CE) a coin was struck by King Alfonso VIII of Castile, who wanted to challenge the Muslim rulers of Spain. He issued a Christian coin (gold Maravedis) with Arabic script, and instead of copying the coins of the Almohads, he chose those of their rivals, the recently conquered Amirs of Murcia. These remarkable dinars were minted in Toledo, the first Islamic city to fall to the Christians.24
The remarkable feature is that the Christian coin has Arabic characters rather than Latin ones, and King Alfonso, in imitation of Prince ‘Abd Allah, calls himself Prince of the Catholics and invokes the assistance of God. Both coins are called ‘dinar’ and mention their mint center and date. The Pope is given the Arabic religious title Imam. Finally, the Bible verses quoted imitate the use of Qur’anic verses on the Islamic coin. Such coins were called maravedis, the name in Spanish of the gold dinars of the Almoravids.25
## Islamic Coinage
Islamic coinage is distinct in that it almost always shuns anthropomorphism and languages other than Arabic. But that is not to say that it completely shunned imitations of both rival Islamic coinage and coinage outside the Islamic world. The following section is devoted precisely to these exceptions.26
The first Islamic precious-metal coins we have are from the era of Uthman’s caliphate. They are copies of silver drachms issued by the Sassanian emperor Yazdegerd III.27 Early Islamic coinage was also influenced by Byzantine coinage. Later, Shi’a dynasties’ coinage and non-Arab dynasties’ coinage became slightly more floral than Sunni dynasties, and false copper coins were generally more floral than silver/gold Muslim coinage.

The first Islamic base-metal coinage of the Ummayads was made of copper (fals/fulus) and showed clear influence of Byzantine and Sassanid designs too. These coins and their successors were known as fals to this day, but they eventually shed kufic letters for more cursive scripts. For example, on the other extreme, Safavid coins were densely cursive with annulets. Furthermore, some Qajar copper coinage was later rectangular, incorporating at times human images and animals.28
Arab-Sassanian coinage featured Arabic inscriptions on the obverse, such as bismillah, while the reverse featured highly iconographic portraits of Sassanid rulers, such as Khosrau II. The copper ones probably circulated only locally; therefore, they had to incorporate recognizable Sassanian motifs.29 Similarly, Arab-Byzantine coinage often incorporated Byzantine motifs, but also Arabic script on the reverse.30
‘Abd al-Malik ibn Marwan, the Umayyad caliph, implemented significant coinage reforms in the late 7th century CE, aiming to create a unified and distinctly Islamic currency. This involved transitioning from coins that imitated Byzantine and Sasanian designs to ones that featured purely epigraphic (text-based) designs with Islamic inscriptions, primarily from the Quran. The reforms also established Arabic as the official language of the empire's administration and introduced a new weight standard for gold coins, the dinar.
The coinage of the Ummayads in Spain, for example, was all struck, never cast, even though it included much copper. More to the point, it first carried bilingual scripts, probably influenced by the Visigoths. Later, it switched to Arabic alone.31 When the Muwahidun took over, its coinage was ironically copied by the Christian kingdoms to the north of Spain. The imitations – Millares -- retained the script with Allah’s name despite the great faith rivalry of the time.
Islamic coinage in Andalusia did not impact metallurgical practices in Europe. However, 13th-century Castilian coins stuck to Muslim-style coins, while Aragon adopted Italian-style coinage for trade.32
Abbasid coinage, on the other hand, was very similar to Umayyad specimens, with only a few more verses from the Koran added. Yet it is remarkable that right through to the early Abbasid era, Sasanian coins were used on occasion.

To be precise, the first Abbasid billon dirham carried Sasanian images. An Abbasid coin from Mosul, c. 750 CE, featured a Palm Tree on the obverse alongside Arabic script.
By contrast, Andalusian coinage from the same era was much less floral (c. 998 CE). And the Idris dirham from North Africa (Shi’a) c. 818 CE carried the name of Ali in obverse. More generally, coinage by Shi’a dynasties was more ornate, forming inner circles and Stars of David enveloping cursive (later Nastaliq) script. To the contrary, Ayyubid coinage was very conservative, only dropping Kufic script for more cursive Nakshi script. However, it incorporated square annulets and, on occasion, Star of David frames.
Ayyubid coinage generally adhered to the ‘Abd Al Malik reform, but its Syrian silver variant was a little more pictographic with a dotted Star of David on the obverse enveloping inscriptions. It did not borrow motifs from the crusaders.33
Early Seljuk coinage imitated a late Byzantine specimen with a lion, a sun and horsemen with Greek script. Just as early Arab coinage imitated Byzantine and Sassanian coinage, the Islamized Seljuks now imitated late Byzantine designs. By contrast, there were fewer animal images on Mamluk gold coinage, which remained Ayyubid in spirit for the most part. However, Sunni coinage did incorporate, on occasion, hexagrams and animals on copper coinage. Later, Seljuk coinage adhered to ‘Abd Malik prototypes based on a trimetallic standard (gold dinar, silver dirham, copper fals). But Artuqid and Zandjids still reverted to earlier patterns of Byzantine and Sasanian provenance.34
Later, there were additional pictorial specimens. Zangid billon coins, for example, often carried horsemen or animal designs for the Indian sector of the empire.35 The Zangid ruler of Mosul, moreover, Saif al-Din Ghazi II, is depicted on the obverse of a coin, with Arabic script on the reverse. The Aruqid and Atabeg did not attain the same level of prominence in Islamic history, but their coinage, too, turned more pictorial than the ‘Abd Al Malik mold – with Greco-Roman and possibly even Hittite inspiration (doubleheaded eagles).36
Ilkahnid coinage was a real revolution in Islamic coinage. Few of the Ilkhanid great khans had left their names on coins that survived them.37 Ilkhanid units of weight, moreover, were very different to Abbasid coinage, and the Ilkhanid dirhams struck in Tabriz carried on their reverse Mongolian script.

Gold was associated with dinars, but the Mongols introduced a silver dinar. The Mongol coinage was also fairly pictographic, with some carrying a 12-rayed sun with eyes and nose on the obverse. The Mongols minted a lot of silver and copper in what scholars term the “first” silver century.38
Timurid tanka coins were plain silver coins in the ‘Abd Malik tradition used during the Timurid Empire, which existed from 1370 to 1507 CE. These coins were part of the empire's monetary system and were used throughout the territories it controlled, including modern-day Uzbekistan, Iran, and parts of Central Asia. The tanka was a significant denomination within the Timurid coinage system.
Early Ottoman coins were small in size and plain-looking, much like the Ayyubid style, but under the impact of early-modern European coinage from the 16th century onward, that coinage became increasingly more elaborate and floral.39 In fact, the famous Ottoman ghurush was an imitation of the German groschen. In 1844, the ‘Abd al-Majid reform of Ottoman currency was launched, and that currency now took the shape of European currency, subsequently with striking machinery. New gold Ottoman coins were launched in 1882.
During the Crusader period, particularly in the early 12th century, gold coins known as bezants were struck in imitation of Fatimid dinars, specifically those of the Fatimid caliph al-Amir. These Crusader imitations were likely produced in Acre (modern-day Akko). The bezants were used for trade and high-status payments in the Crusader states of Jerusalem. There were also imitations of silver Ayyubid coinage.40 And there were Byzantine bezants (solidius), too, which the Arabs and Europeans imitated.
In Medieval Western Europe, the florin, which was first minted in 1252 in Florence, was imitated as seen with the German gulden. These imitations, often called “forints” in other countries, aimed to replicate the desirable features of the original gold florin, such as its standard weight and value. However, the practice of imitating the florin also led to widespread counterfeiting, with over 350 copies of the coin produced.41
There was also a poorer imitation of the florin by the King of Aragon in Spain. And within Italy, the florin faced competition from the ducat and zecchino.42

In the medieval era, but earlier in the sixth century CE, Anglo-Saxon coinage imitated the Merovingian tremisses.44 But then, in a reversal of functions, in the 13th century, English coins were considered stable and were widely copied around Western Europe. Before the 15th century mainland Southeast Asia did not appear to have developed coinage of its own. To be sure, silver coins were produced between the fifth and tenth centuries, but they were strictly for local distribution. The famous temples in Pagan and Angkor did not produce coins at all. Muslim coins from Bengal then inspired Arakan coins and Siam standardized its silver weights not long afterwards. On the other hand, coins were more common in Archipelago Southeast Asia: some kingdoms such as Java, Sumatra and Luzon produced coins between the ninth and thirteenth centuries, but their coinage then gave way to imported Chinese cash (picis in Javanese) and local imitations.47
Makassar and Johor larger gold coins (four times that of Aceh) seem to be an attempt to imitate the Spanish silver real but eventually these coins were less preferred in the market than foreign silver coins and gold assessed by weight. In 1620, Sultan Iskandar Muda of Aceh (r. 1607-36) tried to stem the inflow of silver coin; he minted larger gold mas, imitating the Makassar example (which in turn imitated the silver real) but that 1620 coin was heavily alloyed and therefore refused by merchants despite amputation penalty on merchant refusals. Namely, there was an attempt at gold debasement detected immediately vs finer silver coins of foreign provenance until the Aceh larger mas disappeared (only old smaller mas remained in circulation).48
## Conclusion
Coin imitation essentially refers to the complete or partial copying of an image of another ruler's coins in order to devalue the currency or for economic, political or religious motives. The propaganda function of coins, i.e. the images often appearing in the advertisements of the issuer, has received less attention from scholars.

This article aimed to temporarily redress this imbalance. Using a variety of texts, this article examined the main forms of imitation and differentiation of round coins from different eras.
It is hoped that this study will encourage a wider comparison of coin images to better explain their propaganda characteristics.
- As mentioned above, the main round coins that have been imitated throughout history include the Macedonian gold stater, the Roman silver Antoninus, the Roman silver denier, the French Grand Tournois, the gold florin, and a large number of silver trade coins. In addition, we also focused on the less studied coins in the Islamic world. The opposite of coin imitation is coin differentiation, such as in many Greek city-states in ancient times. Conceptually, Rome is also a good example of differentiation, as it pragmatically allowed the new provinces it conquered to continue to mint their coins.
- At the same time, its famous silver denarius was widely copied outside the empire. Ancient Britain is a good example, where native designs imitated Roman coins, especially bronze coins, and were inscribed with the names of tribal leaders in Latin.
In China, on the other hand, portraits of rulers were avoided to enhance the mystique of the ruler. Since the reign of Alexander the Great, such portraits have become standard and even universal on Western coins. This trend towards portraiture was strengthened with the emergence of silver denarii during the Roman Civil War. In this sense, Chinese and Islamic coins, which both largely avoid the use of images, have important commonalities. But here we need to remember that from the 15th century onwards, the portraits of European rulers on coins became more realistic (“big head” patterns). Curiously, it was not until around 1909 that US dollar coins began to depict portraits to distinguish them from the repressive regimes in Europe.
Islamic currency is unique in that it almost always has avoided anthropomorphism and the use of languages other than Arabic. This is not to say that it completely refrained from imitating other Islamic currencies and currencies outside the Islamic world. Early Islamic currencies were also influenced by Byzantine coins. Later, Shia and non-Arab currencies were slightly more floral than Sunni currencies, and copper coins were generally more floral than Muslim silver/gold coins.
