If the lifetime of our species were imagined as a single 24-hour day, then civilization makes its appearance at the 23.02 mark. And photography? It comes on stage under a minute before midnight.
How marvelous it would have been to have had photography recording our lives throughout those millennia.

## Ambivalent feelings
Civilization! A big, imposing word, with multiple connotations, often conflicting with each other. To Western children of my own generation, around the middle of the last century, it conjured up visions of pyramids and ziggurats, the beautiful Cleopatra (or in truth, a sultry stand-in: Elizabeth Taylor [1932–2011]), ‘the riddle of the Sphinx’ (meaning who sculpted it and why?), exotic Greek or Indian temples with altars of human sacrifice, or the Hanging Gardens of Babylon. Particularly thrilling were tales of ‘lost’ civilizations—Atlantis, El Dorado, The Elysian Fields –– many a young child secretly fantasized: “I will be the one to find them!”
We certainly did not think of our own society as a ‘civilization,’ let alone as fascinating; it was simply the natural social order; as the adage goes, fish discover water last.
For the wealthier nations of the 19th century, living in an era of constantly disruptive mechanical invention — justly called a revolution — civilization meant steamships, smoke-belching locomotives, electric light, the phonograph, the telegraph, and for excitement, Western children thrilled to hair-raising tales of what was then commonly called ‘dark Africa’: cannibalism, and valiant near-naked warriors with spears! Generations before ours were taught that the “3 C’s” — Christianity, Commerce and Civilization — were bringing light into a fascinating but dark and primitive world, the ‘savages' of which were said to represent ’the childhood of Mankind’.3
To the stronger, richer nations of the world, those imposing their technologies, beliefs and values on the poorer, weaker ones, civilization meant progress! And it was, everyone agreed, unstoppable. To its victims, civilization mostly meant oppression and the constant reminder of their social and material inferiority.
For younger people of the 21st Century, what might first come to mind when they hear the word ‘civilization’ is the hugely popular video game of that name, which invites its millions of players to “construct cities and architectural wonders, expand your territory, improve your civilization with technological breakthroughs, and conquer or cooperate with rival civilizations as you explore the far reaches of the unknown world.” 4 Entertainment perhaps, but clearly the core sense of civilization — its grandeur strikes a chord with many. And while they have been busy with their civilizational building, another one hundred million people of various ages are listening to tales of civilizational collapse, via Paul Cooper’s 1989 podcast, Fall of Civilizations: Stories of Greatness and Decline.
As already mentioned, civilization is a word fraught with heavy emotional baggage. If you come from a society where another mightier society once ruled over your ancestors, and claimed exclusive ownership of the term civilization, its mention may make your blood boil; if you come from a society which has benefitted inordinately from the treasure, some of which has been passed down to you, you are perhaps less likely to quite be so critical. The legacy of slavery is fresh in our minds (and even, we know, still deep in some hearts).

## Civilization singular / Civilizations plural
How, then, to define civilization? The anthropologist Claude Lévi-Strauss (1908–2009) reminds us that we are free to use a word (which after all is an instrument or tool), any way we want, provided we say precisely what we mean by it. And here, a problem immediately arises: no two scholars seem to agree! Paradoxically, it may be more helpful to begin with what civilization is not.
5 Ecology and Civilization: A Sample of the Work in Progress,” Brendan Myers, post of December 8, 2015, brendanmyers.net. The book which followed, Reclaiming Civilization, was published in 2017 by Moon.
Most scholars dismiss smaller social units; a civilization is not a clan, a tribe, a single language, even a nation or an empire, a government, nor a specific culture, but can include many of these components. Dynasties and empires, for example as in China, can rise and fall and yet the greater envelope of the civilization remains intact. And distinct cultures and languages can find secure niches in an overarching civilization; even remnants of so-called Stone Age cultures have survived against all the odds in the remote pockets of advanced civilizations.6
No one questions scientist Jared Diamond’s observation that, “over the past millennia, the predominant trend in human societies has been the replacement of smaller, less complex units, by larger more complex ones.” 7 There is also agreement that a civilization is the largest, most advanced form of society in any given part of the world, and that it persists through many generations.
The French historian Fernand Braudel (1902–1985) sums it up eloquently: “Civilizations are extraordinary creatures, whose longevity passes all understanding. Fabulously ancient, they live on in each of us, and they will live on after we have passed away.”8
A legitimate confusion arises from the use of civilization, singular, vs civilizations, plural.
There is no denying that the great civilizations of the past were distinct and unique, and were either isolated from each other, or were in minimal degrees of contact. However, scholars disagree as to how many past civilizations there have been; this is especially true given that modern imaging technologies like satellites or Lidar, which see through the thick cover of vegetation, are uncovering vanished civilizations at a rapid rate. No one disputes the familiar ones: Mayan, Aztec, Incan, Egyptian, Hellenic, Indian, Arabic, Chinese, Western, etc. but some stop counting after nineteen, as does the historian Arnold Toynbee (1889–1975) or nine, as does the political scientist Samuel Huntington

Western ethnocentrism has long ignored most African civilizations; Egypt and Nubia are way up there in the rankings, but Kush, Nok and Aksum? Largely ignored, like still others. Also, at the rate at which long-lost civilizations are being discovered, or smaller societies re-classified as civilizations, even seventy-five might be far too low.
While civilization in the singular can refer to specific examples, it can also have another, much more expansive meaning: as a synonym for humanity as a whole, the aggregate of all those individual civilizations, whether of the past, the present, or as imagined in the future.
This meaning of civilization is nothing less than the common heritage of humanity: language, control of fire, cities (a kind of human particle collider!), the wheel, writing, metallurgy, pottery, textiles, mathematics, art, the cultivation of plants and the domestication of animals, medicine, science and religion — not to mention the microscope, the telescope, the camera, the International Space Station, the computer chip, nanotechnology, and a billion other ingenious inventions and discoveries allowing us 21st-century Homo sapiens to dream of leaving the ‘nest’ of the earth and flying off to settle on distant planets.
Civilizations are no longer isolated from each other; they have been borrowing from each other for millennia, a borrowing which has approached fever pitch in the last few centuries.
Sociologist David Wilkinson speaks metaphorically of a civilizational ‘Big Bang’, when Egyptian and Mesopotamian societies first collided, then fused, starting us on the road to where we are now: witnessing the emergence of a single planetary-wide civilization (also called by scholars World, Universal, Central, Global, and even Electronic Civilization).10
“We may well say that by now all or almost all of us, in every or almost every part of the planet, have become modern,” writes Zygmunt Bauman (1925–2017). “And that means that today, unlike a decade or two ago, every land on the planet, with only a few exceptions, is subject to the obsessive, compulsive, unstoppable change that is nowadays called ‘modernization’.”11

Only a decade ago, a New York Times article reported two billion people were online, predicting that the figure would double in a decade. A decade later, we are well above that prediction, at 5.5. Yes, distinct cultural practices are kept up in places (at least for now, and some kept up artificially), but that great homogenization more often takes the form of a bulldozer, flattening the cultural terrain, and in some places obliterating it overnight. The Japanese/American physicist Michio Kaku believes that the transition “is perhaps the greatest in human history.” He summarizes: “all the technological revolutions (are) leading to a single point: the creation of a planetary civilization.”12
And certainly, in its brief time on Earth, photography has contributed immeasurably to that process! Is it not amazing that we, people of the 21st Century, are here not only to witness this phenomenal social experiment, but that we have well-trained eyes to record it, share it, and pass it on to future generations?
## Civilization: state or process?
It might surprise readers to learn that the term civilization was first used to describe not some great edifice of human society, but human behavior. The first mention of the term seems to date back to 1756, when the French Enlightenment scholar, Victor de Riqueti (1715–1789), used it to delineate the vast gulf between a refined gentlefolk, rational and principled, and uncouth ‘savages’ or barbarians, irrational and violent. Today, the adjective uncivilized is still used to denote vulgar and unruly acts, as in ‘soccer hooliganism.’13
For the great German sociologist Norbert Elias (1897–1990), it was more fruitful to think of civilization not in terms of ‘superior us and inferior them’, but as a long-term formative process that human society had to go through to achieve extraordinarily complex levels. Human Beings had to learn to cooperate to master their physical environment, their fellow animals, and not least, themselves. Shame, remorse, embarrassment, guilt, humility, and self-restraint evolved as effective mechanisms to keep disruptive social tendencies in check.

It is thought today that Neanderthals lost out to our species not because they were less intelligent, but because they were less skilled socially, i.e., less effective in cooperation. By this reckoning, millennia before the Neolithic Revolution some 12,000 years ago, humans were already laying the groundwork for the nascent civilizations of the Nile, the Tigris, the Euphrates, the Indus, and the Yellow River.
And while we tend to think of civilization in material terms, i.e., things, objects made of wood, then stone, clay, glass, bronze, iron, steel, silicone, graphene, carbon-concrete, optoelectronics and printed objects in 4D 14 , civilizations also have immaterial components: ideas, values and beliefs — sometimes more important to their members than those material things; we will actually die, and kill, to protect them! And many things we think are real are actually only ideas, but universally so accepted that they become reified — second and minute, hour and week, century and millennium, centimeter and kilometer, gram and kilogram, degree and the ampere, the 0 and the 1. All are arbitrary measures which we willingly and unquestionably accept.
The year 2025, for example: No! say the Buddhists, it is actually 2564. No! say the Chinese, it is 4721. No! say proponents of the Holocene, its 12025. Personally, I prefer to pay homage to our species, so I claim we are in the year 300,025.
Immaterial names or labels are also central to civilizations. And ironically, they last longer than the material forms to which they were attached. Sumer, Babylon, and Troy go on living in the human mind when they are no more than dust. In the so-called New World, the Iroquois lost out to European settlers but their name for the much-revered river lives on: the Ohio. And when things cannot be transported vast distances, ideas often are: When Europeans migrated to America, they called their new towns Amsterdam, Warsaw, Paris, Athens, London, Berlin, Florence, Hamburg, and… Munich.15
Neatly tying the material and immaterial strands together, the Polish/American scholar Andrew Targowski defines civilization as “an info-material structure developed by humans to effectively cope with themselves, nature, and their creator, whether God or the Big Bang.”16

## A collective and cumulative achievement
Finally, there are two other aspects of civilization particularly pertinent to the subject of photography which need to be addressed. Firstly, civilization is a collective achievement. Challenges are met by cooperative behavior (not necessarily, of course, without friction). Great structures  irrigation systems, canals, dams, buildings, tunnels, bridges, rockets, spacecraft, particle colliders, telescopes, universities, laboratories, the world-wide web, governments, think-tanks  are erected and maintained by teamwork, individuals working in concert (not so different from the components of a machine). We see this harnessing of collective energy in Euclid, the European Space Agency’s telescope, which began with the ideas of a tiny group and mushroomed to harness the skills of more than three thousand scientists and technicians. Thousands of other people  and indirectly millions  were or are involved directly or indirectly in Jupiter’s Cassini probe, the Hubble and James Webb telescopes, CERN, Hollywood, NASA, Polaroid, Formula One, the Academy Awards, the Svalbard Global Seed Vault, the sequencing of the Human Genome, and so forth. And when trouble erupts, understanding a common peril, thousands put their shoulders to the wheel to erect the Great Arch of Chernobyl, to invent Covid vaccines (which many had claimed were impossible!), or to restore the Great Cathedral of Notre Dame.
It is tragic that our collective genius is being devalued while a veritable cult of individuality dazzles us with its superficial glamor. We see this in our worship of a mere handful of athletes during the Olympic Games; we forget the trainers, the schools (encouraging athletics since childhood!), the science, the technology, the social and political support, and all the infrastructure behind the achievement.
Those record-breakers did not jump higher or run faster on their own! For example, the original pole vaulters used bamboo; today they make use of lab-tested carbon-fiber composites. World Records are also our civilization’s achievements!
And talking about the collectivity, what better example than the Olympics as a whole? Virtually every nation on Earth participates; more than half the world’s population, watches some part of the Games.
Secondly, civilizations are cumulative achievements in a time-sense, each generation passing down (or if one prefers, passing ‘up,’ given that we stand on the shoulders of our predecessors), its knowledge and skills to the next. Before Covid, we tended to take our educational systems for granted, which each year pump out young adults ready and eager to take the reins and wield the tools they have learned to master — before being driven to improve them. No longer. Part of the alarm caused by our recent (collective) Covid experience was the fear that young people were losing a year or more of schooling; we count absolutely on the smooth functioning of the educational pump.
Perhaps what works best as a metaphor for a perennial civilization is the humble onion (which, incidentally, is itself a civilizational accomplishment, domesticated in China 7,000 years ago), its accumulating skins representing human generations succeeding each other. And yet, what distance can accumulate between them: when my own grandfather was a child, his family took six weeks to reach New Zealand; I went recently… overnight. It is also sobering to think that only twenty-five ‘skins’ take us back to the time of year zero.
## A narrow but sharply focused lens
Civilization, the Way We Live Now, is not conceived as a critique or condemnation, nor as a celebration, but as a sober (if obviously piecemeal) reflection of the current world, via a very specific and by definition narrow ‘lens’ — narrow figuratively and literally — that of the visual medium we call photography. I say ‘narrow’ in the sense that its images are frozen traces of events which are already in the past; they do not move, or give off scent or smell, and they are mute. They frame the world abruptly, surgically, cutting out far more than they show. They cannot directly depict words, music, names, thoughts, or poetry.
But all these limitations can also be seen as giving the medium its specific strengths. That lack of movement, for example, proved useful in freezing what was in front of the eye, splitting seconds, enabling the viewer to discern the essential order or structure in what appears chaotic when in flux.
That surgical framing forces us to grasp the essentials. In this sense, photography is an extraction, an abstraction — and an extremely useful one. And so convincing that we often lose ourselves in an image, forgetting that we are not looking at the real thing.
Photographers are everywhere on the globe, photographing everything. I speak here of serious full-time photographers, but if one were to add amateurs, we will have to acknowledge the 6.9 billion images they share on WhatsApp alone every day. Except for highly secure government and military sites, nothing escapes the sharp eye of the photographer (even with those restricted places images leak out, as those of the horrors at Abu Ghraib prison).
Photographers may spend a lifetime documenting a specific subject, like prisons, or schools, or laboratories, while other photographers prefer to roam a particular place, a city, or an entire country, or even a continent. Some focus on peaceful activities and construction, others on the violence of war and destruction. Photographers work on the ground or the water or take to the air, using planes, drones, and satellites.
Scientists and technicians have placed cameras on the Moon, Mars and deep within space; Voyager 1 kept on snapping pictures until there was nothing left to see, while high-resolution pictures from a circling satellite caught the Philae probe in the act of landing on Comet 67P at a distance of 510 million kilometers from Earth.17
Some photographers document what they see around them; others construct (or deconstruct or reconstruct) microcosms in their studios. But all wish to relay some important truth about the world and our place in it. And like civilization itself, photography is planetary-wide, collective, and cumulative.

## Photography is cumulative
Photographers know the history of their medium and are proud of it. Moreover, they see themselves as extending the work of their forebears. Every photographer has a figure (or more) they revere: Julia Margaret Cameron (1815–1879), Eugène Atget (1857–1927), August Sander (1876–1964), Edward Steichen (1879–1973), Irving Penn (1917–2009), Diane Arbus (1923–1971) or Robert Frank (1924–2019), to name a mere handful from the past.
These figures give them guidance, inspiration, even courage. Just as civilization is like an onion with its accumulation of fine skins, so too is photography.

## Photography evolves and mutates
Like all human inventions since the stone axe, photography is continually being developed and refined. Nearly every decade some radical invention changes its practice in dramatic fashion. In our own time, the shift from analogue to digital has been the seminal shift, but every generation since 1826 could claim some degree of technological revolution.
The introduction of color, for example, something we take for granted today, was seen as a shock as late as the 1960s and 1970s.18 The great civilizational historian Arnold Toynbee maintained that civilizations developed through meeting challenges with vigorous responses. Those scientists and engineers charged with developing the tools of photography might well claim to be doing the same thing.

## The way we live now
Not a day goes by that doesn’t address climate change, rationally or emotionally, global warming, sea-level rise, deforestation, water scarcity, plastic pollution, the ozone hole, as well as overpopulation, the emergence of terrifying new viruses, the risks of uncontrolled nanotechnology, robotics and artificial intelligence, disinformation, new forms of warfare, human rights abuses, etc. etc., nor propose ingenious (often truly miraculous) solutions. On the other hand, we have heartfelt pleas for diversity, equality, and tolerance. If our civilization is going to survive (there is much talk of de-civilization processes at work), some kind of balance must be achieved.
Lest we be too hard on ourselves, it would be wise to keep in mind Charles Dickens’ (1812–1870) assessment of his own era: “It was the best of times, it was the worst of times. It was the age of wisdom; it was the age of folly. It was the epoch of belief; it was the epoch of incredulity.”19

Meyer might have been thinking of the visionary H. G. Wells (1866–1946) a century earlier, who claimed that “Never before have the conditions of life changed so swiftly and enormously as […] in the last fifty years. We have been carried along  with no means of measuring the increasing swiftness in the succession of events.”21
These sentiments reflected a bewildering, fast-paced modernity which, in our own era, is accelerating off the charts (literally: human beings have travelled at 39,897 kph, and one of their most sophisticated machines, Voyager 1, probing deep space, is now twenty-five billion kilometers from Earth). And we now measure time in attoseconds: one attosecond is to a second, what a second is to approximately 31.69 billion years.
Phonons, magnons, spinons and plasmons: our scientists are racing ahead of us with their particle discoveries. Just five years ago, a computer was able to demonstrate quantum advantage, completing a calculation in two hundred seconds that would take a conventional supercomputer more than millions if not billions of years. Meanwhile the prestigious scientific journal Nature tells us enigmatically that “probability probably doesn’t exist.”22 Or that time passes faster in space than on Earth; even our satellites must be routinely adjusted to take relative time into account. A firm footing anywhere is increasingly hard to find. Arthur C. Clarke (1917–2008) noted of advanced technology that it had become almost “indistinguishable from magic.”23
As for AI, now all around us, it is anyone’s guess as to where that will lead. But we would be wise to recall the words of Nicephore Niépce (1765–1833), inventor of photography, who also saw his creation as a “first uncertain step in a completely new direction.” Taking risks, often blindly, seems to be a very human trait.

From an anthropologist’s point of view, photographs are not simply art works; they are cultural artefacts. They are objects of their time and place, and reflect their enveloping civilization’s values and beliefs, often unconsciously. As the curator John Szarkowski (1925–2007) observed, photographs are both windows and mirrors: windows on the world, mirrors of the observer’s mindset.24
Photographers are not speculators, philosophers, or scientists. They are pragmatists, trying to make sense of what happens in front of their eyes. They have wide and divergent beliefs and viewpoints, of course, and we accept that these can guide their pursuits. But we still demand of them that they produce clear, coherent, and vivid imagery, to which we can bring our own value judgements to bear. In as little as one hundred years, the photographs now on the walls of the Kunsthaus Munich – hopefully preserved! – will seem like witnesses to a distant age.
But viewers will still marvel at the rich picture they had provided of a distant time and place, and search for signs that bind them to the world of their own present.
