Andrew Targowski Former President of the International Society for the Comparative Study of Civilizations Western Michigan University (Emeritus) andrew.targowski@wmich.edu Abstract
This research provides a comprehensive and interdisciplinary exploration of the transition toward a multiplanet civilization, with a special focus on Mars as humanity’s first major step beyond Earth. By addressing technological, political, economic, and ethical dimensions, it aims to inform policymakers, scientists, and the public about the feasibility, challenges, and philosophical implications of interplanetary expansion.
As humanity approaches the threshold of becoming a multiplanet civilization, it faces unprecedented scientific, technological, economic, and ethical challenges. This paper explores the rationale, methodologies, and critical considerations for establishing sustainable human settlements beyond Earth, with a particular focus on Mars. By analyzing advances in propulsion, habitat design, planetary terraforming, governance structures, and economic viability, this research outlines a roadmap for interplanetary expansion. The discussion integrates multidisciplinary perspectives from astrophysics, bioengineering, space law, and environmental ethics, providing an actionable framework for the transition toward a multiplanet species.

Introduction The Imperative of Becoming a Multiplanet Civilization
Humanity has always been a species of explorers, from Homo sapiens' migration out of Africa 70,000 years ago to the Age of Exploration in the 15th and 16th centuries, when European navigators set sail to discover new lands. Each of these great expansions carried immense risks but also unprecedented opportunities. Today, our frontier is no longer the vast oceans of Earth but the depths of space. The transition toward a multiplanet civilization is not just a possibility; it is an imperative.
Figure 1. Elon Musk has proposed to name the first human settlement on Mars as “Terminus.” Perhaps other names should be considered. (Source: Generative AI, Dreamstime.com).
Throughout history, civilizations that failed to adapt or expand have perished. The collapse of the Roman Empire (476 AD), the demise of the Maya civilization (ninth century AD), or the tragic fate of isolated cultures like Easter Island’s Rapa Nui serve as stark reminders that societies dependent on limited resources eventually succumb to external pressures. The same principle applies to planetary-scale civilizations. Earth is subject to numerous existential risks, including:
- • Asteroid impacts (e.g., the Chicxulub impact sixty-six million years ago that caused the mass extinction of the dinosaurs),
- • Supervolcanic eruptions (such as the Toba eruption ~74,000 years ago, which may have caused a population bottleneck in early humans),
- • Nuclear war (e.g., the near catastrophe of the Cuban Missile Crisis in 1962),
- • Biological threats (such as pandemics, bioengineered viruses, or ecological collapse).
In this context, expanding beyond Earth is the next logical step in ensuring the survival and long-term prosperity of human civilization. As the astrophysicist Carl Sagan warned, “All civilizations become spacefaring or extinct.” The alternative to expansion is stagnation, and history teaches us that stagnation often leads to decline.
Beyond survival, interplanetary expansion represents the next phase of human evolution. Just as the Apollo 11 Moon landing in 1969 marked the dawn of human activity beyond Earth, the next great leap must be the establishment of a self-sustaining human colony in another world.
Technological advancements in rocket propulsion (SpaceX Starship), life support systems (NASA Artemis), and AI-driven automation are making this vision more feasible than ever. The question is no longer "Can we become a multiplanet species?" but rather "When will we, and what challenges must we overcome?"
## Why Mars? The Case for the Red Planet
Among the celestial bodies within reach, Mars stands out as the most viable candidate for human settlement. In many ways, Mars today resembles the New World of the 15th century — harsh, unfamiliar, and seemingly inhospitable, yet full of potential. When Christopher Columbus set sail in 1492, no one in Europe knew exactly what lay ahead, and the risks of failure were enormous. Similarly, sending humans to Mars presents daunting technical and logistical challenges, but the potential rewards are equally vast.
Unlike the airless, radiation-bathed Moon, Mars offers several key advantages:
- • A day-night cycle of 24.6 hours, like Earth’s.
- • Significant water ice deposits, essential for sustaining life.
- • An atmosphere (though thin) that allows for some degree of shielding from cosmic radiation.
- • Resources that can be used for in-situ resource utilization (ISRU), including carbon dioxide for oxygen production, water for agriculture, and regolith for construction materials.
Despite these advantages, Mars remains a hostile environment:
- • Surface temperatures average -63°C, far colder than any inhabited region on Earth.
- • The atmosphere is 96% carbon dioxide, making direct human respiration impossible.
- • Radiation levels are high, exposing settlers to long-term health risks.
- • Gravity is only 38% of Earth’s, raising unknown physiological challenges for long-term human habitation.
Nevertheless, Mars presents a far better long-term settlement opportunity than the Moon, due to the availability of water and potential for terraforming. Moreover, Mars is not just a destination — it is a steppingstone to the greater solar system. If we can survive on Mars, we can expand to the icy moons of Jupiter and Saturn, free-floating space habitats, or even interstellar voyages.
## The Multidisciplinary Approach to Mars Settlement
Throughout history, such efforts at discovery and settlement efforts required more than just technology — they required economic, social, and political structures to sustain them. The settlement of North America, for instance, was not just about ships and navigation but also about governance, trade, agriculture, and law.
Similarly, the transition to a multiplanet civilization is not merely a technological challenge but also a multidimensional endeavor that intersects with fields such as:
- • Astrophysics and engineering:
- o Development of reliable propulsion systems, nuclear power stations, and sustainable energy sources.
- o Radiation shielding solutions and AI-driven infrastructure development.
- • Economics and geopolitics: o The role of private companies (SpaceX, Blue Origin) vs. national agencies
(NASA, ESA, CNSA). o How space colonization could trigger new geopolitical alliances and conflicts.
- • Sociology and psychology: o Psychological effects of long-duration space travel and Mars isolation. o Potential governance models: Will Mars be an independent republic, a
corporate outpost, or a colony of Earth?
- • Ethics and philosophy:
- o The moral dilemmas of planetary colonization, including planetary protection (preserving potential Martian microbial life) vs. the right to terraform.
- o Ethical implications of genetic modification for space adaptation.
This paper provides a comprehensive framework for understanding the transition toward a multiplanet species, focusing particularly on Mars as the steppingstone to our interplanetary future. It draws on historical precedents, contemporary research, and emerging technologies to assess the feasibility of long-term Martian settlement and explore the broader implications of humanity’s expansion beyond Earth.
## Historical Analogies for Space Colonization
The following analogies of large-scale territorial human expansions can be recognized, however, on one planet:
- • The Polynesian Expansion (~3,000 BCE - 1,200 CE)
- o Early Polynesians navigated the vast Pacific Ocean using star maps and ocean currents, settling on remote islands like Hawaii and Easter Island.
- o They faced harsh environmental conditions, resource scarcity, and the need for self-sufficient survival — challenges like those of future Mars colonists.
- • The Age of Exploration (15th-17th century)
- o European explorers ventured into unknown lands with limited technology and high mortality rates, yet they laid the foundations for future settlements.
- o The Jamestown colony (1607) faced extreme hardship, mirroring the difficulties an early Martian colony will likely encounter.
- • Antarctic Research Stations (20th-21st century)
- o Modern Antarctic bases operate in hostile, isolated environments, relying on imported supplies, innovative technology, and international cooperation.
- o They provide a model for the logistics, organization, and governance of future Martian settlements.
## Can We Envision a Multi-planet Civilization?
Among contemporary visionaries, there have been outspoken advocates for humanity’s expansion beyond Earth. Through SpaceX, a company he founded in 2002, Elon Musk has made it his lifelong goal to establish a self-sustaining human colony on Mars. Unlike traditional government-led space programs, his approach prioritizes cost reduction, rapid technological iteration, and reusable spacecraft. His plan revolves around several key components:
The Starship Program: Mars Transport at Scale The following space vehicles are planned:
- • Starship, SpaceX’s fully reusable spacecraft, is designed to carry 100+ passengers per flight, making it the most ambitious transport vehicle for interplanetary travel.
- • The booster (Super Heavy) and Starship upper stage are built for rapid turnaround, meaning they can be relaunched multiple times within a brief period, dramatically cutting costs.
- • SpaceX envisions a fleet of thousands of Starships departing Earth in synchronized windows, utilizing orbital refueling before heading toward Mars.
## Mars City: A Self-Sustaining Colony?
Musk’s vision does not stop at landing humans on Mars — he envisions a fully operational city, capable of functioning independently from Earth. The roadmap includes:
- • An initial outpost of robotic construction vehicles and supply caches, preparing for human arrival.
- • The first human crews arriving with Starships will bring critical infrastructure such as solar power systems, pressurized habitats, and food-growing technologies.
- • In-situ resource utilization (ISRU): Extracting water ice for drinking, oxygen, and rocket fuel (via electrolysis) and producing methane from atmospheric CO₂ for fuel using the Sabatier process.
- • A gradual shift toward self-sufficiency, where Mars produces its own materials, grows its own food, and eventually operates without reliance on Earth-based supply chains.
## The Timeline: How Soon Could This Happen?
The current suggested timelines have been ambitious, but the aim is to land humans on Mars by the 2030s and establish a self-sustaining colony by 2050. Milestones include:
- • 2026-2028: First uncrewed missions using Starship for cargo delivery and site preparation.
- • 2030-2035: First human landings, setting up permanent habitats.
- • 2040-2050: Expansion toward a city of one million people, supported by industries such as mining, manufacturing, and agriculture.
The hope is that by mid-century, the cost per ticket to Mars could drop below $100,000, making interplanetary migration accessible to a broader range of people, rather than to only government-backed astronauts.
Challenges to This Mars Vision While the current projections are groundbreaking, significant challenges remain:
- • Technology Readiness: Starship still requires further testing and certification for deep-space travel.
- • Health & Safety: Prolonged radiation exposure, microgravity effects, and psychological isolation must be addressed.
- • Political & Legal Considerations: Mars settlement raises questions about governance, land ownership, and international treaties.
• Sustainability: Creating a fully self-sufficient Martian economy remains a speculative challenge.
Nevertheless, efforts so far have already revolutionized space travel, from pioneering reusable rockets to reducing launch costs by over 90% compared to traditional space agencies. We now see potential competitors like NASA, Blue Origin (Jeff Bezos), and China’s CNSA, all seeking to accelerate their deep-space ambitions.
The extant plan being proposed by Mr. Musk, while audacious, aligns with humanity’s long-term need for interplanetary survival. If successful, SpaceX could lay the foundation for a multiplanet civilization, marking the beginning of a new era in human history, where life is no longer confined to Earth.
The first human footprints on Mars will be as historic as the first steps on the Moon, but they will also mark the beginning of something far greater — the transformation of humanity into a multiplanet species. The road ahead is long and full of obstacles, but history teaches us that the greatest achievements always begin with bold, uncertain steps into the unknown.
## Planning a Settlement on Mars
Establishing a permanent human presence on Mars requires a meticulous, multi-phase strategy that integrates scientific research, engineering innovation, and sociopolitical structures. Unlike past terrestrial colonization efforts, a Martian settlement cannot rely on frequent resupply from Earth. It must be designed for long-term sustainability, addressing fundamental challenges such as radiation exposure, resource extraction, food production, and governance.
As noted above, humans cannot breathe naturally in Mars' atmosphere because it is composed of 96% carbon dioxide (CO₂), with only 0.174% oxygen (compared to Earth's 21% oxygen). To survive, humans on Mars need a closed-loop life support system providing a constant oxygen supply.
Humans will breathe on Mars in the following manner: • Pressurized Habitats with Oxygen Recycling
- o Settlements on Mars will have airtight, pressurized habitats where oxygen is continuously supplied and recycled.
- o NASA's Environmental Control and Life Support System (ECLSS), currently used on the ISS, will be adapted to filter CO₂ and regenerate oxygen.
- • In-Situ Oxygen Production (ISRU - In-Situ Resource Utilization) o NASA’s MOXIE (Mars Oxygen In-Situ Resource Utilization Experiment)
on the Perseverance rover has successfully demonstrated oxygen extraction from CO₂ using electrolysis.
o Future Mars colonies may scale up this technology to produce oxygen for human use and rocket fuel.
- • Oxygen Storage & Backup Systems
- o Pressurized oxygen tanks will be essential for emergencies or failures in oxygen production.
- o Portable life support systems (spacesuits) will provide astronauts with breathable oxygen while outside habitats.
Humans will rely on artificial oxygen production, recycling, and airtight habitats to breathe on Mars. As technology advances, future colonies may develop self-sustaining oxygen production systems, reducing dependence on Earth.

## Potential Evolutionary Adaptations:
The Martian environment, characterized by lower gravity, higher radiation levels, and a thin atmosphere, could drive unique human adaptations over time. Some scientists hypothesize that future generations born on Mars might develop distinct physical traits, such as:
• Altered Skin Pigmentation: To combat increased radiation exposure, humans might develop new skin pigments, potentially resulting in green skin tones.
- • Bone and Muscle Density Changes: The reduced gravity could lead to decreased bone and muscle density, affecting overall physique and strength.
- • Vision Adaptations: Living in environments with artificial lighting and confined spaces might influence visual acuity, possibly leading to nearsightedness.
These projections are speculative but certainly underscore the profound impact that Mars' environment could have on human physiology.
## Site Selection Criteria
Choosing the optimal location for a Martian settlement is one of the most critical decisions in the planning process. The selected site must balance scientific feasibility, safety, and long-term habitability. Key selection factors include:
- • Proximity to water ice: Water is essential for drinking, agriculture, and producing fuel via electrolysis (splitting H₂O into hydrogen and oxygen). Ideal sites include high-latitude regions with subsurface ice or equatorial locations with access to hydrated minerals.
- • Geological stability: Settlements must be built in regions free from major seismic activity and landslides. The presence of solid bedrock is advantageous for construction.
- • Solar exposure: Sunlight is the primary energy source for solar panels, making equatorial regions favorable due to longer daylight hours and minimal seasonal variation.
- • Natural protection: Lava tubes or canyon walls (e.g., Valles Marineris) may provide shielding from radiation and micrometeoroids, reducing the need for artificial protective structures.
Potential landing sites include Elysium Planitia, Arcadia Planitia, and the mid-latitude ice-rich regions identified by NASA’s Mars Reconnaissance Orbiter (MRO).
## Habitat Design
Given the extreme conditions on Mars (low pressure, high radiation, and subzero temperatures), human habitats must provide airtight, temperature-controlled, and radiation-protected environments. The settlement will evolve through several phases of infrastructure development:
##  Initial Surface Modules
• The first humans on Mars will likely live in prefabricated, inflatable habitats, like NASA’s Bigelow Expandable Activity Module (BEAM) tested on the ISS.
- • These modules will be transported by Starship-class rockets and deployed before human arrival.
- • Life-support systems, including oxygen generation, water filtration, and thermal insulation, will be integrated into the habitat design.
##  Autonomous Robotic Pre-Construction
- • Before humans arrive, robotic systems will prepare the settlement site.
- • AI-driven autonomous construction vehicles will 3D-print habitats using Martian regolith, reducing the need for Earth-imported materials.
- • Drones and robotic scouts will map terrain, construct landing pads, and establish solar farms.
##  Long-Term Pressurized Cities
- • Future Martian cities will move underground or into lava tubes to mitigate radiation exposure.
- • Architectural concepts propose geodesic domes with transparent aerogel layers, allowing sunlight while maintaining insulation.
- • These cities may feature centralized hubs for research, commerce, and social interaction, like modern Antarctic research bases.
- • Artificial ecosystems will be integrated, including controlled agriculture zones and enclosed parks.
## Sustaining Human Life on Mars
For a Martian colony to survive long-term, it must generate its own resources rather than rely on costly Earth resupply missions. Three primary challenges must be addressed:
##  Greenhouses and Hydroponic Agriculture
- • Hydroponics and aeroponics will be the primary methods for food production, requiring minimal soil and water.
- • LED-lighted underground farms could produce crops year-round, including wheat, potatoes, algae, and spirulina (high-protein food sources).
- • Advanced bioregenerative life support systems (BLSS) will recycle CO₂ exhaled by humans into oxygen via photosynthetic algae and cyanobacteria.
##  Psychological Well-Being in Isolation
- • The mental health of settlers is a key challenge, given prolonged isolation and distance from Earth.
- • AI-driven companionship (robotic assistants and virtual reality interactions) could mitigate loneliness.
- • Simulated "Earth-like" environments, such as indoor gardens, artificial rivers, and recreation centers, will be crucial for morale.
##  Artificial Magnetosphere Proposals to Mitigate Radiation
- • Mars lacks a strong magnetic field, exposing settlers to cosmic rays and solar radiation.
- • NASA has proposed a large artificial magnetic shield positioned at Mars’ Lagrange Point L1, which could restore some atmospheric retention and reduce radiation levels.
- • On a smaller scale, shielded habitats with water or regolith layers will protect residents.
## Mars Governance Models
The question of how Mars will be governed remains unresolved. Traditional Earthbased political systems may not be suited for the challenges of space governance, resource allocation, and interplanetary law.
##  Legal Implications of Planetary Sovereignty
- • According to the Outer Space Treaty (1967), no nation can claim sovereignty over extraterrestrial land.
- • However, private corporations, such as SpaceX, Blue Origin, or international coalitions, may seek de facto governance rights over Martian settlements.
- • New Martian laws will be required to regulate property rights, crime, citizenship, and political representation.
##  Potential for a "Martian Republic" or Corporate-Led Governance
- • It has been suggested that Mars should be governed democratically, potentially as a direct-participation society where all laws expire unless periodically renewed.
- • Others propose corporate governance models, where companies own infrastructure and enforce their own regulations.
- • A hybrid model, involving Earth-based space agencies (NASA, ESA, CNSA) and private stakeholders, may be necessary in the early settlement phases.
## Mars Economy: The Challenges of an Off-World Financial System
For a Martian settlement to become self-sustaining, it must develop an independent economy that allows trade and production beyond Earth's supply chains.
##  Viability of Off-World Industries
• Asteroid mining: Mars’ proximity to the Asteroid Belt makes it an ideal hub for extracting rare metals (e.g., platinum, gold, nickel).
- • Martian exports: In the early phases, Mars will be reliant on Earth for materials but could later export minerals, scientific patents, and low-gravity manufacturing products.
- • Bioengineering for space adaptation: Advances in genetic modification and pharmaceuticals tailored for low-gravity environments could drive a specialized biotech industry.
- • Space tourism: Once infrastructure is in place, wealthy individuals may pay millions for an interplanetary experience, boosting the Martian economy.
 Cryptocurrency and Decentralized Financial Systems in a Multiplanet
Economy
- • Traditional banking systems will be inefficient due to Mars-Earth communication delays (up to 24 minutes one way).
- • Cryptocurrency-based financial systems could enable rapid transactions independent of Earth-based financial institutions.
- • The introduction of a Martian credit system may help establish a functional local economy.
## Ethical and Philosophical Considerations
- • Planetary Protection: Preventing contamination of potential Martian life.
- • Human rights on Mars: Labor rights, property laws, and social structures.
- • Ethical dilemmas of terraforming: Do we have the right to alter another planet?
- • Religious and cultural transformations in an interplanetary society.
## Future Projections and Conclusion
Humanity is on the brink of a new era of exploration and expansion — one that extends beyond Earth’s boundaries. The establishment of a self-sustaining human settlement on Mars represents not only a monumental technological challenge but also a profound transformation in our understanding of civilization, identity, and survival. While significant obstacles remain, the next few decades will be crucial in determining whether humanity can truly become a multiplanet species.
## Timeline for Human Settlement: 2030–2050
While precise predictions are difficult, current technological trends suggest the following phased timeline for Mars colonization:
Phase I: Preparatory Missions (2026–2035)
• Robotic reconnaissance missions: NASA, ESA, China’s CNSA, and SpaceX will continue mapping terrain, testing oxygen production (MOXIE), and identifying water resources.
- • Uncrewed cargo missions: Starship-class vessels will deliver habitat modules, solar panels, and life-support systems to pre-selected landing sites.
- • Autonomous construction: AI-driven robots will prepare landing zones, build infrastructure, and set up 3D-printed structures using Martian regolith.
- Phase II: First Human Missions (2035–2040)
- • Initial human crews land on Mars: Comprising a mix of astronauts, engineers, and medical personnel, the first teams will establish base operations, test life support systems, and conduct ISRU experiments.
- • Experimental greenhouses: Agricultural trials will begin to determine which crops can be sustained under Martian conditions.
- • First permanent structures: Lava tubes and reinforced habitats will be utilized for long-term settlement.
- Phase III: Early Colonization (2040–2050)
- • Regular Earth-Mars transit: Starship and future interplanetary vehicles will enable a continuous supply of personnel and resources.
- • Expansion of the settlement: Small Martian communities will emerge, with laboratories, energy farms, and resource extraction facilities.
- • Mars economy begins: Industries such as asteroid mining, biotech, and tourism will take root.
- • First generations of Mars-born humans: The physiological and psychological effects of growing up in a 0.38g gravity environment will be studied.
By 2050, the first true Martian society may be taking shape, transitioning from a research outpost to a self-sustaining, independent colony.
## The Role of AI and Automation in Interplanetary Logistics
The survival of a Martian colony will depend on efficient supply chains, infrastructure maintenance, and resource allocation—challenges that AI and robotics will play a vital role in addressing.
 AI-Managed Logistics & Transportation
- • Autonomous cargo delivery: AI will schedule, track, and optimize Earth-Mars shipments, ensuring a continuous flow of critical supplies.
- • Orbital relay satellites: AI-driven communication networks will maintain realtime data exchange despite the 20+-minute signal delay between Earth and Mars.
 Robotic Infrastructure Development
• AI-powered 3D printing will construct buildings using locally sourced materials, reducing the need for imported components.
• Autonomous rovers and drones will maintain solar farms, inspect habitats, and repair damaged systems without human intervention.
 AI in Human Life Support Systems
- • AI medical diagnostics: With limited access to doctors, AI will assist in remote surgeries, health monitoring, and emergency triage.
- • Psychological support AI: Long-term isolation on Mars presents serious mental health risks—AI-driven virtual assistants and companionship systems may help maintain morale.
By the time Mars hosts a significant human population, AI and robotics will not only assist daily life, but they will also likely be integrated into the very fabric of Martian society.
## Are We Ready for Post-Earth Civilization?
The prospect of a self-sufficient human colony beyond Earth raises fundamental questions about our preparedness, both technologically and culturally.
 Technological Readiness While noteworthy progress has been made, we still need breakthroughs in:
- • Radiation shielding: Long-term exposure to cosmic rays remains a major health risk.
- • Sustainable agriculture: Current greenhouse experiments must scale up to support large populations.
- • Interplanetary governance: No clear framework exists for managing Mars politically and legally.
##  Psychological and Societal Readiness
- • Are humans adaptable enough? Long-term isolation and a harsh environment could create psychological distress and social fragmentation.
- • Cultural evolution: Will Martians still consider themselves "Earthlings," or will they develop a distinct Martian identity?
- • Ethical dilemmas: How will laws and justice function in a society millions of kilometers away from Earth?
While we are making rapid advancements, true readiness for a post-Earth civilization may require new paradigms in human psychology, ethics, and governance.
## Philosophical Implications: How Would Becoming a Multiplanet Species Change Our Self-Identity?
For the first time in history, humanity faces the possibility of becoming a multiplanet civilization transformation as profound as the discovery of the heliocentric model or Darwinian evolution.
 Redefining "Home"
- • Earth has always been humanity’s only home, but as we expand, will Mars and other planets become just as meaningful?
- • Future generations born on Mars may never see Earth. Will they feel connected to their ancestral home, or will they develop an independent identity?
 New Ethical Paradigms
- • Will space settlements prioritize individual freedoms or collective survival?
- • Should Mars settlers modify their DNA to better adapt to the planet, fundamentally altering human evolution?
- • Does humanity have a moral right to Mars’ territorium, or should we preserve it in its natural state?
 The Next Evolutionary Leap
- • Moving beyond Earth is not just a physical transition; it is an evolutionary leap.
- • Just as Neanderthals gave way to Homo sapiens, future Mars settlers could become a new branch of humanity—Homo Martis.
