Andrew Targowski
Former President, ISCSC andrew.targowski@wmich.edu Abstract
The purpose of this article is to synthesize those crises in the unfolding of Western civilization that are controlled by human knowledge and wisdom. The methodology is transdisciplinary, utilizing a cognitive science-oriented, big-picture view of civilization and main conflicts. The crises are investigated across three time periods; the Dark Ages (from the fifth to the fourteenth century), Modern Times (from the fifteenth to the twentieth century), and the present century. The findings reveal that today Western civilization faces seventeen significant crises. Knowledge is not sufficient to solve these crises; we need to employ knowledge-based wisdom. This approach presents a new understanding of how wisdom can be employed to lead to sustainable civilization.
## Introduction
This study aims to evaluate how human minds, advancing knowledge, and wisdom have impacted the development of civilization after the fall of the Roman Empire. Civilization is understood here to comprise three subsystems: human, natural (the ecosystem), and the state or level of civilization. The internal system of civilization rests upon the following three dimensions:
- • Society: organized humans in the pursuit of civilization; it is an existence-driven community.
- • Culture: a value and symbols-driven continuous process and product, developing as patterned human behaviors, feelings, and reactions.
- • Infrastructure: a technology-driven additive process of acquiring and applying material means. It is mined, processed, and distributed throughout civilization.
Based on these attributes, the composite definition of civilization is as follows (Targowski 2009a):
Civilization is a large society living in an autonomous, fuzzy reification (invisible-visible) that is not a part of a larger one and exists over an extended period. It specializes in labor and is differentiated from other civilizations by developing its advanced cultural system driven by communication, religion, wealth, and power. It also shares the same knowledge/wisdom system within complex urban, agricultural, and industrial information. It also progresses in rising, growing, declining, and falling cycles.

A short definition of a specific civilization (like the Western or Chinese ones1) is as follows:
A civilization is a complex societal organism of compatibly interactive entities of religion, society, culture(s), and infrastructure in a large frame of territory and time, usually embracing several nations and centuries/millennia. It has its past, present, and future state.
The development and actions of any civilization are controlled by the human ability to solve problems and participate in socio-political activities. This ability is supported by human cognition, and it is based on mental processing (including memorizing, thinking, and deciding) at a level much above that of the animals. The most important result of this kind of ability is wisdom, which is one of the units of cognition.

## The Cognitive Science-Oriented Approach Towards Civilizations and Wisdom
Cognitive science is the interdisciplinary study of mind and intelligence, embracing philosophy, psychology, artificial intelligence, neuroscience, linguistics, anthropology, economics, sociology, and informing technology2 (Fig.2).
The cognitive sciences started as an intellectual movement in the 1950s, often referred to as the cognitive revolution. Its initial goal was to arrive at a better comprehension of the mind and learning. As access to computers increased in the 1970s and early 1980s, artificial intelligence (A.I.) research expanded. Researchers such as Marvin Minsky would write computer programs to understand human thought better and also in the hope of creating artificial minds. The late 80s and 90s saw the rise of neural networks and connectionism as a research paradigm. In the present period, there is a trend to replace human work with A.I.

## The First Great Crisis of Civilization in the Current Era – Two Minds-Oriented Wisdom
The First Great Crisis of Civilization took place in the aftermath of the fall of the Roman Empire in 476 CE and it lasted close to a thousand years, until the Italian Renaissance (the 15th century).
The fall was a result of the moral decay of the Roman elite and the invasions by the barbarians (e.g., Vandals) of Northern Europe, who served in the Roman legions operating on the periphery of the empire, and who rebelled and invaded the capital of the polity they served.
It brought about the period of the so-called Dark Ages, as the removal of Rome's strong, central government left Europe in Chaos. The mighty empire was fragmented into small kingdoms, and in many places, rule was by local lords, each of whom exercised power only near his castle. It was a time of poverty and hardship, with the lack of wealth and consequently of people able to function as patrons; as a result, there was a decline in learning.
In the Middle East, however, learning flourished; the religion of Islam was to prove a unifying force. In Asia and the Americas, however, complex independent civilizations flourished.
The basic mind of most of the society and three or four minds of the small universityeducated elite impacted civilization in the Middle Ages as follows [(Krawczyk and Targowski (2019)]:
RELIGION – dogmas encountered by reasoning in the quest for universal knowledge and wisdom.
- • Islam – Muhammad was born in 570 in Mecca in Arabia; he brought about the Koran, the holy book of Islam, a monistic religion with about a billion followers today; the religion strongly competes in societal wisdom with other faiths. He insists that it was not his teaching but the word of God, whose prophet and mouthpiece he was.
- • The Crusades – eight military campaigns were launched from the West to liberate Palestine, where Jesus was born, from Muslims.
- • Great Schism of 1054 - was the break of unity between what are now the Catholic Church and the Eastern Orthodox churches, which has lasted since the 11th century. The main differences are understanding the Holy Spirit and enforcing clerical celibacy.
- • Disaccord – between secular monarchies and the Church to control the attitude and way of thinking of people.
SOCIETY - practical and pragmatic basic knowledge and common-sense wisdom challenged by the hardship of life.
- • The feudal system – was based on granting land by an influential person to a less powerful man in return for service. It ranged from a king through his barons to lesser nobles and finally to the serfs.
- • Innovations – The tree-field system of crop rotation (the most crucial agriculture innovation in Europe at those times) became generalized. The energy of animals was mastered through the domestication of horses (with the use of horseshoes, stirrups, and shoulder collars) — as water and wind power were harnessed by humans. The water mill became popular. The chimney and stained glass were perfected. The progress made in the use of metals, iron, and steel, was vast.
- • Navigation Science – Henry the Navigator (1394 -1460) founded the School of Navigation about 1418 in Sagres, Portugal. In this school, students were educated in instruments-driven navigation, map-making, and science, to sail down the west coast of Africa and later to America and China. Due to this theoretical knowledge, Portugal became an empire, which lasted about five hundred years, until 1974.
- • Trade – since roads were in a bad state, trade was conducted by sea and rivers. In Northern Europe, the countries around the Baltic Sea produced grain, timber, fish, and furs, while wool and cloth were essential in England.
Many goods arrived at the great international markets at Bruges and Antwerp in the Netherlands. Galleys came from Venice bringing wine, olives, fruit, and salt from warm Mediterranean countries. They also brought luxuries from the East, including silk, jewels, and spices used for preserving food. The merchants traveled in convoys, never at night, for fear of robbers.
- • Black Death was a fearful epidemic of the bubonic plague that caused havoc throughout Europe. The outbreak started in China and spread along the trade routes to the west, carried by fleas on rats. Altogether about twenty-five million Europeans died – about a quarter of the continent's population. The result was to strengthen the position of modest workers and to accelerate the end of the feudal system on which medieval society was based.
- • Wars – there were about twenty categories of wars in those times. The Mongols' invasion of Europe in 1237 gave birth to the Mongol Empire, which embraced parts of Asia and lasted till 1700. Their success was due to the wisdom of organizing military force in small fast-mobile units, which outmaneuvered the European teams with heavy weapons and slowed them. The Hundred Years' War (1337-1453) between England and France was a series of wars about the claim of English kings to the throne of France. The quest for resources and splendor for the rulers drove the wisdom of these wars.
CULTURE – influenced and sponsored by kings and landlords, reflecting the successful life of the upper class and teaching of the organized religion.
• Literature – was dominated at first by stories in verse, the sagas of the Vikings, and the romances of kinghood and chivalry such as the legends of King Arthur and Charlemagne. Shorter lyric poetry grew up in the later Middle Ages, with Italy's Dante Alighieri and Petrarch.
- • Painters – created realistic figures and used bright colors under the influence of Byzantine art. Monks produced illustrated manuscripts. But artists had not yet fully mastered the technique of perspective.
- • Music – the plainsong or Church chant dominated the simple melodic music of the time, but gradually composers began to put two or more melodies together, composing polyphonic (many voices) music.
INFRASTRUCTURE – reflecting upper class taste and adapting to the developing trade and guilds in cities, reflecting the practical experience (knowledge) in building and organizing communities wisely, thus supporting the power of the upper class and professionals:
- • Architecture – a style called Romanesque was developed about 1000; buildings were massive, with sturdy pillars and rounded arches. During the 1100s, a lighter style evolved, now known as Gothic. Pointed arches and slender pillars in cathedrals gave an appearance of grace and elaborate decoration.
- • Cities – they grew due to the development of crafts, centers of trade (guilds), and money circulation, which boosted the mining of silver and gold for coins.
The hardship and complex life in the Dark Ages required realistic (natural), pragmatic and technical approaches to solving daily challenges by applying common-sense wisdom. Eventually, it led to abstract rationalism and fostered the development of the law as a practical discipline dedicated to universal solutions. That abstract rationalism was also behind the mathematical computations of the Arabs, Mayans, and the Indians, whose significant achievements were quickly taken up in China and Europe.
The same combination of pragmatism and abstraction also lay behind philosophy, triggering the desire to master the world through philosophical concepts. Intellectuals, primarily philosophers, influenced the future revolutions in Europe and the Americas. Human experience in the West reached new sophistication, complexity, and expansiveness (Tarnas 1991:220).
Why did humanity survive and emerge from the First Great Crisis of Civilization in our era? Because in the Dark Ages, the debate between faith and reason steadily prioritized reason and practical, common-sense knowledge and wisdom. In the next five hundred years, this process led to the evolution from religion to science and the rapid growth of civilization as the product of multi-minds-oriented knowledge and wisdom, as illustrated in Figure 5.
What can we learn from the Dark Ages that is of help today? It is that realistic and pragmatic common-sense wisdom can solve complex problems generated by the BASIC MIND.

## The Rapid Development of Modern Civilization from the 15th to the 20th Century Based on Multiple Minds – Oriented Wisdom.
Modern civilization, which has been developing in the last five hundred years, is the outcome of an extraordinary convergence of events, ideas, and figures which established advanced civilization but is too advanced to sustain itself in the endless race for higher productivity, effectiveness, and well-being. Its knowledge and wisdom will be evaluated as follows in a very synthesized manner:
RELIGION – entered the stage of the open clashes and wars, applying terrorism with the intolerance strategy. Roman Catholic Christianity has issues with internal moral and power dimensions, problems in a demanding society looking for a clean attitude.
- • Reformation – 1517 – The monk Martin Luther nailed a protest of the sale of indulgences (pardons) to the door of a church in Wittenberg. It eventually led to Protestantism, a simplified version of Roman Catholicism, based on a minimal hierarchy, the cult of work, and the simple life. The West has widely accepted it and has achieved the highest standard of living globally. Undoubtedly, this success had been due to good (wise) decision-making.
- • Science and religion – Early scientists were believers, even professional clergy like Copernicus. With the rise of the U.S. as a modern republic, the church and state are separate and act via legally defined agreement and tolerance. Pope John Paul II (1920-2005) even stated that there is no conflict between religion and science and specified that the evolution of humankind was and is controlled by God.
- • Compromise and conflict – The main organized religions like Christianity, Islam, and Judaism now live in a form of peace, but there have been clashes and wars not only in the past but in the 21st century, too. These conflicts and wars demand irrational and emotional decision-making, not predictive of humankind's promising (wise) future.
SOCIETY – in the 21st century, society is more knowledgeable but confused due to the development of the post-modern mind.
- • Renaissance – due to printing (since Gutenberg’s movable type, 1454), the development and dissemination of ideas has boosted the questioning of established theories in religion, art, and science. Scholarship began to develop independently of the church, and the human rather than divine in life and art was emphasized; the well-rounded individual became an ideal. The truth and reason led to wise decision-making and the Reformation (enhanced religion).
- • Scientific Revolution (since the 15th century) - born in Poland and educated in Italy, Nicolaus Copernicus (1473-1543), using the scientific graphic and mathematical apparatuses, negated the church's teaching that the Sun goes around the Earth. He has scientifically proven the intuitive Ptolemaic model of this claim.
Unfortunately, Giordano Bruno (1548-1600) lost his life for pursuing the Copernicus model, while Kepler improved the mathematics of the Copernicus model. Still, Galileo Galilei (1564-1642) could not work on it due to the church's order.
Eventually, Isaac Newton (1643-1727) provided the solid foundation for cosmology and modern physics advanced, represented by Albert Einstein (gravity), Maria Sklodowska-Curie (radiation), Werner Heisenberg (quantum physics), and James Maxwell (electromagnetism) in the 20th century. Science led to the development of the atomic bomb, nuclear power stations, computers (20th century) and Wi-Fi smartphones (in the 21st century).
- • Enlightenment - a European intellectual movement of the late 17th and 18th centuries, this highlighted reason and individualism rather than tradition. It was profoundly impacted by 17th-century philosophers such as Descartes and Locke and physicists like Newton, and its prominent members are Kant, Goethe, Voltaire, Rousseau, and Adam Smith. It led to the development of the Western open mind, enabling the making of knowledgeable and wise decisions.
- • Philosophy moved from religion to science, strongly influenced by the Scientific Revolution. Francis Bacon (1561-1626) followed Aristotelian ideas, promoting an empirical, inductive approach known as the scientific method, which is the basis of modern scientific inquiry. He argued that the true philosopher directly approaches the natural world and studies it without falsely anticipating and prejudicing the outcome. René Descartes (1596 -1650) published a book La Method, thus defining the scientific problem-solving method: first doubting everything there was to challenge; second, dividing a big problem into small ones; and third, solving each one. It leads to realism and pragmatism based on real-life issues, which eventually can lead to wise decisions.
- • Societal Revolutions – English (1688) – parliamentary democracy; American (1763 -1789) – modern republic; French (1789 -1799) - citizenship, equality, and freedom; Spring of Nations (1848) – liberalizing absolute monarchies; Mexican (1910 -1920) – constitutional republic; Bolshevik (1917) – social equality (at the cost of 94 million dead); and Polish (1980 -89) – anticommunistic dictatorship.
- • Industrial Revolution – since 1820 the invention of steam, diesel engines, and electricity led to the factory system and mechanization of work, providing more time for education, and generating widespread thinking about the society's wellbeing based on justice and equal access to resources.
- • Technological Revolution (20th century) – cars, aircraft, medications, computers, and networks led to better information processing and communication for better awareness and wise decision-making.
- • Rebirth of Germany and World Wars I and II – united Germany in 1870 [after almost 1053 years of the (Germanic) Holy Roman Empire, from 753 to 1806].
- There followed the waging of wars for more resources for the German "higher" race. It was the example set by Ferdinand II's thoughtless ambition, culminating in Adolf Hitler's blind racism, leading to about 110 million dead in both wars.
- • Information Wave – since 1980, when the quiet and creeping Revolution of Microcomputers led to mass informatization and the development of the Internet
(1983).
- • Globalization Wave – in the 21st century, the developed countries decline, and developing countries go up, as the e-global economy where Western businesses outsource manufacturing and minimize the middle class and its discretionary income. It is short-term-oriented, long-term stupidity.
- • Virtualization Wave (21st century) – the massive application of smartphones among the young generation moves the users from reality to virtuality, leading to losing skills for living despite practical skills in a pseudo-friendly virtuality. The freedom of expression in virtuality leads to direct democracy characterized by chaos.
- • Robotization Wave (21st century) – the naïve trend of applying artificial intelligence leads to a labor-free economy and a communistic society living on financial help from the government to pay bills. Education will be unnecessary, useful mainly for developing and maintaining robots. It may be the end of humanity as we know it today.
CULTURE – after long and prosperous cultural development, Western culture is reaching an uncomprehending level of fragmented culture with the tendency to apply vulgarized culture. This may be illustrated as follows (Targowski 2015:156-157):
- • High culture - the set of cultural products and services, in the arts (painting, music, theatre, and opera), held in the highest esteem by a culture. It is the culture of an upper class such as an aristocracy, plutocracy, or the intelligentsia.
- • Folk Culture – local values, fashion, music, crafts, customs, food dishes, and drinks of the lower-class people. This forms the counterparts to the concurrently developing high culture of the landlords and wealthy merchants. It is rooted in regional/national identity.
- • Popular culture is the totality of ideas, perspectives, attitudes, memes, images, and other phenomena that have been within the mainstream of Western culture from the beginning of the 20th century through the dawn of the 21st century. The mass media profoundly impacts pop culture. An example is television, which sets the ways of acting.
- • Mass culture –a commercial culture, produced for mass consumption and driven by mass media. It is the marketing machine. It may be compared to American and West European cultures. Its goal is to provide large markets for products and services that can (or should) be consumed as broadly as possible. This, as a result, brings significant profits to big business. Mass culture faces heavy disapproval from religious and oldtimer groups which perceive it as shallow, consumerist, melodramatic, and degraded. This culture is, in some contexts, a synonym for popular culture.
- • Pop culture – conveys non-traditional forms of popular culture where one such form is counterculture. Pop culture became fashionable in the second part of the 20th century and the early 21st century. Since the 1950s, one can distinguish such periods of social change as the Big Band era, the Rock era, the Beatles era, the Hippie era, the Woodstock era, the Disco era, the Punk era, the Hip-hop era, the Video era, the Rapper era, the Sexuality & Drug era, the TwitterFacebook era, the Computer Games era, the Casual Dress era and so forth. Some of these eras take place concurrently.
- • Global culture – this includes recognizable symbols like “CNN,” “The Wall Street Journal,” “Martini,” and values; instant electronic communications (iphones, e‐mail), the Internet, the “dollar/euro” in international businesses and banks, and behavior; speaking English (“Globish”) and the Western dress code. It is a new layer applied during professional (business, education, sport, artistic) dealings with people abroad or at home. In effect, global culture makes the world a “global village,” thus “shrinking” every day.
- • Middle culture – the adapted values of assimilated immigrants, symbols, and behavior from the country's national culture where they have settled. It is a blend of at least two cultures or even more if a family has multicultural roots.
- • The modern mind (born after Enlightenment) – is based on understanding the natural order and bending that order to mankind’s benefit through intellectual independence from the former, medieval sense of contingency upon God (Tarnas 1991:319-320).
- • Post-Modern Mind (in the 21st century) – is based on the awareness of reality as being multiple, local, and temporal, and without verifiable foundation (Tarnas 1991:401). It applies relativism, skepticism, pluralism, complexity, and ambiguity, leading to a potentially new vision with disappointment in the current situation.
When the truth is relative depending on an individual’s experience and attitude, it leads to the decline of the intellectual class, which is perceived as those who cannot solve anything rationally, without tension and confusion. Apparently, the post-modern mind is against the uncontrollable development of technology which conquers humanity.
INFRASTRUCTURE – in the 21st century, the infrastructure defines culture and humanity, particularly when moving to global and virtual settings. People are communicated with, but are they wiser?
- • Transportation –humans are highly mobile and have even reached the Moon. We plan to settle on Mars, which will require advanced theoretical knowledge and wise decisions to avoid catastrophes of cosmic proportions.
- • Info-Communication – reached its peak in the 21st century in the sense of global reach and hope that it will revolutionize the world as printed books did since
1454. So far, there are also many negative side-effects, including cybercrime, wars, and hate culture intensification worldwide.
- • Military – nuclear and other weapons of mass destruction, drone-oriented battles and terrorism bring the question of the ethics of wars. The planned use of military in space (by the U.S.) should also be considered here.
- • Health – infrastructure is well developed in the Western Civilization, where the lifespan is three times longer than it was a thousand years ago.
- • Education – in the Western and Eastern civilizations is very well developed, where the literacy level is close to 75 - 99% of the population, and college education has reached 30% of the population. It means that every third person has at least five minds or more.
Modern Civilization faces a great crisis in the 21st century, one which most people feel but do not see at first glance. ## 4. The crisis of ecology - originates from the overpopulation of the planet and is the result of a civilization that destroys the natural environment. Overpopulation and wasteful lifestyles mean that the footprint is too big to keep the environment intact. Note: “Footprint” is a measure of the land and water needed by man to stay alive and the land required for buildings and roads, plus the air for breathing that can absorb the CO2 emitted.
The size of the footprint changes over time as it depends on the dynamics in the civilization (its development and fall). This measure assesses how fast people consume resources and generate waste compared to how fast nature can absorb our waste and how fast it can recreate its added resources.
According to recent estimates (2010), civilization now uses 1.5 of the planet's resources and waste absorption capacity. It means that one-third of the consumption draws upon “spare” Earth resources. In other words, we are taking these resources from the planet's inventory, which we cannot replenish to the initial levels. U.N. estimates imply that if the current trend of population expansion and consumption of resources continues, around the year 2030, civilization will need two Earth-like planets to assist its day-to-day operation. In 2050 we will need as many as three planets like ours to keep civilization going at the current quality of life levels (Global Footprint Network 2012).
It is appalling that within 20 years, we will need an extra planet if we have two already. In 2070, given today's pace of civilization-based life, another world will be required, and yet another by 2090. By the end of the 21st century, assuming continued population growth, we will need five planets to sustain our civilization, an apparent illusion, and self-deceit.
The footprint is a measurement of an individual's activities, city, region, or state and aspects of socio-economic life. It is estimated that in 2007 one person had a footprint of 2.7 global hectares (gha), whereas the ecosystem footprint capacity was 2.1 gha. An average inhabitant of the Earth used 30% more (renewable) bio-resources than nature could provide, which has been possible as the inhabitant uses reserve bio-resources, which is of a character that we will not be able to renew (Ecological Footprint 2012).
The part played by civilization in forming the footprint can best be seen at the country level. The rich countries, such as the United States, Qatar, or Kuwait, need 8-10 gha, whereas the poorest countries like Haiti, Somalia, or Afghanistan need below one gha (Ecological Footprint 2012). Poland uses 4.3 gha, with the ecosystem allowing 2.1 gha, which means that a statistical Pole lives twice beyond their ecological means and does so by borrowing reserve stocks of bioresources by using the additional -2.26 gha (108%) of eco-reserve bioresources (Ecological Footprint 2012). This comparison implies that the wealthiest countries function thanks to the interception of ecosystems from other countries, usually the poorest – they go on at their expense.
The paradox of the situation is that the least developed nations will be able to survive the possible fall of the advanced civilization as we know them, a highly likely development. Their civilizations are the least complicated and the least resource consuming.
- 5. The crisis of strategic resources depletion - modern power needs for advanced societies entail the extensive use of non-renewable resources such as oil, gas, coal, and uranium. Several estimates demonstrate that these resources are running out fast. The peak of oil production was reached in 2005, but in 2011 it was even more significant (Almeida and Silva 2016). There have been no essential discoveries of new oil deposits to make up for the growing demand.
Although the oil demand is high but well catered for, it is estimated that the resources will have run out soon. Advanced drilling techniques, including fracking, mean that 60% to 80% of the extant deposits are being hauled away instead of the mere 40% so far. Still, the depletion of our fossil fuels is inevitable, whether within 40, 50, or 80 years.
The American Petroleum Institute estimates that between 2062 and 2094, oil reserves will have become exhausted (ZME Science 2018). The price of oil will grow as its drilling techniques become increasingly advanced and expensive. The same is in stock for natural gas, uranium, and coal resources. The first two will suffice for the next 40-50 years of automated civilization. Coal deposits are more significant and can suffice for 134 years (B.P. Global 2018).
The answer to the depletion of sources of energy has been a hope that human knowledge will provide answers to the issue of the automation of civilization. Indeed, humanity has engineered elaborate renewable energy solutions based on wind power, solar radiation, and biofuels. The large-scale application of this type of resource is being slowed down by the strategies of oil corporations, which manipulate the prices of oil or gas and can jeopardize the viability of renewable energy projects. It is how the development of electric cars was once prevented in a bid to uphold the monopoly and a great business based on fossil fuel energy.
Small, too, are the resources of non-ferrous metals, whose most significant deposits are in China. Non-ferrous metals are indispensable in producing electronic devices, the most characteristic products of the automated civilization. They control the systems that secure the automation of civilization.
When these metals are gone, robotic civilization can stop being automated. As can be seen from the problems with energy policy, man can secure the comfort of automated life thanks to knowledge.
However, man is not wise and effective enough in social actions to introduce renewable energy as a priority based on an appropriate regulatory policy. When this is necessary, it might be too late to sustain civilization at a level suitable for most of its people.
