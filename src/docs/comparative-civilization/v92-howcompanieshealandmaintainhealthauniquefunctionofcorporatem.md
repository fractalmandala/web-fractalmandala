Aki Kinjo Faculty of Intercultural Studies, Gakushuin Women’s College Tokyo, Japan aki.kinjo2012@gmail.com Abstract
Capitalism is embedded in our civilization. Despite its faults, weaknesses, and sins, modern civilization cannot prosper without capitalism. Companies are the engine of capitalism. Yet, just like humans, companies make mistakes, sometimes gross. How do companies overcome unforgivable mistakes? This paper argues that corporate museums in Japan play an important role in helping companies heal from their sufferings, recover, and maintain organizational health.
I deploy a cultural approach to management, analyzing corporate museums as a platform for conveying explicit and hidden narratives. A cultural approach to Japanese management helps us understand why Japan has so many corporate museums, well over one hundred. Japanese corporate museums are platforms for marketing and selfpromotion. They also function as temples and shrines that represent transcendence, implying that Japanese capitalism is not as simplistic as its Anglo-Saxon counterpart, which is driven by insatiable greed.

Since Per Hansen argued the limits of archival research and the relevance of studying narratives, they have been largely accepted as an important subject to research in the study of business history10. Narratives are defined as “basic instruments for ordering reality, assigning causality, and constructing meaning,” 11 and are “shared and collective, not just individual stories.”12 Narratives are ways of working the past to identify who we are and what we wish to be. Narratives help articulate what the company and its constituents think or wish to think of themselves. Because narratives are stories that are told, they are difficult to derive from focusing solely on corporate documents. Narratives are not necessarily factual; rather they are statements of what the company chooses to present.
Although it is accepted that narratives are integral in conducting research in business history, the discussions regarding narratives have remained theoretical and abstract.
Where to search, identify, analyse, and compare narratives in companies remains an open question. Corporate museums play a significant role in consciously transmitting narratives within the company and to the outside world. I argue that corporate museums can become a primary source to investigate narratives through methodological research.
Narratives play a supplementary role to corporate documents. I argue that narratives also play a vital role for companies to heal from wounds caused by their mistakes and maintain their organizational health. The healing effect of narratives is particularly important in hidden narratives. In that regard, corporate museums can be a place to heal by establishing and conveying narratives.
There are two conditions, however, to maximize the value of corporate museums as a primary source to investigate narratives.
First, the research on corporate museums should accompany an international comparison of similar corporate museums in a different country or region. Investigating a museum on a standalone basis risks failure to the researcher if focusing only on the explicit narrative. In addition to the explicit narrative, there may be a story in which the company intentionally or unintentionally omitted information present in the museum: the hidden narrative.
Hidden narratives can be more important than the explicit narrative to achieve a nuanced understanding of the company because it is a story that the company has decided to airbrush. Hidden narratives, compared with explicit narratives, are not only difficult to identify but can be overshadowed by the explicit narrative.

That is because explicit narratives are well crafted. The company has invested substantial resources to convey a powerful and convincing message designed to mesmerize the public.
Companies in the same industry and country are likely to share similar pasts. Therefore, in these instances, simply comparing corporate museums becomes a challenge in identifying and unveiling narratives which companies have, in effect, intentionally decided to forget.13 Using corporate museums of a similar company in a different country makes it much easier to highlight the differences.
Leveraging corporate museums as a primary source to investigate narratives does not diminish the importance of using corporate documents. The narrative conveyed at the corporate museum should be recognized as one narrative among others. Therefore, analyzing corporate museums needs to be combined with orthodox archival research. That leads us to the second condition to effectively use company museums as a primary source of research: verifying narratives against company documents.
Ideally, business historians should have full access to the corporate archives which possess complete sets of original historical sources. But such conditions are rarely met. Often companies — especially Japanese — do not have archives that meet the rigors of academic research. Even when they do, full access is limited. In turn, often companies have internal and external publications relevant to their corporate history that can substitute for archival research. Fortunately, many of them are accessible to business historians as well as the public,14
Investigating narratives at the corporate museum is only the start. Combining archival research with international comparative research of corporate museums is critically important to verify the explicit narrative and to understand the hidden narratives.
The contribution of corporate museums can be maximized by combining detailed research on documents from corporate archives. Investigating corporate museums and documents is critically important because museums may exhibit and convey selected — explicit or hidden — narratives of the company. There is no guarantee that these narratives accurately or comprehensively represent the history of the company. Therefore, it is the responsibility of the business historian to verify narratives with corporate documents to achieve a holistic picture of the company’s history.

Since corporate museums can reveal hidden as well as explicit narratives of the company, they enable the researcher to propel a nuanced understanding of the corporation in its entirety. To validate this argument, I conduct a case study in this article.
This paper compares the corporate museums of Volvo and Isuzu, both of which are medium sized automobile companies with similar characteristics, one in Sweden and the other in Japan, respectively.

Volvo also supplies marine and industrial drive systems and financial services. In 1999, Volvo Cars, which focused on manufacturing, was sold to the Ford Motor Company.

The Volvo Museum, the corporate museum of Volvo Car Group and Volvo Group (including Volvo Trucks, Volvo Buses, Volvo Penta, and Volvo Construction Equipment), was established in 1995. ## The Display of Volvo’s Foundation and Development
- At the beginning of the museum tour, statues of founders Assar Gabrielson and Gustav Larson are exhibited as a symbol of the beginning of Swedish car manufacturing company. These two founders both worked for SKF, a Swedish bearing manufacturer, before founding Volvo in 1927. At that time, SKF possessed numerous patents and advanced technological capabilities in bearings, which are also components of automobiles, and supplied bearings to automobile companies in other countries. And that is introduced as the background that led to the concept of “making a Swedish car.”
Volvo's first car, the ÖV4, is also on display at the beginning of the museum tour. According to documents, in 1926, nine prototypes of the ÖV4 were built as open-top vehicles, and 205 ÖV4s had been produced by 1929. At the same time, Volvo was also producing the PV4, a saloon version of the ÖV4. The PV4 is also on display at the museum. But, at that time, Volvo did not have a high reputation as an automobile manufacturer, so passenger cars such as “ÖV4” and “PV4” did not sell well. On the other hand, the production of commercial vehicles such as buses and trucks supported the company's management. For these reasons, the company's earliest buses such as “LV4” and “LV651” are also on display at the museum.
In addition to the episodes of Volvo in its foundation period, the museum also introduced the development of Volvo after World War II. The exhibition of “PV444,” which was born in 1946, is a symbol of this. Sweden remained neutral during World War II, but its neighbors were devastated by the war. Seeing this situation, Volvo predicted that after the war, automobiles would need to be economical, and that compact cars would become popular. The compact car “PV444,” which adopted a monocoque body, became a popular model immediately after production began in 1947, and about 200,000 units were produced.
The overall trend of Volvo car history exhibits in the Volvo Museum is to link the development of each car to the situation in Swedish society. For example, in the back of the truck exhibition, there is a large panel that shows a truck driving through the city of Gothenburg at the time. There is also an area where cars are exhibited in a booth that reproduces the city of Gothenburg at that time.
In addition, in the founding period exhibition, it was also introduced that Volvo prototypes were designed and developed for the natural environment and climate conditions of Sweden, and that they were made of high-quality Swedish materials and components. In that sense, the Volvo Museum can be seen as a facility that attempts to convey “Volvo as a Swedish car” as a narrative.
## “Safety” as Volvo’s Corporate Identity
An exhibition on “safety,” which is one of Volvo's identities, is also held at the museum. One of them is the exhibition of “VESC (1972): Volvo Experimental Safety Car.” While other cars are exhibited with multiple vehicles arranged in one area, VESC is exhibited to monopolize an entire area.
In addition, on the wall of the area, a chronological table is displayed under the name “Volvo's safety history.” The chronology explains that Volvo's focus on safety dates to the 1940s. Other car exhibits also play a role in linking Volvo with “safety.” For example, the museum also displays the PV544 (1958), the successor to the PV444. The PV544 is the world's first car with three-point seat belts. The display of this car also serves as a symbol of Volvo's commitment to “safety.”

## Strong Ties with the Swedish Government and Military
In addition to these historic Volvo cars, the Volvo Museum also displays trucks, buses, construction equipment, industrial engines, and more. One of the unique features of the Volvo Museum is the exhibition of Swedish Air Force fighter Saab 37 Viggen. The fighter itself is manufactured by Saab, but since the engine is manufactured by Volvo, it is on display at the Volvo Museum.
In addition to the Saab 37 Viggen, military vehicles are also on display in the museum.
They are on display to show that during World War II, when Volvo was unable to sell cars to households, the military was an important customer for them.18 In this way, the Volvo Museum takes the stance of clearly introducing the relationship with the military in explaining the development of Volvo.
At Volvo, its involvement with the military during World War II was seen as crucial to the company's development. Volvo's development activities at the time were entirely funded by state and defense authorities.
## Framework for Comparative Analysis with Isuzu
I examined the analytical points obtained from the Volvo Museum considering narratives of automobile companies in the corporate museums. In the case of the Volvo Museum, the company's narrative is based on biographies of the key individuals, strength in technology, and strong ties with the Swedish government — including the armed forces. For example, the Volvo Museum dramatically introduces not only the meeting of the two founders, but also their business concept and the hardships they faced afterwards.
The Volvo Museum presents the two founders as trying to capitalize on the strengths of Swedish industry at the time, and as men who tried to create a car that was designed for Sweden's natural environment and climate. This representation of the two founders can also be found in other literature describing the history of Volvo.19 By doing so, the Volvo Museum creates an atmosphere in which the two are imaged as Swedish heroes. Biography of key individuals is one of the principal elements that contribute to a favorable narrative.
For Volvo, the need to tell the story of “safety” in its own museum is unquestionable. Volvo has acquired the brand image of “the safest car in the world.” In the museum, Volvo introduces “safety” as a “strength in technology.” “Technology” is an important factor in presenting favourable narratives for the automobile industry, such as acceleration and autonomous driving. In marketing for the automobile industry, communications such as advertisement have long been used to present favourable narratives by highlighting automotive “technology.”
As is shown via the example of the Volvo Museum, such methods are often adopted in the transmission of narratives through museums.

## The Isuzu Plaza and its Narrative
Isuzu Plaza, the corporate museum of Isuzu Motor Limited (“Isuzu”22) was established in 2017, commemorating the company’s 80th anniversary, given its establishment in 1937.23 The Plaza is adjacent to Isuzu’s Fujisawa Plant, an hour by train from central Tokyo. The museum is open to the public, and it is accompanied with a restaurant and hotel.24 The museum is candid and transparent about the company’s past. Isuzu’s corporate museum presents a clear and coherent narrative, positioning Isuzu as a solid contributor to civil society.
The narrative facilitates building an image of Isuzu as an earnest innovator through importing technology and talent from abroad, as demonstrated by displaying the Wolseley CP Truck and Coupé 117.
The stated theme of Isuzu Plaza is “transporting day-to-day lives” (kurashi o hakobu); this expresses a commitment to enhance the lives and livelihoods of ordinary citizens.

The Isuzu Plaza is three stories high, and two floors of it are used for exhibition. The theme of the first floor is “Isuzu for All Your Transportation Needs.” The main exhibition is a gigantic, detailed diorama demonstrating how automobiles manufactured by Isuzu serve society. An approximately ten-minute drama — depicting a day in a fictional city where Isuzu cars, trucks, buses as well as fire engines, ambulances, and other specialty vehicles are shown — are widely popular among children, their parents, and grandparents.
Both museums of Volvo and Isuzu showcase vehicles in impeccable condition, ones that built the history of the two companies with similar characteristics. Taking a careful look, however, we notice that the Isuzu Plaza lacks three elements which the Volvo Museum possesses. First, Isuzu exhibits no individual — no founder, executive, engineer — at the museum. Despite the many names of companies that eventually became Isuzu Motors Limited in 1949, visitors are left clueless as to who built Isuzu. This is strikingly different from the Volvo Museum where the exhibition starts with a detailed introduction of the company’s founders.
Second, although Isuzu boasts its leading edge in automotive technology, the museum does not specify which technology. As shown, Volvo’s advantage was developing innovative technology in safety features. To emphasize that point, the Museum presented the Volvo Experimental Safety Car on a stand-alone basis. By contrast, Isuzu has no specific display of the technology, inventor, or final product. What was the technology that drove Isuzu to success?
Third, Isuzu, unlike Volvo, appears to be very reluctant to talk about their relationship with the Japanese government, particularly with the armed forces. At the Isuzu Plaza, there are no military vehicles presented except for a few pictures of trucks provided to the Japanese Self Defence Force. Description of Isuzu’s activities is circumscribed as follows in the chronology displayed within the “History of Isuzu” section:
Tokyo Automobile Industries (later renamed Diesel Automobile Industries Co., Ltd.) was placed under the control of the Army25 and designated to manufacture military vehicles and engines until the end of WWII. The production of military use ended in 1945 when the war came to an end.
This narrative gives the impression that the Army forced Isuzu to manufacture military vehicles against its will. This is quite different from the case of Volvo in which it, as a duty of being Swedish, eagerly volunteered to contribute to the war effort.
The three elements which Volvo had but which Isuzu Plaza lacked — people, technology, and relationship with government — are intertwined. That depicts the other side of Isuzu which is not represented in its corporate museum.
To investigate what is missing in the museum, we must take a detour to carefully examine the corporate documents of Isuzu. What emerges is a radically different narrative from what is told at the corporate museum. I shall call it the hidden narrative of Isuzu.
Section 3: The Other Side of Isuzu The Long and Winding Road
Isuzu Motors is the oldest Japanese automobile manufacturer.26 But the making of Isuzu was not an easy path. Isuzu started as an amalgam of three companies and subsequently had to morph three times before becoming Isuzu Motors. The Japanese government was actively involved in the history of Isuzu, as a customer, regulator, sponsor, and engine for innovation.
Thus, the intimate relationship with the government started from the genesis of companies that eventually became today’s Isuzu.
Isuzu has three headwaters: Ishikawajima Automotive Works, DAT Automobile Manufacturing Inc., and Tokyo Gas and Electrical Industrial Co.

Ishikawajima Automotive Works was a spin-off from the Tokyo Ishikawajima Shipbuilding and Engineering Co., Ltd. which had its origins dating back to 1853 as the Tokugawa shogunate’s shipbuilder. 27 DAT Automobile Manufacturing Inc., established in 1926, was the first Japanese company to produce a passenger car in 1933.28
Tokyo Gas and Electrical Industrial, established in 1910, aspired to become an industrial company, and it began producing automobiles in 1918 after profiting from World War I29.
The three companies that would become Isuzu had a common challenge: they lacked scale and struggled to make money. The government, particularly the army, nurtured the then-nascent industry.
The Japanese automobile industry was unable to survive without the protection from the Army. Every automobile company starts manufacturing with great dreams and high aims. Sooner or later, they all struggle to keep afloat.30
The first major legislation that was prescribed by the Army to support the automobile industry was the Military Vehicle Subsidiary Act (gunyo jjidosha hojo hoh) promulgated in 1918. The Act provided substantial subsidies to automobile companies to manufacture and maintain certain vehicles (called “Protected Vehicles”) during peacetime for five years. The Act stipulated that in the event of war, the Army will pay automobile companies to procure such vehicles.
The only three operating automobile companies at that time in Japan, i.e. Ishikawajima Automotive Works, DAT Automobile Manufacturing Inc., and Tokyo Gas and Electrical Industrial Co., all produced Protected Vehicles under the Act. But to the Army’s disappointment, this measure to promote the development of the Japanese automobile industry failed. The reason was because the companies lacked both technology and scale.
Taking advantage of the situation, U.S. firms such as Ford and GM established subsidiaries in Japan in 1924 and 1926, respectably, and aggressively distributed vehicles.
As a result, in 1928, domestic car companies, i.e., the three companies that would become Isuzu, had only a total of 1.3% of market share in Japan.

Lieutenant General Tomio Hara, who oversaw the Army’s technology development, praised Isuzu’s contribution in transforming gasoline engines to diesel for all military vehicles43.
Indeed, Isuzu’s diesel engine technology was second to none at that time in the world, ahead of the U.S., Germany, and the USSR. The Tank 97 CHI-HA was the only medium tank active in combat that had an air-cooled diesel engine.44 Although airbrushed at the corporate museum, in terms of technological breakthrough, company sales, and net profit, World War II was one of the brightest times in Isuzu’s history. It was an accomplishment of a clear corporate strategy that began in 1933 to focus on the developing diesel engine technology45.
As a result, Isuzu’s production of automobiles increased seven-fold (1,210 in 1937 to 7,767 in 1941), and net profit sky-rocketed from JPY 200,000 in 1937 to JPY 9,000,000 in 1944.46 The period that led to and during World War II was the brightest of days for Isuzu.
Isuzu’s dominance in diesel engine technology continued after World War II. The defeat of World War II devastated the company. Ironically, however, Isuzu’s post war recovery was also propelled by demand from the military. This time it was the U.S. Army fighting the Korean War that erupted in 1950. Moreover, the demand was even larger than that from the Japanese Army. Furthermore, the technology developed during WWII, e.g., the DA6 air-cooled diesel engine, was finally implemented for military trucks.47

## Conclusion and Discussion
I have argued that Volvo and Isuzu crafted significantly different narratives in their corporate museums.
Volvo’s narrative could be summarized as being the “pride of Sweden”. To support this narrative, the Volvo Museum presented the key individuals, technology, and strong ties with the Swedish government — including the armed forces — as crucial elements to make the narrative convincing.
By contrast, Isuzu intentionally carved out a portion of its history that was not consistent with the explicit narrative of “transporting day-to-day lives” at the Isuzu Plaza. The strong relationship with the Japanese government, particularly the Army, and significant involvement in supplying diesel engines as well as military vehicles was carefully airbrushed out. Isuzu’s corporate museum focused on conveying the commitment to enhance the lives and livelihoods of ordinary citizens in a peaceful society.
Every company must adhere to the norms of its market and society. To understand why Isuzu avoided highlighting its ties with the pre-war military industrial complex, the post-war social discourse cannot be dismissed. Japanese society, as a backlash to its overly militaristic years, was dominated by a cult-like eagerness for peace.
The post-war Constitution of Japan states that “never again shall we be visited with the horrors of war through the action of government” in its preamble. Furthermore, the constitution clearly stipulates the renunciation of war:
Article 9. Aspiring sincerely to an international peace based on justice and order, the Japanese people forever renounce war as a sovereign right of the nation and the threat or use of force as means of settling international disputes. In order to accomplish the aim of the preceding paragraph, land, sea, and air forces, as well as other war potential, will never be maintained. The right of belligerency of the state will not be recognized.
Such notion of absolute Pacifism is not only enshrined in the constitution but is also engrained in the social fabric of post-war Japan. To this day, Japan does not legally possess “land, sea, and air forces”.
Instead of army, navy, and air forces, she has the “Self Defense” Force to abide with the constitution. Anything that is related to the image and implementation of war has been a social taboo in Japan.48 Under these circumstances, nothing can be more devastating to Isuzu’s brand image than to be perceived as a collaborator for past war efforts. Such an image is diametrically opposed to the commitment of Isuzu to deliver peace and prosperity to society through transporting goods.
One side of a large exhibition hall that presents the landmark automobiles that Isuzu built is dedicated to explaining the history of Isuzu. That section is titled: “Request of the times; Isuzu’s choice (jidai no yousei, Isuzu no sentaku). Before World War II, Isuzu’s decision was to serve the Army through developing diesel engines. That choice brought enormous benefits at the expense of destroying peace and justice.
Post-war Japan became a different society with correspondingly different values. Isuzu’s decision was to help society prosper by peaceful means. We believe that the corporate museum is a testimony to Isuzu’s commitment to do so. Nakamaki and Hioki argue that corporate museums should be viewed as temples, rather than commercial facilities, places that enshrine what the company aspires to become.49 Corporate museums have an element of a temple that consecrates and hallows the sacrifices of employees and executives to the company, while seeking harmony with society: a place to heal.
Isuzu’s choice was to convey a heritage with minimum reference to its military aspect at their corporate museum. Isuzu established the museum as a place to overcome a past stained with blood on its hands and to transform itself into serving a civil society by focusing on commercial vehicles. The Isuzu Plaza became a place to heal, recoup corporate health, and uniquely contribute to civilization by “transporting day-to-day lives.”50
Corporate museums have been an untapped asset for investigating narratives in companies. As I have shown, corporate museums, when approached methodologically, can enhance the nuanced understanding of capitalism and civilizations, including how companies heal and recuperate from their wounds.
