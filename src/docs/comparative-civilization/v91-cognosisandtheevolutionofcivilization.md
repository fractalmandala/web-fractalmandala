Ken Baskin Independent Scholar Baskinman@protonmail.com
[We] must start thinking about life as an information-processing system that calculates solutions to survival challenges — in other words, a computational system. This description applies both at the level of the individual and at the collective level.
Barry Azarian, The Romance of Reality (2022)
From the time when the complex states such as Egypt or Sumer emerged roughly 5,000 years ago, the civilizations they represent have generally become more populous, more socially varied, wealthier, and more technologically advanced. As a result, the innovations they produced would begin to change the conditions in which they existed, and their cultures have had to evolve to adapt to this ongoing change. For instance, the cultures of Bronze Age Egypt and the Iron Age Han Dynasty had to be quite different, even though both were agricultural societies. And, of course, the Electronic Age cultures of the United States or China had to be dramatically different from Bronze or Iron Age societies. Today, we stand at a critical point where our societies must again change their cultures or watch as any of several possible existential threats blow up in our collective faces.
Yet, the dynamics by which the cultures of major civilizations have evolved remain poorly understood.1 For example, as Dmitri Bondarenko and I have shown (2014), religion — especially in its myth2 — has consistently played a critical role in cultural evolution and, therefore, the survival of those cultures. In this paper, I want to build on my work with Bondarenko, as well as recent work in neurobiology, to suggest the outlines of the dynamics of cultural evolution and to examine the contribution the comparative study of civilization can make to addressing our existential challenges, by developing this sort of theory.
- 1 I was surprised to realize that much of what Bondarenko and I concluded reflects the work of Donald T. Campbell. His thoughts concerning multi-disciplinarity and evolutionary epistemology had made their way into my thinking without my realizing it.
- 2 It’s hard to explain how surprised I was at this realization, as my understanding of myth shifted from reflecting Scott Atran’s description as stories about “a counterfactual and counterintuitive world of supernatural agents” (2002:4) to David Christian’s “shared map of understanding that shows members of the community their place in a rich, beautiful, and sometimes terrifying universe.” (2018: 8).
The dynamic I am suggesting is what I am tentatively calling cognosis, an adaptive positive feedback loop that functions most clearly in major periods of social transformation — the Neolithic Revolution, the Axial Age, and Modernity.3 However, for the sake of argument — or, rather, to avoid argument — I will touch mainly on the Axial Age and Modernity, where the societies we examine are uniformly agreed to be “civilizations,” and the written records give us a fuller understanding of what was going on.
I ask the reader’s indulgence in my coining a new word. Much of the dynamics examined in cognosis will seem more familiar if I characterize them as “religion.” Myth and ritual are both key to cognosis, but I am reluctant to use the word “religion” for several reasons. For one thing, defining “religion” is a thankless task, as Wilfred Caldwell Smith (1962) noted more than sixty years ago.4 Everyone “knows” what religion is, but finding agreement among the word’s dozens of definitions has proven impossible.5 The word also triggers strong feelings, ranging from ecstasy to disgust. Moreover, most Westerners grew up assuming that our Western monotheism is typical of all the world’s religions.
So, we learned to think of all religions as a set of activities, mostly focused on worship and a spiritual path, that should be set aside from other social behaviors. But this is hardly accurate for most pre-modern religions. From Ancient Chinese polytheism to Hinduism and Medieval Christianity, most traditional religions were woven so deeply into their culture that, outside the West, people had no word to express what we mean by “religion.”
The case I will make in this paper is provisional. I have only started to flesh out the idea of cognosis, and even if it is accurate, more investigation and consideration will be required. To make my case, I want to explore four key issues:
- • A case history of cognosis
- • Cognosis as a neurobiological imperative
- • Modernity as an extended example, and
- • Implications of cognosis in the study of civilizations.

Because this idea is radical, I wanted to begin with some clarification in the form of a familiar example — the Greek Axial Age.
## A Curious Resemblance
In 1953, Karl Jaspers coined the term Axial Age to describe the period (c. 800-200 BCE) when many of the dominant political entities across Eurasia were transforming from relatively small, mono-cultural Bronze Age kingdoms, like Shang China or Mycenae, into multi-cultured Iron Age empires, like Rome or Han China.6 Bondarenko and I had examined these transformations in The Axial Ages of World History (2014), which compared the Axial Age with Modernity.
During the Age of Kingdoms, similar socio-political forms emerged among dominant polities across Eurasia. They were governed by semi-divine kings who relied on their inner circles of nobles, a polytheistic religion, and bronze technology. From 3000 BCE, when the first of these kingdoms arose, many of them would make significant advances as populations grew; trade networks produced growing wealth; and technology developed, especially horse domestication and the spread of ironworking, which made it possible for armies to travel far and fast enough to conquer empires. By 1000 BCE, these factors had come together to begin overwhelming the ability of these kingdoms to govern, notably in states such as China, India, Greece, and Israel.
The result would be a period of violence, experimentation, and transformation. The Axial Age would lead to the emergence of empires — where an emperor would depend on a bureaucracy, universalist religion, and iron technology. To clarify what I mean by cognosis, we will look at the Axial Age experience of Greece and how its kingdoms would be transformed into the empires of Alexander and then Rome.
For reasons that are still poorly understood, by 1170 BCE, such Greek kingdoms as Mycenae and Crete were destroyed as part of the migration/invasion of the “Sea Peoples.” 7 Whatever the reasons — speculations run from climate change to earthquakes or volcanic eruptions to political infighting — the movement of these people across the Eastern Mediterranean would overwhelm many states, including the Hittites. Battling them would exhaust even Egypt, whose primacy in area politics would never return.
The damage was so thorough that complex states would not emerge in Greece until about 800 BCE, with the rise of its networked city-states.
- 6 In our book, Bondarenko and I referred to the dominant form of the state by slightly different names. What I call kingdoms in this essay, we referred to as “pre-Axial states.” What I call empires were “post-Axial states.”
- 7 Eric Cline’s 177 B.C. (2021) offers a good overview of current thought on the migrations/invasions of the Sea Peoples.
The key challenge the Greeks addressed with the city-state was how to organize society to enhance the trade it depended on in a land of islands and geography that isolated habitable parts from each other.
Greek myth, at the time, appears to have closely resembled the myth of Olympian gods that Homer and Hesiod recorded. Homer’s myth of the Trojan War was especially important. In that myth, a collection of independent kingdoms, including Mycenae and Sparta, fight like brothers, competing for their father’s favor. When they are insulted by their Enemy from the East (Troy), they come together, defeat that enemy, and return to fighting like brothers. Homer’s myth describes the gods as petty and irrational the Trojan War, after all, results from a beauty contest between Hera, Aphrodite, and Diana — but the humans in that myth accept that irrationality as just the way things are.
Much to my surprise, the history of fifth-century Axial Age Greece closely resembles Homer’s story of Greek kingdoms. Like Homer’s kingdoms, the Greek city-states, by 700 BCE, were independent polities fighting with each other like brothers. However, when they were invaded by Persia, their Enemy from the East, in 490 and 480, they united, defeated that enemy, and returned to fighting like brothers. Those conflicts culminated in the Peloponnesian War (431- 404 BCE), which exhausted the Greek citystates, reducing their power and prestige and setting the stage for the rise of Macedonia and the empire of Alexander the Great.
These similarities between Homer’s Trojan War and the Greek Axial Age are so striking that Bondarenko and I wondered whether the central myth of any society might create a sort of mental template that shaped the decisions of individuals or even whole societies. This possibility was supported in all four of the successful Axial Age societies—Greece, Israel, India, and China—where rewriting their myth was key to their successful transformations for the Age of Empires.
Israelite culture would produce the Bible, which places responsibility for its near-death experience at the hands of the Babylonians on its people, who had turned away from worshiping their god as the One True God. In India, the warrior myth of the Mahabharata was transformed into the myth of the spiritual warrior in the Bhagavad Gita or Buddhist myth. The Chinese myth during the pre-Axial Age Zhou Dynasty would be reshaped into the myth of Confucianism. Myth, Bondarenko and I agreed, made contributions to the process of cultural evolution that were generally overlooked in the literature that treats it as “religion.”8
We also realized that this myth seemed part of a larger cultural process. In Greece, the wars of the fifth century BCE created massive terror and uncertainty.
8 Among the most interesting exceptions to this rule are in the work of Milton Yinger (1970), who treats religion as key to a cultural feedback loop; Robert Bellah (2011), who examines it as critical to cultural evolution; and Merlin Donald, for whom mythmaking is central to being fully human.
During the Persian War of 480, for example, Athens was sacked twice, and the possibility of conquest by an alien culture must have haunted everyday life. Even more terrifying was the quarter-century of attack and counterattack during the Peloponnesian War, when, as Thucydides wrote, “Frantic violence became the attribute of manliness…. The advocate of extreme measure was always trustworthy; his opponent, a man to be suspected…. Thus, every form of iniquity took root…” (as quoted in Kagan 1995: 170).
We can see this moral confusion in the tragedies of Aeschylus, Sophocles, and Euripides, which essentially rewrote the myth of Homer and Hesiod. Those tragedies describe the same irrationality of the gods but focus on the enormous suffering it causes good people, Orestes, for example, or Iphigenia. As the crisis deepened, thinkers such as Plato and Aristotle would further rewrite it into philosophical myth, whose mythic elements shine through in the Parable of the Cave or the idea of an Unmoved Mover. The result would be, as Jeremy Lent notes (2017), a religion of rationality, which would be most strongly manifested in the Roman Empire.
Myth, I began to think, is critical to what I am now calling cognosis, the positive feedback loop that produces cultural evolution. Moreover, this function of myth is grounded in current neurobiology, so that myth, especially as we humans have combined it with ritual,9 can determine the way people throughout a society think and perceive their world. In a very tangible way, it can “hypnotize” the people who accept it, thereby shaping their history.
To understand this phenomenon, we turn to the neurobiology of storytelling.10 A Neurobiological Imperative
As I look around the room where I am drafting this essay, I have the feeling that my eyes are drinking in its images, like an HD video cam, and projecting them onto my consciousness. But neurobiologists have shown that this process is not nearly so simple. Both my retinas have one hundred million rod and cone cells, each of which records one dot of light. This enormous field of fragmented information is conducted to my brain, which decodes them into images, combines the images with memories, and runs the output through my mental models, which tell me what the world should and should not be like. All this goes on subconsciously in about thirty neural networks.
- 9 D’Aquili, et al (1979) present a fascinating portrait of how human ritual evolved from social insects to birds, mammals, and primates. Its power to bind members of any society – human or animal – would be geometrically amplified by integrating it with myth.
- 10 Like the word “religion,” “story” has a profusion of meanings. For me, it describes the product of choosing and ordering details in a phenomenon so that the story becomes coherent, taking the form of everything from the construction of human perception to fairy tales, novels, and “white lies.” For a fuller discussion, see Baskin (2020).
In fact, the subconscious can even eliminate images it deems unimportant or likely to make decisions difficult. It can also “confabulate”—invent details that I do not know and present them as factual — if I need those details to make my perceptual stories meaningful. The result is that I experience coherent, story-like models of events around me.11
These perceptual “stories” must answer three questions: What is going on? What should I do? And why is it happening? These are the questions each of us must answer every minute of the day to survive. So, what we perceive is not the world-as-it-is, but a reduced, sometimes distorted, or even partly invented model of the world before our brains process all this information. It is reduced because our senses evolved not to create an accurate picture of the world but to gather the information we need to survive (Hoffmann 2019). If you have ever had a dog, you know how much more that dog could smell, and act on that sense, than any human because a dog’s survival depends so much more on its ability to smell.
Our perceptions also must be coherent–that is, they must tell a story that makes sense– so that we can make decisions that will help us survive. So, if you are walking through a field and see a snake wriggling at your feet, if you do not know what it is, your brain is likely to tell you it is dangerous and get you to run away. After all, you are more likely to survive if you run away from a garter snake than if you google its image and find out it is a rattlesnake.
Storytelling, then, is a neurobiological imperative that enables us to transform the chaos of events happening around us into coherent stories that enable us to make decisions to help us survive.
All this is critical to cognosis. Myth, I have concluded, emerges as a form of storytelling that members of a community use to explore the invisible, powerful forces that surround them. Some myths focus on personal challenges, as in Joseph Campbell’s The Hero’s Journey (1968), where people must answer the three perceptual questions as they apply to themselves. Here, however, I am writing about those myths that must answer these questions for societies as whole entities. That is, our cultural survival depends on knowing what is happening to our communities, what we should do as a group to respond, and why it is happening to us.
As a feedback loop, cognosis begins when a society faces an existential challenge that its members cannot address. Because of the dynamics of perception, they will tell stories to explain the challenges, act on them, learn from their actions, rewrite their stories, and discuss those stories with people around them.

In this way, their explanatory stories become myths, debated, disputed, and filtered in social interactions, as Merlin Donald (1991) notes. At some point, this myth will enable community members to meet their current challenges, and, as if by a form of cultural natural selection, it will become part of that society’s cultural template for living. As such, it will help members thrive and innovate until the changes, often those that this myth helped to create, result in new challenges, which call forth the need to rewrite it.
So, as the Persian invasions of 490 and 480 created a powerful wave of fear and uncertainty, the Greek elite would examine what the invisible forces were doing, how its society should deal with the problem, and why it was happening. Aeschylus, for example, shows us how the demands of Poseidon (for the sacrifice of Agamemnon’s daughter) would tear his family apart; Sophocles traces the suffering of Oedipus and his family at the hands of the gods; and Euripides explores how both too little and too much rationality can cause similar suffering.
With the Peloponnesian War (431- 404 BCE), this questioning of the gods as symbolic of all the invisible forces of the world would intensify. As Thucydides notes, the repeated attacks and counterattacks between the alliances of Athens and Sparta destroyed any faith in the gods and made civil society all but impossible.
For Plato, this disillusion with the old myth culminated in the execution of Socrates in 398. From the perspective of cognosis, the philosophic myth that he and Aristotle wrote during the first half of the fourth century was their attempt to answer the third perceptual question for their society. This myth of rationality would set the stage for the Roman Empire.
We can understand the mythic philosophy of Confucians and Taoists, the editing of the Hebrew Bible, or the rise of Buddhism, during the Axial Age, in much the same way. This is cognosis in action, as existential challenge leads to mythmaking, social negotiation, a new symbolic order, success and innovation, change, and, eventually, new challenges.
But these events occurred two-and-a-half millennia ago. The last five hundred years give us a far more vivid (and well-documented) example of cognosis at work.
## Bacon’s Vision
In 1620 CE, people across Europe must have felt as fearful and anxious as we do today after both societies had experienced more than a century of terrifying wars. What they faced would have been disturbing enough if the “religious wars” of the 16th and 17th centuries were just the spectacle of Protestants and Catholics slaughtering each other to prove which type of Christianity worshipped the Prince of Peace as God had intended. But the real crisis was a top-to-bottom social restructuring.
For several hundred years, Europe’s commercial class had become increasingly wealthy, to the point that it would begin to challenge the feudal alliance of the aristocracy and the Catholic Church for political power. The Black Death of the 14th Century killed off a third to a half of Europe’s population, from Princes of the Church to milkmaids.
So, with the Protestant Reformation, a century-and-a-half later, members of that commercial class now had a “religious” partner with which to make their challenge. Thus, if my understanding of cognosis is accurate, we should expect the resulting political conflict to drive the elite of Western Europe to rewrite the society’s myth to better deal with current conditions.
Which is exactly what Sir Francis Bacon, former Chancellor of England, would do. In the 1620s, he published several books, such as Novum Organum or The New Atlantis, to explore the existential threat involved in the emergence of this world, where science was being perfected, and capitalism was maturing. Society, he concluded, had grounded itself in thinking about the world in terms of the speculations of theologians rather than through evidence of what was going on. In these books, Bacon explained that to encourage human thriving, society should be grounded in knowledge of how the world actually worked — that is, a scientific cosmology.
At the heart of this cosmology was the scientist, whom Bacon compared to Medieval priests. Their job was to “torture” Nature to discover her secrets and apply those secrets in creating innovations, such as the printing press, which would enhance human life, reduce violence and promote prosperity. This goal of creating what Bacon called “the Empire of Man” would be shared in the philosophical myths of both René Descartes and Thomas Hobbes.12 In this way, Bacon was rewriting the Thomistic theology of the High Medieval Period, transforming Aquinas’s rational God who created Nature so we can better understand Him into the scientist who examined it to improve on God’s creation and claim true dominion over Nature.
And, as cognosis again suggests, Bacon’s myth would be debated, disputed, and filtered over generations to create a new cosmology. Among the issues that shaped it were the astronomy of Copernicus and Galileo, Newton’s physics, England’s Royal Society, the debate between Descartes and Hobbes over the nature of science, and the opposition of groups such as the Anglican clergy and the Romantics. All this and more was being filtered through cognosis.
During the first half of the 19th Century, science was, as Bacon predicted, uncovering Nature’s secrets, including theories of electricity and evolution, to give humans “dominion” over Nature.
12 For a thorough discussion of the approaches of these three seminal thinkers, see Gillespie (2009).
And the Industrial Revolution was remaking Europe’s economy into the capitalistic machine we have today. By the mid-19th century, the Clockwork Universe had fully emerged and was becoming widely accepted.
This cosmology described our world as a deterministic machine made of separate, single-purpose parts, unfolding in a chain of cause-and-effect. In many ways, this myth had been fantastically successful, producing scientific medicine, mass education, and a still-expanding explosion of knowledge. But, in winning a mechanical dominance over Nature, society also created new existential challenges, just as cognosis suggests.
## Our Nightmare
Over the last 60 years, those challenges have become our nightmare: For instance, in 1962, Rachel Carson’s book Silent Spring appeared, exposing the extent of industrial pollution, and in 1992, the UN Convention on Climate Change made the challenge of global warming clear. Yet these challenges remain. While noteworthy progress has occurred in cleaning up industrial pollution, at least in developed nations, troubling examples continue to surface, as with the recent crisis of lead pollution in the Flint, Michigan, water supply. Ten years after the problem was demonstrated, that “crisis” has not yet been fully addressed.13
Even more troubling is the three decades of international conferences and treaties to deal with global warming. The result might be a “talking” tee shirt: “Our political leaders went to Paris and signed their agreement to reduce global warming, and all we got is 126o temperatures in New Delhi and flash floods in Afghanistan.” As Einstein observed, these existential challenges cannot be solved with the logic that created them. What we need is a new cosmology, just as the Axial Age Greeks needed the more rational cosmology of Plato and Aristotle.
And as cognosis suggests, the Clockwork Universe helped create our current challenges. For instance, describing Nature as a set of machines that should be exploited so that we can exert dominion over it is at the heart of both extensive pollution and global warming. These problems were exacerbated by its understanding that the world is composed of distinct, separate “things.” As a result, it did not encourage people to think about how waste products, from plastics to greenhouse gases, could degrade the environments into which they were disposed.
Much has been written about these elements of the Clockwork Universe that have impacted our world. However, one element has been overlooked.

That element is the way this view of reality as a collection of distinct “things” shapes our understanding of how society should function. In pre-modern societies, the social world was conceived as an integrated fabric in which everyone was expected to operate from the same mythic understanding of the world. Social systems for government and religion, economics, and knowledge operated accordingly, not only to the same cosmology but the same myth, which, as noted earlier, appears to work as a mental template for living. In Ancient Egypt, Ma’at was the goddess of the right order, which applied to the movement of the stars, the functioning of society, and even the behavior of the semi-divine Pharoah. Her consort, Thoth, was the god of knowledge, writing, and mathematics, whose realm included the science and engineering key to building religious monuments like the pyramids. Egypt’s temples even filled an “economic” function — to collect and distribute grain.
On the other hand, our Clockwork Universe presents society as a collection of separate, disintegrated social institutions. While they share the same mechanical symbolic cosmology, each has developed its own myth, reflecting its unique values. As a result, the Modern West does not work as a single social fabric but as a patchwork of institutions whose myths may be incompatible.
As a result, three key institutions — science, capitalism, and nationalism — that could easily be working together to address global warming are in a standoff, based on their incompatible myths, that makes solving the problem all but impossible.
As noted earlier, this symbolic order pictures life as a mechanical process composed of passive, unthinking parts whose motion depends on the kickstart of an outside force, after which one part causes movement in the next, like the springs and gears of a clock. Each part is distinct; none of them has significant agency. They simply move as the forces of the Universe drive them to move. The job of the scientist, then, would be to take Nature apart to understand its forces.
Bacon expected the work of scientists to lead to innovations, such as the printing press, which could help improve human life. Social salvation, then, would result from learning Nature’s secrets and using them to reinvent society.
In terms of science’s approach to global warming, the result has been a deep dive into meteorology and the science of climate change. And for the last 40 years or so, these scientists have produced a generation of warnings about the dangers of continuing to dump vast amounts of greenhouse gases into the atmosphere. While much of their work has been challenged, weather events of the last decade have made it all but certain that global warming is occurring as, for example, unprecedented flooding has afflicted both Southern Florida and India, and forest fires have struck both Siberia and Southeastern Canada.
In November 2024, the UN will be holding its twenty-ninth annual conference on the subject. Yet, as cognosis seems to suggest, all this discussion — and the treaties that have resulted from it — have had a negligible effect on climate change. Why have these conferences and treaties had such a negligible effect on climate change? I would like to suggest that, largely because of the myths of capitalism and nationalism, they have, over and over, blocked the actions recommended by science.
The myth of capitalism, for instance, emerged from the Protestant Ethic, with social salvation from prosperity and wealth as a sign of God’s favor.14 As a result, value is almost always measured in terms of money, to the exclusion of matters such as social costs. Nowhere is this sort of cultural hypnosis more emphatic than in American education. On one hand, public education has become so underfunded that teachers now regularly pay for materials for their classes from their own pockets. And the cost of higher education now excludes large numbers of people from even thinking about attending. In addition, people who are essential for a healthy society, like those same public school teachers, often end up with so much debt that they are likely to be paying it off for decades.
All of this seems absurd in a world where both political and economic power increasingly depends on a highly educated population. As Artificial Intelligence rises in importance and as healthcare becomes more dependent on high technology, an education system that encourages young people to study math and science has become essential. Yet, instead of providing financial enticements to enter this critical arm of the workforce, public policy often makes it financially prohibitive, as if policymakers had been hypnotized.
On a more comic note, this sort of cultural hypnosis about the value of money expresses itself in the salary negotiations of professional athletes. These days, it is not unusual to hear sports commentators commiserating with athletes who are offered contracts for a mere $10 million to play their sport for a year. Is it any wonder that capitalism’s leaders object to the loss of profits in the oil and gas industry, making effective action of global warming far more difficult?
And nationalism, grounded in “worship” of the tribal state, is, by definition, a myth that puts the interest of individual states above the planet’s welfare. As a result, mathematics of equity over the clean energy costs expected from any state has emerged, once again making it more difficult to implement a policy that would do something about the problem.
As a result, our cosmology, the Clockwork Universe, has become an obstacle to the changes cognosis demands.

Fortunately, disciplines such as complexity science and big history have begun to develop the principles that rewriting our mechanical myth demands.15 And once thinkers get beyond a literal interpretation, Lovelock’s myth of Gaia, Earth as a metaphoric organism, can also help develop a new myth for the 21st century.
While this analysis does not look beyond the last 5,000 years, current work in archaeology is making it possible to explore cultural evolution going back to the end of the Ice Age.16 As a result, I suspect it may be possible to explore cognosis as a process that has been functioning throughout human existence. But even if we limit it to the period in which civilization has been predominant, cognosis offers some provocative areas for examination. I would like to close this essay with a few comments about one of those areas.
## A Closing Speculation
Perhaps the most interesting question cognosis evokes is: Can centuries-old myths shape the unfolding of history? Earlier, I pointed to the similarities between Homer’s Trojan War and fifth-century Axial Age Greece, as well as how Bacon’s reworking of the theology of Aquinas, written three centuries before Bacon lived, would allow him to predict Modernity’s world of Western Science. One other example is worth noting.
In The Religion of Technology (1997), David Noble examines the Medieval Christian roots of Western Science. His comments on the Apocalypse are especially interesting, considering the idea of cognosis. He points to four stages — Armageddon (the final war between Good and Evil), the Ascent of the Blessed, the perfection of Man after the return of Jesus to govern the Kingdom of Heaven on Earth, and eternal life. He also points out that four of the greatest technologies of the 20th Century are strangely parallel — atomic energy is reminiscent of Armageddon; space travel, of the Ascent of the Blessed; genetic engineering, of the perfection of man; and AI, of eternal life.
What, then, is the relationship between a civilization’s myth and the behavior of individuals and groups within it?
Cognosis suggests surprisingly close parallels between biological and cultural evolution. From this perspective, myth may function like an organism’s DNA. Current evolutionary theory views DNA not through the cake recipe metaphor that Richard Dawkins (1976) used but more as a library of possible behaviors available to respond to events.17

And, just as we saw in the mythic rewriting during the Greek Axial Age or Modernity, this new evolutionary theory has found that the rate of mutation increases when the survival of an organism is in danger. Moreover, the many mythic rewritings of cognosis must be acted upon in a process not unlike biological natural selection until the civilization identifies the myth (DNA mutations) that enables it to meet its existential challenges.
As I noted, cognosis still demands a great deal of further thought and research. But if it is even partially accurate, it may offer a powerful new way of thinking through the challenges societies across the globe today face. For one thing, it suggests a process for exploring the way different civilizations have evolved and why they often approach situations so differently. For another, it offers a way to draw together findings in a variety of fields that can complement each other, as I have in this essay with anthropology, history, neurobiology, and complexity science.
In many ways, this essay is itself an example of the debating, disputing, and filtering that lies at the heart of cognosis. As such, it presents a way of thinking about the myth that will have to emerge if our societies are to survive their current challenges. While cognosis could be a dead end in our efforts to find a path toward successful ways of addressing these challenges, it is, at the very least, the sort of attempt at a new direction that we are all seeking. I look forward to your thoughts on cognosis and your cooperation in pursuing this way of thinking.
