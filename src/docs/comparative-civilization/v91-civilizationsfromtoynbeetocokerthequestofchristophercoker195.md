Greg Lewicki Vice President, International Society for the Comparative Study of Civilizations greglewicki@alumni.lse.ac.uk The Gentleman’s Heaven
In the gentlemen’s heaven, Christopher Coker must surely have his separate suite, one with golden busts of travelers, oldfashioned English furniture, and coldtoned walls resembling those of Athenaeum, a Pall Mall club in London, one of a few clubs where he liked to dine.
Under the tall ceilings of Athenaeum, there was a magical library with a door masquerading as a bookshelf and a bookstand containing names of famous members — such as Charles Darwin or Charles Dickens. It should be updated with Christopher Coker’s details as soon as possible, as he, too, will be remembered as internationally renowned, along with other exceptional gentlemen of past eras — those who wore the fragrance of cigars and knowledge as their second suit.
Many of Coker’s colleagues would certainly agree that his heavenly suite must be the place where all the precious things from his London School of Economics cabinet of curiosities would reside, including weapon-shaped vodka bottles, otherworldly contraptions from political desks, the memoirs of dictators recorded in their own handwriting — and, of course, my Lego-like bricks themed Tannenberg 1410 with a figurine of Polish king Jogaila on a warhorse. Also, lots of books: on war, on international relations, and on the civilizational condition of the West. Whereas, of course, Christopher’s main lifetime focus was international politics and war; his interest in comparative approaches to civilizations and civilizational identities has permeated his entire work.
## Twilight of the West?
Coker is the author of Twilight of the West (1997), 1 an extremely important, internationally translated — yet somehow underrated in the West — magnificent essay on the condition of Western civilization. Replete with references to thinkers such as G.W.F. Hegel, Oswald Spengler, Jose Ortega y Gasset and Arnold Toynbee, the book is, in fact, a philosophical-historical call for the reinvention of the West understood as a transatlantic community with shared values.
Coker writes: “What strikes one most forcefully about contemporary Europe is the extent to which the Europeans … discovered that their own history, to quote the philosopher Alasdair MacIntyre, is no longer universal, but ‘autobiographical.’ The European project is not a great venturing out into the world but a disengagement from the world.”2
Certainly, Coker does not dream of expansive universalisms summarizing Western history. On the contrary, he calls for the reinvention of dialogical Western interest in the world based on classical truth-seeking. He writes: “We should not regret the passing of the Hegelian age. The trouble is that we have not replaced it with anything else.”3
What exactly is this “else”? Philosophical-historical imagination, that these days sometimes gets lost. According to Coker, many modern scholars are no longer willing to enunciate great certainties and even less are willing to fight for them. “The Western community cannot long survive in such an age,” Coker writes. “We deceive ourselves, of course, if ... we think we can act without imagination or that we can live a life based on purely pragmatic rather than normative concerns. It is probably too late for the West to find new ground on which to stand, but it is not too late for the Western powers to engage with history on other terms”.4
Where to look for the innovations in Western identity? Perhaps, Coker thinks, the new Europe symbolized by Czech statesman Vaclav Havel could reinvigorate the debate about innovative ideas that would revive the West. In the concluding part of Twilight, he quotes the following fragment from the speech by Czech statesman and dissident Havel entitled “The Hope for Europe”:

Europe has one final possibility if it so desires: it can reclaim its finest spiritual and intellectual traditions and… look for what they have in common with other cultures and other spheres of civilization and join forces with them in search for the common moral minimum necessary to guide us … so that we can confront jointly whatever threatens our lives together.5
Quoting Havel at the end of the book was no coincidence. Coker was an attentive observer of the cultural, ethnic, and religious transformation of Europe, and he thought Eastern Europe could play an important part in the future.
In 1996, he wrote a press essay, What’s Left of the West6, where he put forward an analogy between the current Western civilization and the Roman Empire — in particular its western and eastern parts. He wrote:
Remember that the Western Empire, unlike the East, was unable to cope with the assimilation of the barbarian tribes which had been flooding across its frontiers since the third century. In the West, the German newcomers wanted nothing more than to become equal citizens, the one concession Rome would not make, though towards the very end it relied on them totally for its own protection. In the East, the Greeks had been second-class citizens as well — yet by the middle of the fifth century, much of the Eastern Empire was recognizably Greek in nature. The process which would later transform the Eastern Empire into the Byzantine Empire was already far advanced. Now, perhaps, the greatest contemporary challenge that confronts the Western world is whether an increasingly multiracial society can also become a multicultural one.7
## Coker’s Hope in East-Central Europe
Coker thought that the American part of the West would fare better throughout the process of multi-culturalization due to — among other reasons — the geographical setting separating the Americas from other continents, and because migrants from Latin America generally wish to succeed in the mainstream American culture instead of separating themselves from it. In Europe, the main extra-European population is Islamic, and their willingness to assimilate is not as strong. This could stimulate conflicts, additionally fueled by radical right-wing political movements.

When we discussed the article nearly two decades after its publication, Coker claimed that it is entirely possible that Western Europe, like the Western Empire, could risk losing Western civilizational identity, but that Eastern Europe — not financially attractive for non-European migrants — would carry on European culture like the Eastern and then Byzantine Empire did with the Roman culture in the past.
Coker also claimed that although some governments of Eastern European nations are illiberal, their citizens remain deeply anchored in Western culture thanks to the communist era and their conscious rejection of Russian communism that was antiWestern in nature.
This prediction seems ghastly accurate from the 2024 perspective, especially when one thinks of Ukrainians who are dying in trenches for a dream of belonging to the Western civilization and not the “Russian world”.
## Aladdin’s Cave
Christopher Coker was an inspiring mentor for students, always generous with time. His official memorial page lists more than a hundred stories. One of them is by Alison Carter, who was Coker’s secretary: “He always delighted in telling me the latest gossip, or about his exploits abroad or at The Travellers Club, and in showing me the latest bizarre and fascinating acquisition for his Aladdin's cave of an office. He was always drafting another book, several of which, in the early days, I typed up for him from the microtapes onto which he dictated ... I was always impressed by the number of former students and friends he managed to keep up with, all around the world”.8
I was one of them — not only did he shape my academic sensitivity but also my awareness regarding the evolution of academic trends and fashions one could either reject or surf onto. I first met Christopher around 2004 in Krakow as a student of international relations at the Tischner European University.
Coker came there to give a lecture by invitation of his former student, Dr. Lukasz Kamienski, who is currently the director of LSE IDEAS Central and South-East Europe Program at the Jagiellonian University in Krakow, one of two regional centers like this in Europe, the other one being in Romania, at Babeș-Bolyai University, Cluj-Napoca.9

On our first meeting, I was thrilled to be able to discuss neo-medievalism with Coker, who was a student of Hedley Bull — a renowned proponent of the neo-medieval concept within the framework of international relations. Coker patiently read my meager papers, which sketched analogies between medieval macrostructure and the emerging postmodern ones. This was inspiring — to the extent that it prompted me later to develop Bull’s initial ideas into a broader, civilizational prognostic framework.10
There is certainly some pride in the realization that just as Hedley Bull mentored Christopher Coker, Christopher Coker mentored me. With his English wit and a profound sense of academic purpose masquerading as cynicism, Christopher also made me want to read more about British contributions to civilization theory.
## Coker and Toynbee
When I once showed him my 2012 book about Arnold Toynbee’s civilization theory and its applications to prognostics,11 he was both amused and interested. “So, you are trying to resurrect Toynbee!” he exclaimed, smirking. “This will not be an easy task!” he added. When I remarked that the book predicts the return of civilizational concepts as a useful analytical lens, we started to discuss Toynbee’s rise to prominence and the subsequent criticism of his works by fellow Oxford historians that diminished his popularity in the 1960s.
Toynbee — a renowned British scholar and one of the founding members of our International Society for the Comparative Study of Civilizations (henceforth ISCSC) — created an evolutionary model of civilizational development based on the generalization of the history of Hellenic civilization. He believed that every civilization goes through four phases of development, with the first one being genesis and then growth. Then, if a civilization fails to maintain both political and spiritual unity, a third phase — disintegration — commences. This phase is marked by wars and conflicts and then by the creation of a universal political entity. Ultimately, the fourth phase follows: decay.

Coker, on the one hand, appreciated Toynbee’s macro-historical approach and vivid, stimulating analogies; on the other hand, he was aware of the theoretical flaws of his model. In a similar case with another civilization theorist, Samuel Huntington, Christopher believed that even though The Clash of Civilizations12 was influential and shaped the public perception of international relations dynamics in the first decade of 21st century, it is in fact too simplistic, and a future return to comparative civilizational frameworks would have to take a different form.
Years later, in 2014 when he sent me a paper from The Times Literary Supplement by Krishan Kumar entitled “Civilized Values: The Return of Arnold Toynbee?” we both confirmed our feeling that the civilizational concept — although broadly criticized will once again experience momentum.13 A few years later I elaborated the critique of the civilizational concept — as well as possible theoretical ways forward — in my 2017 PhD thesis on Joseph Tainter’s concept of civilizational collapse and comparative civilizations, which Christopher graciously agreed to review.14
## Civilizations and Civilizational States
Considering all the above, Christopher’s penultimate book, The Rise of The Civilizational State, presents itself not only as “just another book”, but in fact as the fruit of decades-long reflection on the civilizational concept, its problems, its biases, but also its explanatory potential — one that could offer an additional, non-trivial insight into the dynamics of geopolitics. This book marks Christopher’s return to the civilizational concept driven by a conviction that comparative civilizational analysis must be reinstated as a useful analytical layer in the realm of international relations theory.
In the preface, Coker notes: “Neither civilizational identities nor national loyalties can be written off as the delusions of those who cannot make the most of globalization. They exist even if the historical conditions that gave rise to them have changed, and they are likely to be exploited by politicians, even if this means ironing out many of the other complexities of political life”.15

According to Coker, whereas the Western left tends to deny the existence of civilizational values whatsoever, the right tends to falsely assume Western values are exceptional.16 In turn, Coker seems to believe, much like Huntington, that civilizations are not universal but unique.17 According to Coker himself, civilizations have no essence, but distinctive features.18
In The Rise of Civilizational State, Coker defines civilizations as “societies that have developed a market of some kind (a system of production and distribution) as well as a developed urban life (with a hierarchical class/caste system at its heart). Add to that, bureaucratic infrastructure based (usually) on literacy which at least allows records to be kept and history to be written. And then there are material achievements … when we talk of a civilization, think of it, if you will, as a lifestyle start-up that becomes over time a business conglomerate.”19
He also elaborates on the problems of Twentieth Century comparative civilizational works, to mention, specifically, those of Oswald Spengler, Arnold Toynbee, and Samuel Huntington. While criticizing their “intellectual overreach”,20 Coker also acknowledges that civilizational self-identification is still occurring around the world and that many people feel they belong to a particular civilization, a one-among-many, civilization.
Recognizing the complex nature of civilizational taxonomies that prompt some experts to wrongly treat civilizations as unified or solidified entities, Coker narrows his analytical focus to the concept of a civilizational state, namely a state with an ambition to represent, symbolize, and cultivate a particular civilization as its main host. Civilizational states include Russia, India, and — most importantly — China.
Coker claims normative orders that these states proclaim as their own do matter on the international scene — regardless of biases, exploits, or abuses the concepts of civilizational identities may generate. He writes: “The civilizational state is an eclectic concept: it’s largely a device to legitimize the power of a particular regime and to help it shape the political landscape in its own interests.”21

It is not a goal of this in-memoriam article to summarize Coker’s last “civilizational” book, as the richness of its content could be a basis for many separate works. What is important is that Coker should be recognized as a scholar who tried to reconstruct the civilization theory on non-ideological, unbiased grounds, to give scholars and experts new tools useful in analyzing international relations through a comparative civilizational lens. He had no illusions that the civilizational concept is often used and, in fact, abused by international powers to justify or secure their geopolitical actions.
In a discussion with ISCSC Board Member Stephen Satkiewicz, Prof. Coker admits that he is not even sure if the president of China, Xi-Jinping, or the president of Russia, Vladimir Putin, genuinely believe in the concepts of civilizational state they promote, or they only find them useful to beat the West and to proclaim their own superiority over the old nation-states.22
## Coker’s Comparative Civilizational Legacy
Christopher Coker managed to unlock the civilizational concept for future scholars by demonstrating that despite its inherent problems, it is useful in creating a theoretical apparatus to understand geopolitics better. By understanding the ideological and intellectual underpinnings of specific civilizational states, we can better understand their actions on a global scene.23
Because, even if we assume that civilizations do not clash, civilizational states appear to do so.
In this spirit, Coker’s work encourages others to look at the international order not only through the lens of realism, idealism or constructivism, but also through the lens of civilizational theory. It is worth mentioning that he closely followed the activities of the International Society for the Comparative Study on Civilizations. He was a friend of the society and shared the society’s belief that civilizations matter.
What is Coker’s legacy? A globetrotting cultural diplomat embodying the spirit of Englishness with its dignity and profoundness of thought, Coker managed to link pessimism about human nature with a friendly everyday wit. One day, when we were introduced to some new "amazing scholars," he would light up a cigar, have a sip of vodka and then lean forward towards the newcomers saying: “Very well, so amaze me now.”

Whoever passed a similar wit-test could become his friend. Or when discussing religious matters with a person openly declaring their piety, Coker could ask: “Do you sin?” Even if such remarks went against the English norms of stiff-upper-lip small-talk, they were always a genuine invitation to intellectual friendship.
Coker’s attitude towards the world becomes more understandable if we realize that Christopher, well versed in Latin, believed in the transforming power of the Latin motto “dulce est desipere in loco” — it is nice to misbehave from time to time. Misbehavior in this context means challenging what exists to understand the broader truth behind the cognitive conventions and societal fashions to encourage criticism and creative syntheses.
“Energy flows and matter cycles,” he once told me over a heavy chunk of Irish black pudding and whisky, reflecting on human condition. “As we are tied to matter, the goal is to do something that matters.” Indeed, an important part of Coker’s legacy is the belief that civilizations matter. I am convinced the annual Christopher Coker Prize established post-mortem by the Palgrave Macmillan journal International Politics24 indicates one of the venues where the continuators of Coker’s thought could find an agreeable place to publish — a spiritual equivalent of The Travellers Club to smoke a cigar with Chris’s spirit. In a postmodern era that too frequently reduces comparative civilizational analysis to just stating the difference between civilizations and nothing else, spaces like this are much needed. Another venue of this kind is of course our own Comparative Civilizations Review.
We, Christopher’s mentees, must learn to feel comfortable with equivalents like that as our next proper dinner with Christopher will take place no sooner than in eternal planes.
