Mojtaba Sadeghi Independent Scholar Mo.sadeghi@alumni.um.ac.ir Abstract
The Geographic Clash of Civilizations represents a significant geographiccivilizational reality and process, a phenomenon that has occurred and is expected to unfold increasingly in the third millennium. Understanding, analyzing, and forecasting this phenomenon relies solely on the geographic conceptualization of civilization. Through that perspective, the world is undergoing another instance of the geographic clash of civilizations, a clash more profound than the Clash of Civilizations, impacting the survival or collapse of human civilizations. The geographic clash of civilizations epitomizes an inescapable conflict between the soul (das-man) and the body (no-dasein) of a civilization, stemming from historical asynchronous changes between their essence and existence. This article seeks to analyze the geographic clash of civilizations, which is poised to become an increasingly fundamental challenge in the techne-oriented world of the third millennium.
Key words: Civilization, Clash of civilizations, Techne, The third millennium Introduction
The Geographic Clash of Civilization (GClC) stands as a long-term historical phenomenon in world history. With technological advancements marking the onset of the techne-oriented third millennium, the world is increasingly perceiving another iteration of the GClC. Consequently, uncovering, understanding, analyzing, and forecasting this phenomenon becomes essential for the survival and prevention of the collapse of civilizations.
Within this framework, three fundamental questions arise:
- 1. What is the geographic clash of civilizations?
- 2. Why does the geographic clash of civilizations occur?
- 3. What phenomenon does the geographic clash of civilizations gestate?
## What Is the Geographic Clash of Civilizations?
The geographic clash of civilizations can be seen as arising from a geographic conceptualization of civilizations (Table 1). It is solely through the geographic perspective that we can reveal, understand, analyze, and forecast the clash of civilizations.
Table 1: The Geographic Conceptualization of Civilization (GCC)
As seen in Table 1, civilization represents a geographic space comprising both a soul and a body. The soul encompasses the realm of thoughts, encapsulating all non-material facets of civilization. This category comprises thoughts on civilization and thoughts in civilization layers.
Within the soul of civilization, there exists a hardware component (thoughts of civilization-managing) alongside three software components or three types of da-sein, each embodying its distinct “being-in-the-world.”
This concept considers the soul of civilization as the gemüt layer from Heidegger’s viewpoint; gemüt includes all immaterial aspects of humans. (Jamadi, as translator of the Being and Time book, 2009: 112).
The body of civilization contains all material and physical components and aspects (the human layer, the man-made layer, and the natural layer). In contrast to the thoughts layer, we can consider the human layer as the no-gemüt layer. Based on the geographic concept of civilizations, this layer includes only material and biological aspects of three types of da-sein.
Each of the three kinds of da-sein are a fusion of material-biological aspects (no-gemüt) and immaterial-intellectual ones (gemüt). The no-da-sein layer includes the natural and man-made layers because they are not human beings and have not the gemüt.
Thus, here we draw inspiration from Heidegger’s Being and Time to discern the existential layers of civilization (such as gemüt, no-gemüt, da-sein(s), no-dasein(s) and das-man). The soul of civilization is not the sum of the civilization-managing layer and three types of da-seins (managers, thinkers, and dwellers).
Heidegger (1996) in Being and Time states:
Being-in-the-world is a fundamental constitution of da-sein (Heidegger, 1996: 56). “The clarification of being-in-the-world showed that a mere subject without a world “is” not initially and is also never given. And, thus, an isolated I without the others is in the end just as far from being given initially.” (Ibid:109).
In that Da-sein is at all, it has the kind of being that is being-with-one-another. Being-with-one-another cannot be understood as a summative result of the occurrence of several “subjects.” Encountering several “subjects” itself is possible only by treating the others encountered in their Mitda-sein merely as “numerals.” This number is discovered only by a definite being with and toward one another. (Ibid:117-118) Based on this like-with being-in-the-world, the world is always already the one that I share with the (Ibid:111) others. The world of Da-sein is a with-world. Being in is being with others. The innerworldly being-in-itself of others is Mitda-sein. (Ibid:112).
Hence, from this perspective, the soul of civilization, due to its three types of da-sein, exceeds the mere summation of its constituent elements. In other words, it embodies a das-man (the they) as Heidegger conceptualizes it in the Being and Time (1996):
“Da-sein stands in subservience to the others. It itself is not; the others have taken its being away from it. The everyday possibilities of being of da-sein are at the disposal of whims of the others. These others are not definite others. On the contrary, any other can represent them. What is decisive is only the inconspicuous domination by others that da-sein as being-with has already taken over unawares. One belongs to the others oneself and entrenches their power. “The others” whom one designates as such in order to cover over one’s own essential belonging to them, are those who are there initially and in everyday being-with-one-another. The “who” is not this one and not that one, not oneself and some and not the sum of them all. The “who” is the neuter, the they. […]. The they always “did it,” and yet it can be said that “no one” did it. In the everydayness of da-sein, most things happen in such a way that we must say “no one did it.” (Heidegger, 1996: 118-120).
It is only through the geographic concept of civilizations that the civilizational clash reveals itself as one of the civilizational phenomena that increasingly are occurring as the third millennium dawns. Now, that question is: What is geographic clash of civilizations?
The GClC differs from Huntington's The Clash of Civilizations. According to the geographic perspective (Table 1), Huntington's concept primarily focuses on the clash of das-man(s), (the souls) of civilizations, as he conceptualizes civilization as a cultural entity (Huntington, 1993:4). The soul of civilization (das-man) is the most vital component of civilizations in The Clash of Civilization:
A civilization is thus the highest cultural grouping of people, and the broadest level of cultural identity people have short of that which distinguishes humans from other species. It is defined both by common objective elements, such as language, history, religion, customs, institutions, and by the subjective self-identification of people. (Huntington, 1993: 4-5).
Huntington's delineation of the world into distinct territories as Western, Confucian, Japanese, Islamic, Hindu, Slavic-Orthodox, Latin American, and potentially African civilizations (Huntington, 1993: 5) underscores the paramount importance of the civilization's soul in his perspective.
Consequently, The Clash of Civilizations manifests as a spiritual and cultural clash unfolding among diverse souls — the das-man(s) of civilizations, in a more idealized context. His hypothesis states:
The fundamental source of conflict in this new world will not be primarily ideological or primarily economic. The great divisions among humankind and the dominating source of conflict will be cultural. (Huntington, 1993:3).
The geographic clash of civilizations, however, represents an intra-civilizational conflict between the soul (das-man) and the body (no-da-sein(s)) of a civilization (Figure 1). This conflict arises due to the slower spiritual changes in das-man compared to the swifter physical changes in the body.
This unavoidable phenomenon was initially a normal geographic gap but until das-man of a civilization  despite its fundamental backwardness from bodily changes  it was still able to resolve its spiritual and bodily vital problems, crises, and dilemmas. Over time, civilizations have been confronted with vital crises during which their das-man(s) no longer was able to resolve; here the geographic gap is transformed into a Geographic Clash (GC). As the gap transforms into the GC, the disparity between the das-man and the body of a civilization widens significantly. Consequently, the das-man becomes incapable of resolving crucial problems, crises, and dilemmas.
For instance, in the nineteenth century the Industrial Revolution created new conditions and problems that none of the existing social, economic, and political models (such as feudalism, monarchism and so forth) could cope with. Therefore, new models such as liberal democracies were developed (Harari, 2018: 42).
Some civilizations collapse because their das-man(s) fail to resolve and alleviate critical crises. Conversely, certain civilizations consciously or subconsciously initiate the creation of new techne(s)1 (new knowledge and tools) to address their crises and transform themselves back into the geographic gap . In the past, this transformation was possible because the transition was feasible as even newer techne(s) were simpler and more rudimentary. Hence, while these newer techne(s) resolved existing crises, they did not introduce new ones.
In contemporary times and the near future, due to the cumulative advancements in techne, new techne(s) are and will be significantly more sophisticated. Consequently, as these new techne(s) resolve current crises, they simultaneously give rise to newer crises, necessitating the creation of further techne(s) to address them. This perpetuates a cycle 'from gap to gap.' Consequently, at the onset of the third millennium, the transformation of the geographic clash to a geographic gap becomes unattainable. Civilizations must adapt to coexisting with the clash, as it evolves into an inevitable civilizational phenomenon.

## Why Does the Geographic Clash of Civilizations Occur?
The inevitability of the Geographic Clash of Civilizations occurs universally across civilizations due to the unavoidable asynchronous changes in the das-man and the body of civilizations (Figure 1). Why are these changes inevitable? Aside from natural factors such as earthquakes, volcanoes, and others, techne, as the primary human factor for crisis resolution, projects the das-man of civilizations and alters their physical structures.
Why are these changes asynchronous? By reason of:
- • A far slower speed of projection of das-man and the difficulty of being projected because of the dictatorial nature of das-man.
- • The faster speed of changes of/in the body because of advances of techne.
1. Far slower speed of projection of das-man of civilizations -- and the
difficulty of being projected because of the dictatorial nature of das-man.
The “they” is existential and belongs as a primordial phenomenon to the positive constitution of Da-sein. It itself has, in turn, various possibilities of concretion in accordance with Da-sein. The extent to which its dominance becomes penetrating and explicit may change historically. (Heidegger, 1996:120).
Hence, the transformation and projection of the das-man are not impossible, but due to the inherently dominant nature of the das-man, this projection and alteration occur across all civilizations at a significantly slower and more challenging pace:
“[…] since the “they” constantly accommodate Da-sein, it retains and entrenches its stubborn dominance. Everyone is the other, and no one is himself. The “they” which supplies the answer to the “who” of everyday Da-sein is the nobody to whom every Da-sein has always already surrendered itself, in its being-among-one-another.” (Heidegger, 1996:120).
As seen in Table 1, the gemüt layer, a component of the das-man within civilizations, encompasses all immaterial aspects of humanity. Similarly, Huntington, by highlighting the disparities among civilizations, acknowledges the significantly slow and challenging nature of das-man's evolution, echoing similar sentiments:
Differences among civilizations are not only real; they are basic.
Civilizations are differentiated from each other by history, language, culture, tradition and, most importantly, religion. The people of different civilizations have different views on the relations between God and man, the individual and the group, the citizen and the state, parents and children, husband and wife, as well as differing views of the relative importance of rights and responsibilities, liberty and authority, equality, and hierarchy.
These differences are the product of centuries. They will not soon disappear. They are far more fundamental than differences between political ideologies and political regimes. (Huntington, 1993:3).
From the geographic viewpoint (Table 1), political ideologies and political regimes are also a part of das-man of civilizations. They have been categorized as the hardware part (the civilization-managing). Based on what Huntington expressed, it can be said that changes of/in the software part of das-man (managers, thinkers, and dwellers) are more difficult and slower than in the hardware part of das-man.
It is possible that da-sein(s) of a geographic-civilizational territory (such as a country) change their pre-modern “dominant and formally structured discourses, ideologies, values, rules and organizations” to modern “dominant and formally structured discourses, ideologies, values, rules and organizations”, and vice versa, but being projected, “worldviews, beliefs, discourses, traditions, values and norms” of da-sein(s) (managers, thinkers, dwellers) occur slowly and with difficulty.
For instance, there are many countries where their political structure is republican and democratic, but their das-man is not democratic because - on basis of the GCC - dasman, in addition to structures, also involves three kinds of da-sein(s) (managers, thinkers and dwellers) and their values. In alignment with this perspective, Yuval Noah Harari (2016) writes:
People rarely manage to produce a completely new value. The last time this happened was in the eighteenth century, when the humanist revolution preached the stirring ideals of human liberty, human equality, and human fraternity (Harari, 2016: 378).
## 2. Faster speed of changes of/in the body of civilizations because of advances of techne.
In contrast to das-man, which is far slower and difficult to project and change, the bodies of civilizations have been increasingly modified over time by techne.
On the basis of Table 1, although technology  as mere means  is one of the nongeometric factors which lies in the man-made layer of civilization’s body, technology  as techne and a way of revealing (Heidegger, 1977:12)  occurs in all the soul and body of civilization (Tables 1 & 2).
So, what is techne?
We must observe two things with respect to the meaning of this word. One is that techne is the name not only for the activities and skills of the craftsman, but also for the arts of the mind and the fine arts. Techne belongs to bringing-forth, to poiesis; it is something poietic. The other point that we should observe regarding techne is even more important.
From earliest times until Plato the word techne is linked with the word episteme. Both words are names for knowing in the widest sense. They mean to be entirely at home in something, to understand and be expert in it. Such knowing provides an opening up. As an opening up it is a revealing (Heidegger, 1977:13).
According to the Greek sense of techne, it is linked with bringing-forth and episteme; and based on this reality, it gathers within itself four modes of occasioning-causality  and rules them throughout.” (Ibid:13), We can thus recognize two kinds of creations of techne with their layer of appearance in civilization (Table 2).
The four modes of occasioning-causality are: “(1) the causa materialis, the material, the matter out of which, for example, a silver chalice is made ; (2) the causa formalis, the form, the shape into which the material enters; (3) the causa finalis, the end, for example, the sacrificial rite in relation to which the chalice required is determined as to its form and matter; (4) the causa efficiens, which brings about the effect that is the finished, actual chalice, in this instance, the silversmith.” (Ibid:6).
On basis of these statements, (1) “the modes of occasioning, the four causes, are at play, then, within bringing-forth.” (Heidegger, 1977:11) and (2) there is also, through bringing-forth, the growing things of nature as well as whatever is completed through the crafts and the arts. (Ibid:11).
Table 2: The Two Kinds of Creations of Techne Based on Its Greek Senses3
There will be four kinds of creations:
 If a creation of techne is a material thing such as silver chalice, all the four modes of occasioning-causality are present in that material creation. Thus, all man-made creations which are made by materials of or in nature (the natural layer) or made materials by humans, lie in this kind of creation and in the body of civilization.
 If a creation of techne is an immaterial thing such as all of components of civilization’s soul which are not material (episteme, music, poem, thoughts and more), all the four modes of occasioning-causality are present except the causa materialis.
In short, techne is a way of revealing (Heidegger, 1977:12) and Technology  as mere means  is one kind (Table 2). Therefore, techne is the most mainly human (nonnatural) factor of changes in/of the soul and the body of civilization.
Due to the prevalent and persistent dominance of the das-man, changes induced by techne manifest in the physical aspects of civilizations, contrasting with the slower and more challenging alterations within the das-man(s) of civilizations.
The far slower and difficult changes within the das-man continually position it as the last aspect of a civilization to undergo transformation and projection. This occurs even later than its individual components, as per Heidegger’s understanding that the das-man is not merely the summation of its constituents. “The “who” is not this one and not that one, not oneself and some and not the sum of them all. The “who” is the neuter, the “they.” (Heidegger, 1996: 118-119).

## What Phenomena Do the Geographic Clash of Civilizations Gestate?
The speed of becoming projected as das-man - because of its dictatorial5 nature  is slow, but vital problems, crises, and dilemmas which das-man must resolve are more variable and emerge faster. Thus, the phenomenon of the Geographic Clash of Civilizations causes disability and failure amongst das-man(s) of civilizations in resolving their spiritual and bodily “life-or-death”6 crises. What the clash gestates is dependent on the reaction type of civilizations to the clash. There will be two civilizational possibilities/reactions:
- 1. Protection of das-man at the cost of not resolving “life-or-death” crises. Consequently, the clash gestates a “collapse of civilization.”
- 2. Projection of das-man at the cost of self-sacrifice da-sein(s). Consequently, the clash gestates “survival of civilization.”
 Protection of das-man at the cost of not being resolved via crises, in a final,
collapse of civilization:
Because of das-man’s dictatorial nature, das-man(s) have a fundamental tendency to protect.
This being-with-one-another dissolves one’s own Da-sein completely into the kind of being of:
“the others,” in such a way that the others, as distinguishable and explicit, disappear increasingly.7 In this inconspicuousness and unascertainability, the “they” unfolds its true dictatorship. (Heidegger, 1996:119).
Thus, if a civilization’s das-man becomes incapable of resolving crises and problems, it also attempts to protect itself, even at the cost of not resolving crises.

We enjoy ourselves and have fun the way they enjoy themselves. We read, see, and judge literature and art the way they see and judge. Thus, it is possible a das-man is projected to a new das-man with its new dictatorship, and with dissolving da-sein(s) (managers, thinkers, and dwellers) into its kind of being.

While the projection of a das-man is possible, it is an immensely challenging process. This transformation occurs over an extended period, transcending the lifetime of any individual da-sein.

Although projection of das-man is a historical phenomenon which unconsciously and unintentionally occurs, we cannot ignore conscious and intentional attempts of dasein(s) (managers, thinkers, and dwellers) to project das-man.
Although initially, “I” “am” not in the sense of my own self, but I am the others in the mode of the “they.” In terms of “they,” and as the “they,” I am initially “given” to “myself.” Initially, Da-sein is the they and it remains so. […] if Da-sein explicitly discovers the world and brings it near, if it discloses its authentic being to self, this discovering of the world and disclosing of Da-sein always comes about by clearing away coverings and obscurities, by breaking up the disguises with which Da-sein cuts itself off from itself. (Heidegger, 1996: 121).
In other words, when a das-man becomes unable to resolve life-or-death crises, the living da-sein(s) becomes impossible. Those da-sein(s) which explicitly discover the world and bring it near, enable them to disclose an authentic being to self (Ibid:121) and begin to attempt to change this situation.
This long-term historical moment is a contradictory-tragic moment. On one hand, dasein(s) must eliminate life-or-death crises to survive themselves; on the other hand, the eliminating of those crises needs to project the same repressive das-man which its projection needs  self-sacrifice of da-sein(s) (from sacrificing their pleasures, desires to their job, money, healthy, and even the most unavailable: their lives).
How many people must sacrifice? What do they have to sacrifice? That depends on the degree of repressiveness of the dominant das-man. Because the software (civilizationmanagers, civilization-thinkers, and civilization-dwellers) and the hardware (civilization-managing) of das-man(s) are different, their intensity of reactions to change will be different.
The worst das-man is a das-man in which da-sein(s) sacrifice themselves to protect their lives! At this historical moment, Kant’s “Have courage to use your own understanding!”11 (Kant, 1784: 1) goes on to  Have courage to self-sacrifice12.
In sum, projection of das-man occurs at the cost of the self-sacrifice of da-sein(s). Consequently, the Geographic Clash of Civilizations gestates a “surviving civilization.” The world revolves around the inventors of new values (Nietzsche, 2008: 37). But, also, the “invention of new values” itself revolves around a more fundamental value: self-sacrifice.
