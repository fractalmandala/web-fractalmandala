Julian Scott Literary Executor of the Estate of Christopher Dawson julian.scott@zen.co.uk Abstract
Christopher Dawson was a historian of religion and culture who wrote and lectured in England and America between the 1920s and his death in 1970. The author of this review, Dawson’s grandson and literary executor, argues not only that Dawson is very interesting to read as a historian, but also that many of his insights and proposals such as his writing on European unity, nationalism, the consequences of a secularized culture and the conflict between humane individualism and state totalitarianism remain highly relevant today. This article highlights some of Dawson’s most interesting and insightful ideas and aims to encourage readers to rediscover the writings of Christopher Dawson through his twenty-three books.
## Introduction
Since 2005, I have been acting as literary executor for my grandfather's estate, the historian Christopher Dawson (1889-1970). I have always felt drawn to his ideas, but only when I took over the literary executorship did I start reading his works in earnest. I have created a website with an introduction to each of his 23 books (www.christopherdawson.org ) to make his work known to a wider audience. In April 2011, I was invited to deliver a lecture at the University of St. Thomas, Minnesota, entitled The Life and Times of Christopher Dawson, which was subsequently published in The Political Science Reviewer in December 2017.1
My focus here is to highlight the many elements in Dawson’s work that continue to be relevant today and to encourage people to read his works. Christopher Dawson has a dedicated and growing following around the world, particularly in the U.S. and Brazil, but the readership remains relatively small. The reason for this, I believe, is, firstly, that one of Dawson’s main ideas is that religion is the main driving force in history. As we live in a predominantly secular world, this idea does not fit in with the current zeitgeist. The other reason is the fact that Dawson has been pigeonholed as a “Catholic historian” — and even a “Catholic apologist” — and is therefore seen by some as being only of interest to Catholics.
1 Scott, J., The Political Science Reviewer, Volume XLI (No. 2), 2017, 141.
Such labels do him a disservice because although he was indeed a devout Catholic and promoted the study of Christian culture, he was sympathetic to all forms of religion and spirituality. As he writes in The Crisis of Western Education, the world religions “are the great spiritual highways that have led mankind through history from remote antiquity to modern times.”2
On the other hand, he cannot be called an “apologist,” because while he regarded religion as the source of vitality in cultures, he acknowledged that it has often been a divisive force in history. He also acknowledged the obscurantism of the Dark Ages and the anti-scientific spirit of the later phase of Scholasticism.
His first book, The Age of the Gods, was a survey of prehistoric and ancient cultures. In some way, it is typical of his later works, because it is full of the penetrating and original insights which are so characteristic of his writing. This, I think, is what makes reading Dawson, whatever one’s background, so interesting.
In a sentence or a paragraph, he can give a key to understanding something about a culture or offer an original hypothesis to consider. In this work, for example, he points out that it was Egypt, not Rome, that initially gave the West its model of public administration. The Egyptian administration, he writes, “exerted a vital formative influence on the tradition of European state administration through its inheritance by the Hellenistic monarchies and the Roman Empire.”3
When discussing the prehistoric culture of Stonehenge, he uses the power of imaginative deduction to bring to life what Gordon Childe called “the dry bones of archaeology.” Making an analogy with the Irish fairs that lasted until well into the 19th century, where people would travel from near and far and engage not only in religious celebrations but also in games and barter, he conjures up an image of the vitality of the prehistoric religious festivals. He also uses the knowledge of Celtic customs that have come down to our days (such as the coronation ceremony in Westminster Abbey) to explain the veneration paid to sacred stones.
This sympathetic approach to ancient cultures, whose mentality might have been vastly different from our own, follows from one of his guiding principles as a historian, that a culture can only be understood from within.

## The Role of Religion in Culture
In The Age of the Gods, Dawson defines religion in the following terms: “Wherever and whenever man has a sense of dependence on external powers which are conceived as mysterious and higher than man’s own, there is religion.” Thus, he asserts, “the religious instinct is part of the nature of man.”4
Probably the most well-known idea of Christopher Dawson as a historian is that religion is one of the main driving forces of history. In Enquiries into Religion and Culture, he writes:
The great civilizations of the world do not produce the great religions as a kind of cultural by-product; in a very real sense, the great religions are the foundations on which the great civilizations rest. A society which has lost its religion becomes sooner or later a society which has lost its culture.5
This is one of his ideas that remains tremendously relevant today as our society becomes increasingly secularized. Although a Catholic himself, he approaches all religious expressions with respect and profound insight, even when he does not endorse them, and he sees a basic spiritual intuition at the root of all religious forms, from the most primitive to the most developed.
The idea that religion is the greatest source of vitality in a culture was in direct opposition to the prevailing notion of his time — that religion has always had a stultifying effect on culture.
In Medieval Essays, he shows the fallacy of this idea by pointing out that many of our scientific discoveries — in mathematics, chemistry, astronomy, architecture, etc. come from the Islamic world, which was a deeply religious culture. The European Middle Ages is often held up as the exemplar of a static culture, but in reality, there was a constant dynamism. It was a religious movement — the Crusades — that opened Europe up to the East and the civilizing influences of the Arabic world. From these influences came the ideas of chivalry and courtly love, which were later “Christianized”.
The great scientists of the Middle Ages, such as Robert Grosseteste and Roger Bacon, were mostly priests or monks who came up with many ground-breaking theories and inventions. Apart from his chemical discoveries, for example, Roger Bacon predicted “automobiles, flying machines and devices that will destroy a whole army at once.”6

He also warned against the dangers of science when not controlled by ethics. In his wide-ranging works, Dawson cites countless examples to illustrate his thesis.
An example Dawson gives of the vitalizing function of religion is that of Rome in the first century B.C.E., when it had conquered the world but lost its soul. Dawson shows how Augustus changed this situation with his vision of restoring the religious and social foundations of the culture, resulting in “the profoundly spiritual and catholic genius of Virgil.”7
Drawing a parallel with the 20th century, Dawson comments: “Our civilization stands in no less need of moral and social reconstruction.” We can see from this that, despite being a Catholic, Dawson was able to appreciate and respect the religious forces at work in a culture that had a religion totally different from Christianity. Continuing with the parallels with ancient Rome, he writes: “What our civilization lacks is not power and wealth and knowledge, but spiritual vitality.”8
## Political Views
Dawson wrote a range of what might be labelled “political works” where he applies his knowledge of history to the problems of the contemporary world, ranging from Religion and the Modern State (1936) to Understanding Europe (1952).
One of his most interesting claims is that the tendency towards totalitarianism can be seen not only in fascism and communism but also in the uniformity and mechanization of Western democracies. This was shown to tragi-comic effect in Charlie Chaplin’s film Modern Times (1936), but it could be argued that this tendency continues to prevail, though under different forms.
Dawson refers to it as a materialistic tendency towards the mechanization of human life and the complete subordination of the individual to the state and to the economic process. He believed that this tendency was equally present in capitalism, communism, and fascism, which he saw as simply three different forms of the same phenomenon.
An example he gives of the power of the state over the individual is the mobilization of millions of citizens who were persuaded to sacrifice themselves in the First World War, a war which is now recognized by many historians to have been unnecessary. He also used the example of the two World Wars to highlight the extreme violence and aggression of the 20th century, a fact which he attributed to the starvation and frustration of man’s spiritual nature.
- 7 Dawson, C., The Modern Dilemma (Sheed & Ward, 1935), 26
- 8 Dawson, C., Enquiries into Religion and Culture (The Catholic University of America Press, 2009), 242
Dawson frames this as a struggle between humane individualism and state-centered totalitarianism. The term “humane individualism” is a remarkably interesting one and Dawson himself was a particularly good example of it. He was immensely learned, he thought for himself, and his humanism was a belief in the sanctity of the human spirit but also a respect for the quality and traditions of each place, which make society “more human.”
Thus, in The Formation of Christendom, he expresses this soil and spirit duality in the following terms: “Every culture is like a plant. It must have its roots in the earth, and for sunlight it needs to be open to the spiritual. At the present moment we are busy cutting its roots and shutting out all light from above.”9
In our times there is often antagonism between those who have a cosmopolitan mindset and those who value their national or regional identity. A good example of this is the dichotomy of a united Europe, on the one hand, and national sovereignty on the other. Long before Brexit, Dawson authored a book dedicated to this subject, entitled Understanding Europe, which makes for very illuminating reading today. He also dealt with the subject twenty years earlier in The Modern Dilemma, which was subtitled The Problem of European Unity.
His views on the matter are by no means black and white but quite original. On the one hand, he has great sympathy for the idea of a united Europe and at the same time, a dislike of exclusivist nationalism.
Nationalism, he writes in The Movement of World Revolution, has never been a force for peace. “As a means of evoking common loyalty and common action within a single society, there is no denying the value and efficiency of nationalism. But as an ultimate principle of human action, it is morally inadequate and socially destructive.” 10 However, he does advocate what he calls “national life.” While recognizing the “evils and exaggerations of modern nationalism,” he believed that Europe has always been a “society of peoples”11 and therefore it is only by strengthening the unique traditions and values of each nation within a common European culture that Europe can be saved.
However, he did not believe this could be achieved by political and economic agreements but only by a restoration of the spiritual tradition(s) on which that unity was based. To quote from Understanding Europe:

- 10 Dawson, C., The Movement of World Revolution (The Catholic University of America Press, 2009), 112-113
- 11 Dawson, C., Understanding Europe (The Catholic University of America Press, 2009), 45
The activities of the modern planners and international reformers bear the same relation to the world crisis as the activities of a mining engineer or even a plumber to a volcanic eruption. They are sensible and rational and scientific, but they belong to an essentially different plane from that of the forces which they are attempting to master. The foundations of our world are shaken, and we shall not save it by replanning the superstructure.12
How, then, can the world be saved? His proposal was to reintroduce the spiritual into life through non-political institutions and, on an individual level, to turn the human mind from the circumference to the center. With this he returns to the whole problem of secularism which characterizes the modern world. The spiritual is the true life of civilizations, and all these divisions cannot be overcome without returning to the spiritual source.
## Education
Christopher Dawson believed that education is one of the most important tools in bringing about cultural change. “The scientists and the technicians, the philosophers and the theologians and the men of letters have played a larger part than the politicians themselves in producing those great changes of culture which have transformed the modern world”13.
In The Crisis of Western Education, he points out that for around 2,500 years, the European model of education (which has influenced many educational systems around the world) was based on the ‘liberal arts’ tradition that began in ancient Greece. This tradition originally had two strands: the first — the tradition of the liberal arts — was intended “to train men to be good citizens”14 by equipping them with the skills necessary to exercise their functions as free men in a democracy, such as rhetoric and a knowledge of history. The other strand was originated by Plato, who saw the role of education as being “to guide the mind by science and philosophy towards its final spiritual goal.”15
This tradition remained unbroken until the late 19th century, when the forces of utilitarianism and secularism led to greater specialization, particularly in the fields of science and technology, resulting in an increase in scientific and vocational courses and a reduction in the value given to the humanities.

The mind of the student is overwhelmed and dazed by the volume of new knowledge, which is being accumulated by the labor of specialists, while the necessity for using education as a stepping-stone to a profitable career leaves him little time to stop and think. And the same is true of the teacher, who has become a kind of civil servant tied to a routine over which he can have little control16.
Conscious of the lack of interest in the world of Greece and Rome which formed the basis of liberal education, and not denying the importance of specialist scientific and vocational courses to meet the demands of the modern world, Dawson nevertheless believed that a unifying vision of the spiritual sources of our culture was essential to the survival of Western civilization. He therefore proposed a 6-part programme for the study of Christian culture, starting with early Christianity and ending with secularized Christendom.
This program was first implemented by Bruno Schlesinger at St. Mary’s College, Notre Dame, and since then has been and continues to be implemented at different universities throughout the United States as a basis for the liberal arts.
## Dawson the Historian
Nowadays, it is extremely common for the study of history from a European perspective to be labeled “ethnocentric” or “colonialist.” This was already the case in Dawson’s time, and he was highly critical of this approach. In different works, he pointed out that the global civilization of the 20th century was, in many respects, an extension of Western ideas and values, such as individual freedom, democracy, and nationalism. International communism, for example, whether in Soviet Russia, Maoist China, some African states, or South America, was the child of Karl Marx, a German.
The anti-colonial nationalism of countries like India, China, or the Middle East and Africa was born out of the nationalism that had developed in Europe. Those who came to the West to learn about its technology, Dawson writes, returned as “converts” to its ideas and then became “apostles” of those ideas in their own countries.17
In this context, he states that “all the living forces that are moving the world today, whether scientific, economic or political, have their roots in European culture and would wither if that culture were to break down”.18 According to Dawson, the survival or Europe and civilization in general depends on four ideals: the Christian ideal, the ideal of humanity, the scientific ideal and the ideal of democracy.

How did today’s globalized world come about? According to Dawson, it was the result of the “energy of spiritual unrest”19 in Western culture, which he regards as a side effect of the worldview of the Christian religion and its missionary spirit — “a spirit that strives to incorporate itself in humanity and to change the world”20. He traces the source of the dynamic element in Christian culture to the tension between two distinct social traditions and spiritual worlds: the warrior society of the barbarian kingdoms with its ethos of heroism and the society of the Christian Church with its ideals of asceticism and sanctity.
Dawson was a self-proclaimed metahistorian,21 a concept which he not only defends but positively advocates, pointing out that the great historians such as Tocqueville and Ranke were all possessed of a distinctive worldview.22 It is not necessary, in his view, for historians to aspire to a kind of clinically scientific impartiality.
## Some Conclusions
I would like to end by highlighting a few points which, in my view, make Christopher Dawson an extremely stimulating and rewarding author to read.
One is his style, which is lucid, elegant and at times poetic, as many contemporary reviewers remarked.23 For example, speaking of the effects of Rousseau’s philosophy in The Gods of Revolution, Dawson writes:
It is impossible to exaggerate the effect of Rousseau’s teaching on his generation. It came into the brilliant artificial world of the Enlightenment like a warm west wind from the fields into a lighted salon, extinguishing the tapers and filling the air with the scent of damp earth and rain-soaked vegetation.24
He also makes the reader think, because of the originality of his approaches and his freedom from academic shackles. For example, we can note the “ethnocentric” question, the dichotomy of nationalism and cosmopolitanism, or the issue of the “uniformity and mechanization of Western democracies” discussed above.

His originality was partly due to the fact that he was not part of the academic establishment but an independent scholar, and one of his great merits was that he is always sympathetic towards the cultures he was writing about, whether it is prehistoric peoples, the ancient Romans, or the Islamic culture of Spain.
Some of his insights into religious and cultural questions can help the reader understand certain perplexing issues about history, such as his thesis that the division between Catholics and Protestants was more cultural and psychological than theological or the idea that a culture is never a closed system, but is constantly being subjected to outside influences which modify it.
- As regards his guiding idea that religion is the principle of vitality in any civilization, it makes one think about how it might be possible to move away from the secular and materialist model of civilization. It is interesting to note that he does not advocate a return to the Middle Ages but a reintroduction of the spiritual into life through nonpolitical institutions.
His erudition is extraordinary,25 without ever being dry or pedantic. He was also an example of interdisciplinary study, being immensely knowledgeable in many different fields, such as anthropology, sociology, comparative religion, and world literature. As a result, one of the effects of reading his works is the desire to know more about the historical figures and phenomena he mentions.
Finally, I think that one of his most attractive qualities as a writer is that his approach is always humane, “liberal” (in the Latin sense of the free human being), broadminded, and ecumenical. During his time as vice-president of The Sword of the Spirit, a wartime movement founded by Cardinal Hinsley, whose aim was to unite all people of goodwill against totalitarianism, he had many battles with more narrow-minded Catholics of the time.
Although he is at present still a marginal figure, in his own time, his work was praised by such notable and diverse figures as Aldous Huxley, T.S. Eliot26 and Arnold Toynbee, as well as the philosophical essayist E.W.F. Tomlin27 and the world expert on Eastern religions, R.C. Zaehner.

I hope this review will inspire readers to explore the works of Christopher Dawson and discover in them an enlightening new source of knowledge and insight.
