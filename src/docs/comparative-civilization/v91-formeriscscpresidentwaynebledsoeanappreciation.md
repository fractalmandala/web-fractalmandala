Michael Palencia-Roth Past President, ISCSC, 1986-1992 Emeritus Professor of Comparative and World Literature University of Illinois palencia@illinois.edu
About three weeks before Wayne Bledsoe died on the 2nd of June 2024, at 83, my wife Elaine and I commented to each other that we simply had to get in touch with him and Deb again, for too much time had passed since we had seen each other. We did not know that he was ill, and so the news of his death surprised and distressed us.
Though the genre of this professional appreciation obliges me to write of Wayne as a colleague and scholar, I must also write of him from more personal perspectives, for it was his person and his manner that, indeed, made him so effective as an editor of the Comparative Civilizations Review, and as President of the ISCSC, and as so good a friend.
Wayne was born on the 29th of January 1941 in Winston-Salem, North Carolina. He never forgot his North Carolina roots. He was the first of his family to go to college. After graduating from Tennessee Temple University, he attended graduate school at Michigan State University, where he received both his M.A. and Ph.D. degrees.
In his dissertation, on “Problems in Medieval Historiography (1969),” Wayne explored a paradigm shift in the historiography of the civilizing process in the West. He argued cogently against the notion that the Middle Ages was a “dark age” and explored the shifts in historiography that heralded the period between 800 and 1300 as an initial blossoming of Western Civilization, an interpretation also put forward, for example, by scholars like R.W. Southern in The Making of the Middle Ages, Jacques Le Goff in Medieval Civilization, 400-1500, and Norman F. Cantor in The Civilization of the Middle Ages.
Beginning in 1968, Wayne spent his entire career at the University of Missouri-Rolla, was promoted to full professor in 1982 and served as Chair of the History and Political Science department for four years before retiring as emeritus in 2004.
He won the University of Missouri system’s most prestigious prize, the Thomas Jefferson Award, in 1995. He won the Outstanding Teacher Award six times; tributes from his former students were the kind that every professor would be deeply grateful for. I find such universal praise unusual these days for a teacher who taught almost entirely material from the history of Greece and Rome through the Middle Ages.
These are details available in the public record. They do not do justice to the kind of scholar/member he was for the ISCSC. I first met him in 1987 after becoming President of the ISCSC in 1986. He was then about to be named co-editor of the Comparative Civilizations Review with Vytautas Kavolis, its founding editor and the Society’s first American president after Benjamin Nelson. Vytas was a forbidding presence to many members: intense, rational, forthright, an editor and scholar of remarkably high standards. Wayne was more gentle, more “Southern” if you will. The Lithuanian and the Southerner made quite a team.
Their uncompromising devotion to quality was recognized in the late 1990s or early 2000s when the educational ministry of Spain independently selected the CCR as one of the top twenty comparative journals in the world. Wayne edited the CCR, both with Vytas and on his own, from fall 1990 through spring 1996.
Wayne produced civilizational essays of high quality. For example, he wrote on the theories of the origins of civilization for a volume entitled The Boundaries of Civilization in Space and Time, edited by Matthew Melko and Leighton Scott. His introduction to his edited book, The Advent of Civilization (1975), presents a survey of the significant issues as good as one would ever wish for.
His essay on “Globalization and Comparative Civilizations: Looking Backwards to See the Future” (CCR, no. 45) is a lucid defense of the need for comparative civilizational analysis in today’s world.
He ends the essay with a statement that could well be part of a mission statement for the ISCSC: “Comparative civilizationists are best qualified to demonstrate, particularly to developing nations, the need to stop thinking of international economic integration [globalization] as an end in itself and the need for developing nations to engage in the world economy on their own terms and not on the terms set by multilateral institutions.”
In addition to doing superb work on the journal, Wayne helped organize meetings from 1991 through 2006. He was most central to the meetings in St. Louis (1999), AlabamaMobile (2000), Rutgers (2001), Frenchman’s Cove in Jamaica (2002), St. Petersburg in Russia (2003), and Paris with the École Pratique des Hautes Études (2006).
Presiding over a conference is not an easy task because tragedies, problems and complaints sometimes impact a President’s work.
In Jamaica, for instance, a long-time member, having received a terminal cancer diagnosis, swam out to sea, and disappeared, leaving behind a distraught companion and conference attendees. Another member, ignoring this tragedy, complained to Wayne about the conference being held so close to the ocean because its waves made too much noise at night. During the Russia meeting, one member tried to split off several sessions into another venue.
These are but a fraction of the issues and difficulties that besiege every ISCSC president. What I would like to remember most, however, are the moments I and other members shared with Wayne at ISCSC meetings. Let me call one to mind. The Paris meeting of 2006 occurred during the final of the World Cup. France was playing Italy. On the night of the championship game, Wayne insisted and would not be denied though we were dead tired from a very long day at the conference — that he, Deb, Elaine, and I go to a bar not far from our hotel that he had scouted beforehand.
We got there about an hour before the start of the game, and it was already packed with fans of the French team and of the great Zidane (Italy’s fans were in another bar on the next block). After a few rounds of beer, everyone became raucous and happy. I remember a couple of Frenchmen buying us a round or two. And we reciprocated.
After the match, as we enjoyed the glow of the French victory, a man of about thirty came to our table, sat down, and demanded that we have another beer. He then blearily thanked us and the US Army for D-Day, saying that he owed America his life. I sensed a story behind his alcoholic haze and asked, how so? During the invasion of Normandy, he said, the Americans had saved his grandfather from being executed by the Germans.
Only after the war did that then-young man go on to have a family and thus, more than fifty years later, make it possible for his grandson to sit at our table on that warm Parisian night. Often, the best encounters at ISCSC meetings occur on the margins.
After Wayne retired in 2004, he devoted himself principally to bluegrass music. Having founded the Bluegrass Now magazine in 1990, he was at the center of Bluegrass, its music, and its culture. He hosted two radio programs for decades, one on Saturday night, the other on Sunday morning. One listener commented on “sitting in the dark, listening to that music, and hearing Wayne’s gentle voice. It was almost holy.”
Wayne not only spoke; he also wrote. And in 1996, he was named “Writer of the Year” by the International Bluegrass Music Association (IBMA); he was also honored as a “Kentucky Colonel” for his service to the state. These were just two of the many honors and tributes that he received.
Wayne taught also in Australia, England, and France, always on civilizational issues. Through his teaching, lecturing, and devotion to music, he made the world a better place. And through his commitment to civilizational studies and the ISCSC, he made us a better Society.
