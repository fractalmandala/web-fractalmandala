Liah Greenfeld University Professor and Professor of Sociology, Political Science, and Anthropology, Boston University lvg@bu.edu
The purpose of this essay is to offer several hypotheses based on the concept of civilization and the conceptual scaffolding for the study of the cultural reality, proposed in the Spring 2024 CCR paper,1 regarding the three civilizations coexisting in the world today: the monotheistic, the Sinic, and the Indic. It introduces the modal dimensions along which civilizations may differ on the level of the individual mind — cognitive (pertaining both to the structure and contents of thought), moral, and emotional; and that of the society (separate institutions and institutional systems) and history.
In each civilization these modal dimensions reflect, and may be deduced from, the first civilizational principles, i.e., the ideas framing the fundamental image of reality of a given civilization. Codified in the written language, developed in the process of the formulation of these principles, i.e., simultaneously with their expression, they are subsequently (for the duration of the existence of this language and others formed in translation from it, that is, on its foundation) transmitted through language itself. Thus they shape, i.e., orient, open to certain possibilities, and limit the mind through the neural conditioning of the brains of the language users.
As discussed in my previous CCR essay,2 the mind is an integral, seamless process, the dimensions of which can be observed as separate only in the state of disintegration or illness. Analytically, however, cognitive, moral, and emotional dimensions are distinct, and focusing on each of these dimensions in turn allows for a deeper understanding of the whole.
The first dimension to be distinguished for the purpose of the present analysis is that of the mode of thought, both because it is most closely related to language and is defined by it as it develops in the process of the articulation of the first principles (that is, the mode of thought comes into existence simultaneously with the articulation of the first principles and the evolution of the language which codifies them), and because all the other modal dimensions of civilization are clearly dependent on, and reflect, it.

- 2 In much greater detail, the nature of the mind as an element of the cultural reality is discussed in my Mind, Modernity, Madness: The Impact of Culture on Human Experience, Ch. 2, “The Mind as an Emergent Phenomenon,” Harvard University Press, 2013.
These other modal dimensions include: 1) contents of thought: cognitive and moral concepts — ideas regarding various aspects of reality and values underlying institutions; and 2) corresponding emotions or psychological dynamics. On the collective, societal level of the cultural process these mental modal dimensions express themselves in the historical trajectories or patterns of change of institutional systems.
Of all these analytically discrete elements, the first civilizational principles and modes of thought are the most durable. They prescribe the parameters within which changes in other dimensions may occur and are impermeable to the influences of these historical changes. Contents of thought, which are changeable, affect psychological dynamics, and both concepts, values, and psychological dynamics influence changes in institutional systems. But nothing prevents a feedback loop between developments in these dimensions; in general, they are interdependent.
The present analysis focuses on the monotheistic civilization as native and most familiar to most of the readers of this journal (and yet, still poorly understood). It will serve as a blueprint for the analyses of the other two civilizations on which I plan to collaborate with scholars who are expert in those civilizations.
These will be identical to this essay in their structure, the analysis following the Sinic and the Indic civilizations from their first civilizational principles through characteristic modes of thought, concepts and values, psychological dynamics, and historical trajectories. The juxtaposition of the three separate analyses will form a systematic cross-civilizational comparison. In this essay, these other two civilizations will be referenced only for contrast.
## Monotheism: First Principles
The definition of the “cultural family” (per Durkheim and Mauss) 3 to which European/Western societies belong as monotheistic civilization, including Russia and, most importantly, Islam, which, from the moment of their appearance, have been significant others for European/Western societies4 allows one to answer Weber’s question why in the West and only in the West “cultural phenomena have appeared which (as we would like to think) lie in a line of development having universal significance and value”.5

The underlying postulate of this civilization — of One God, the Creator of the Universe, the source of all, one generative will, one consistent intelligence behind the whole of existence — presupposes universality. This image of God implies One World, united and consistently ordered, its principles, when revealed and correctly understood in any sphere, apply to everything in it alike.
It is possible that the universality of God is a late feature of Judaism, developed out of the more primitive (elemental) idea of the exclusive relationship between God and the people of Israel. In the history of religions, the exclusivity of this relationship was already a significant departure from the existing rule, an innovation which sharply distinguished the emerging people of Israel from the surrounding communities, who also each had their gods.
“The gods of the nations (goyim),” writes Reuven Firestone of religious communities of the ancient Near East, “surrounding Israel protected their tribes or peoples just as the God of Israel protected its own people… But [these other religious communities] had more flexibility in their worship. Their gods… did not seem to require absolute and exclusive obedience as did the God of Israel. It would not be uncommon for a member of a tribal religion visiting a neighboring people to make an offering to the neighbor’s god(s). Not so among Israel, or at least not according to the covenantal agreements.”6
Such unusual insistence on exclusivity would make sense in the light of the equally unusual history of the people of Israel which, as scholars now think based on the juxtaposition of Biblical history and archeological research, was created intentionally by a group of indigenous rebels against the hierarchical ancient regime in their explicit desire to distinguish themselves ideologically from it.7 This group consciously wanted to redefine itself, create for itself a new identity, and to do so, in the conditions of the time, would need a new and unusual god to believe in, a god who would keep the community separate from and unable to mix with others by forbidding them to accept others’ beliefs — insisting, in other words, on their absolute and exclusive obedience to himself.
Such a god, exclusive to Israel, could in principle coexist with other gods, and so long as the community worshipped him to the absolute exclusion of other gods, did not have to be universal. Exclusivity of the relationship alone would sustain a new, separate, and distinctive identity.8

But identities, whether individual or communal, usually are not created intentionally and, having emerged (however created), are never experienced as intentionally created.
As a rule, identities are created accidentally and involve very little forethought, or any thought. They are products of contingent historical circumstances, because of which people are brought together, stay together due to social inertia, and gradually develop a characteristic way of life and common self-perception.
The explicit verbal thought that is necessarily involved in an intentional creation, however, leads to more thought, making thought more complex and ordered through interpretation and reinterpretation, interpreting experiences in its light, articulating it orally, and in the process of articulation, encouraging writing. So developed, thought gives rise to principles which then dictate behavior — the way (of life), in Hebrew halakhah – laying down rules.
This symbolic development is gradual, certainly taking several generations, and during it the original intention to create a separate identity is realized and forgotten. The intentionally created identity is experienced as natural to the community: this is who we are, and this is our way of life. The framework for a specific cultural process – a civilization – is established. With the special identity of the people of Israel a fait accompli and the reason behind the exclusive relationship between it and the God of Israel forgotten, this exclusivity, which put the Israelite worship in a class by itself, naturally raised questions.
It was not self-explanatory and needed justification. If the God of Israel were the One and Only God, the exclusivity of Israelite worship of Him would follow directly. This explains why the first book of the Hebrew Bible, Genesis, which postulates the one universal God, was not the first one written. It also explains why ancient Israelites a people with a clear identity — did not “religiously”, that is, necessarily, keep to the straight and narrow path of the exclusive worship of their God:
One of the nagging issues that plagued biblical Israel was its backsliding from absolute obedience to its zealous God. Many biblical passages describe Israelites engaging in foreign devotion, the worship of other gods. This would suggest that the notion of monotheism, of a truly universal deity, was not a universal belief in ancient Israel. The Bible is a kind of devotional history of a people, and current biblical scholarship agrees that true and consistent monotheism does not become dominant among the Israelites until at least the time of the classical prophets. This was not until sometime around the destruction of the First Temple in the sixth century BCE.9
9 Reuven Firestone, op. cit., p. 114; Nili Fox, “The Concepts of God in Israel and the Question of Monotheism,” pp. 341-343 in G. Beckman and T. Lewis, eds., Text, Artifact, and Image: Revealing Ancient Israelite Religion, 2006.
It should be stressed that the behavior of a population is only one, and not necessarily the most accurate, marker of the character (contents, principles) of a belief system. Not even actual beliefs of a population would often accurately reflect it. A belief system requires articulation. Most people do not have systematic or even articulate beliefs unless they are spelled out in authoritative texts. A complex, elaborate belief system such as Jewish monotheism (which, among other things, encourages thinking and discourages ritualistic repetition that leads to the formation of tropes) must be codified in written language and transmitted over generations before it can be expected to be adequately attested to in widespread practice.
In the sixth century BCE, such a codification of monotheism came into being in the Hebrew Bible. There it was: consistent monotheism, with a truly universal deity at its source, and the monotheistic image of reality.10
## Logic in Monotheism
The monotheistic image of God implies privileging the logical mode of thought with its principle of no contradiction. The essential unity of the world intentionally created by the One and Only God (with its innate universality) encourages the assumption of the logical character of the universe, i.e., a belief that the principles of logic are discoverable in the universe. (Incidentally, this is a belief that lies at the root of mathematics and eventually suggests the placing of mathematics at the root of physics.)
Necessarily, the mind that reflects and comes into being together with monotheistic culture (civilization) is a logical mind. We believe that the logical character of the monotheistic vision of reality calls for the logical thinking to appreciate and interpret it and that the principles of logic are discoverable through the examination of thinking. This prompts us, the denizens of the monotheistic civilization, to argue that logic is the natural human way of thinking, that it is hard-wired into the human brain, in fact being the characteristic of the human species which most sharply distinguishes us from other animals.

Yet, this presumption is wrong on both accounts — logic distinguishing humans from other animals and logic being humanity’s characteristic mode of thinking. Logic is indeed a natural way of thinking in the sense that its principles are discoverable in the cognitive functioning of all animals with highly developed brains. Mammals and birds are obviously incapable of formalizing logic and thus cannot boast of having a furry or feathered Aristotle. But, as proven by numerous animal experiments conducted by neuroscientists, compared to humans, wild animals, contrary to our presumption, are firmer logicians.
On the whole, when comparing randomly selected group vs. randomly selected group, the logical performance of wild animals is invariably better than ours (as, inter alia, is so convincingly demonstrated by the disappointing results of our IQ tests, imperfect even when administered to arguably the most intelligent populations, when compared to the stellar, 100% correct record of run-of-the-mill rats in solving transitive inference puzzles11). Besides, the cognition of wild animals is necessarily or exclusively logical.
As explained elsewhere:
There are very good reasons…why rats are, in general, better than humans at solving logical puzzles. An animal exists in a world naturally limited by the place its species occupies in the evolutionary process, its own evolutionary niche to which it is, by definition, well adapted, and which it, therefore, perceives as consistently ordered. It does not and, unless caught by us, cannot know any other reality. The physical environment presents itself to an animal in terms of signs, which, unlike symbols, are not open to interpretation. A piece of food is a piece of food and cannot under any circumstances turn into a representation of a divine power or be experienced as ‘impure’ and, as a result, become inedible; a predator feeding on one’s kind is a danger to be escaped and will not be interpreted as a possible partner in peaceful coexistence. This, and the biological intolerance of physical contradiction (stimuli, whether genetic or environmental, which simultaneously instruct the organism to do A and to do –A) necessitate that any relationship between quanta of environmental information present itself to the animal in accordance with the strictest rules of logic.
A rat, of course, does not know that it makes a ‘transitive inference’: in this sense, its mental — imaginative — process literally represents ‘jumping to conclusions,’ i.e., traveling unconsciously some distance over individual links of a logical chain.

Human imagination, in contrast, works differently.
Cultural environment presents itself as systems of symbols, rather than as sets of signs, and, as every symbol, whatever it is in fact, is at the same time more than it is by nature of being a symbol, most symbolic systems are not subject to the fundamental logical principle which states that a thing may not be and not be at the same time. Being and not being (or having and not having a quality) simultaneously is a regular occurrence in the human world and, as a rule, arouses no consternation.
This is particularly clear when one compares the monotheistic universe with the disorderly cosmos of Greek polytheism.13 The latter has no principles behind it. It emerges from the nothingness of the primeval chaos haphazardly, originally taking the shape of the anthropomorphic pantheon — immortal superhumans (gods conceived in the image of man), subject to no law whatsoever — and later through a series of accidents springing the world of mortal men. Gods rule this world, but the gods themselves are essentially unruly. Self-seeking and capricious, basically irate, and easily provoked, touchy and tetchy, supremely sensitive to offenses and jealous in their pride, they are quick to anger and are in constant need of appeasement and flattery. Disloyal and undisciplined, submissive only when coerced, having no regard for family ties, incestuous, constantly betraying and trying to undermine each other, they involve mortals in their endless quarrels. Playthings to these erratic masters, humans live their lives subject to fate, which they cannot anticipate.
The Greek cosmos is not a unity. Homer’s protagonists live simultaneously under many masters and many laws. These masters are warring, and the laws are inconsistent. Everything is full of contradictions, which nobody perceives as contradictions. Monsters — creatures of contradictory nature — are de rigueur. Zeus, the ruler of Olympus, whose rule is forever thwarted, is immortal, and swans are mortal, yet, pursuing Leda (for the Thunderer is unable to control even his own libido), Zeus is a swan. The principle of no contradiction does not apply here.
This was the cultural framework of a specific historical society for over a millennium. It shaped its people’s idea of reality and affected their existential experience. We can only regard it as fantastic, a product of imagination completely unrestrained by reality, and our psychiatrists could consider it schizophrenic.

But, clearly, though Ancient Greeks must have perceived their world as exceedingly complex and, in comparison to us, taking surprise in stride, had extremely high tolerance for the unexpected, they, no less than we, were perfectly able to distinguish (what was for them) the real from the unreal. They were not delusional, not mentally disordered. Patently, they were not discomfited by what we would be discomfited.
Unlike us, they simply did not privilege logic above other modes of thought. It is not surprising, therefore, that classicists are taken aback by and find it impossible to explain “the transition from mythos to logos” circa the fifth century BCE, which, as a result, they call “the Greek miracle.”14
The unquestionable idea in our world that logical thinking is a universal human attribute — the normal way of thinking — which leads us to consider illogical thinking a symptom of the severest psychiatric ailment, schizophrenia, makes it impossible for us to imagine that a human population anywhere can live for centuries thinking differently. For us, this would mean that this population lacks a built-in characteristic of the human brain and is not fully human. Least of all can we imagine this of the society which we consider the cradle of our civilization!
This is why a British, French or Russian scholar of Ancient Greece would feel uncomfortable employing the vernacular word unambivalently referring to logical thinking (“logic”) in the description of the sudden and colossal shift in the mode of thought from the haphazard and unpredictable continuities characterizing the body of Ancient Greek original literary tradition to the regular principles forming the foundation of Greek philosophy, which occurred around the 5th century BCE (allowing Jaspers, as we remember, to include Ancient Greece among the Axial civilizations and Eisenstadt to consider the 5th century BCE an axial moment)15.

The recognition of a sudden and inexplicable change from one mode of thought to another does not have to presuppose an opposition between the two (i.e., “reason vs. myth or rational vs. irrational” or “progress from benighted credulity to at least the dawning of some form of critical enlightenment”) as is presupposed by William Wians in “From Logos and Muthos to…,” his introductory essay to Logos and Muthos: Further Essays in Greek Philosophy and Literature., SUNY Press, 2019. It is quite enough to acknowledge that the two modes of thought are radically different. The valiant defense of myth from the presumed accusation of primitivity piles one misinterpretation on another and throws the baby out with the bath water. The task is to explain why the transition occurred, not to pretend that there was no transition.

The straightforward “logic,” the meaning of which in the scholarly community is in no doubt, is substituted by the hazy “logos” which the reader is invited to interpret at will; this calls for “mythos” instead of “myth,” and we end with an intentionally disorienting road-sign to a central event in cultural history which, ostensibly, we would like to understand.
The unfathomable by definition “Greek miracle,” as all will agree, is a cultural event, i.e., an event in symbolic reality. Like any event of this nature, it is contingent on its historical context. What was the new element that changed the context of the Greek culture immediately before the fifth century BCE, bringing about this momentous intellectual revolution? It was the first systematic presentation of monotheism (Judaism) in the redaction of the Hebrew Bible in Babylon.
The redaction was accomplished in the half-century between 587 and 538 BCE. In 587, Nebuchadnezzar II, having destroyed Jerusalem in retaliation for the Jews’ unwillingness to take the side of Babylon in its war with Egypt and insistence on their sovereignty, reduced the independent kingdom of Judah to the status of a Babylonian province (Yehud). He exiled the Jewish elite to his capital, where, deprived of their Temple, which Nebuchadnezzar had razed together with the city where it stood, they decided to compile and make themselves the community of the Book. In 537 BCE, Cyrus the Great of Persia captured Babylon, less than a century after the Babylonian Empire replaced the Assyrian one, of which it was previously a province, and which destroyed the Jewish Kingdom of Israel.16 The next year, Cyrus allowed the exiled Jews to return home.
In the intervening 50 years, in the cosmopolitan intellectual circles in Babylon, where Aramaic was the lingua franca and the exiled Jews represented a salient segment, the Jewish Book must have been the talk of the town. Coming back to their native lands, foreign visitors who participated in its discussion carried everywhere in Asia Minor the strange, novel, and attractively orderly monotheistic worldview, which made it possible to connect diverse aspects of reality and make sense of it all, rendering existence understandable if not predictable.
It is likely that reverberations of the conversation in Babylon were especially audible in nearby Miletus, which would explain the otherwise inexplicable leading role of Milesians in the “Greek Miracle.”17

It is even eminently possible that the leader of “the transition from mythos to logos,” widely regarded as the first Greek philosopher and even “father of science,” Thales of Miletus (c. 626/623 – c. 548/545 BC), lived in Babylon at the time. Admittedly, none of the ancient sources is fully trustworthy, but no ancient authority contradicts those later writers who claim he did. It is generally accepted that Thales introduced logic in Greece, being the first to practice deductive reasoning and to argue that everything that exists comes from a single arche — variously translated as “first principle,” “authority,” “beginning,” “origin,” “source,” “command,” and “ultimate indemonstrable principle.”18
The sudden appearance in Greece of the core monotheistic idea and the mode of thought it implies precisely at the time when these ideas and mode of thought are systematically articulated in nearby Babylon, if not related, would be too much of a coincidence. Without taking the first redaction of the Hebrew Bible into consideration, “the transition from mythos to logos” around the fifth century BCE remains unexplainable — indeed a miracle.
The idea of a miracle — per OED, an event inexplicable by natural laws and therefore considered to be the work of a divine agency — presupposes nature. In this context, it is important to note that together with logic, the Milesian philosophers, Thales and his two followers, were responsible, in Lloyd’s words, for “the discovery of nature.” He continues: “By the ‘discovery of nature,’” he writes, “I mean the … recognition that natural phenomena are not the products of random or arbitrary influences, but regular and governed by determinable sequences of cause and effect.”19
This “discovery” of what appears to us, denizens of the monotheistic civilization, absolutely self-evident (and what, clearly, did not appear so to the Greeks of earlier times) must also be related to the ground ideas of monotheism just articulated and systematically presented in the Babylonian Bible — specifically, the idea of one consistently organized world, certainly startling for the polytheistic consciousness.
The Hebrew Bible represents the compilation of disparate texts written over a period of at least a thousand years. The monotheistic image of reality presupposes one world, a universe, created and ordered in consistence with His nature by One God. This consistent order of the world (still the fundamental assumption on which all science is based) makes the mind sensitive to deviations from (any idea of) this order, which are perceived as irksome contradictions requiring an explanation.

It took two centuries, i.e., 6-4, according to Lloyd (ibid.), for the new concepts and mode of thought inspired by monotheism to be processed in the best Greek minds to the extent of coining Greek terms for them and allowing clear definition and formalization – a development which found its fullest realization in Aristotle.
The principle of no contradiction, in turn, favors binary thinking — thinking in terms of good vs. evil, truth vs. falsehood, spirit vs. matter, and so on. More generally, it calls for exclusive terms, “either/or, but never both together,” and thus requires the creation of categories and encourages clear (relative to other images of reality, of course) definitions of phenomena and concepts.
The formulation of the monotheistic image of reality, in other words, necessitated at the very least the development of an extensive special vocabulary to present and discuss it, of the kind that none of the languages of the surrounding polytheistic populations possessed. This vocabulary then allowed for the further development of the monotheistic vision, encouraging the corresponding logical mode of thought.
In the original texts that the redactors of the Babylonian Bible carried with them into exile (written and oral, on scrolls and in memory), the language of monotheism was already developed. But the narrators who composed and copied them over numerous generations sometimes (and inevitably) contradicted each other. The redacted Bible included the contradictory passages, and yet mightily strengthened the logical character of the overall narrative.
The original texts were assembled not in the order in which they were written but in a logical order, so impressively declared by the opening words of the opening book, Genesis: “In the beginning” (BeReshit – at first).20 The logical mode of thought ingrained in the basic monotheistic idea of One God, the Creator and King of the Universe (Elohim Boreh Ha-Olam; Elohim Melech Ha-Olam) and perpetuated through the structure and language of the Bible set in motion a dynamic, inherently creative cultural process of the monotheistic civilization, carried on by encountering and eliminating contradictions and capable of continuation even in the absence of this idea.
The dynamism of the monotheistic civilization is a function of its special logical character, the place logic occupies in it, and of the intolerance of contradictions logic implies. Potential for change is built into the cultural process in this case. The sensitivity to contradictions which must be resolved, and their creative resolution, allows developments in unanticipated directions while contained within the logical framework, created by the idea of One God, and therefore always dependent on the first civilizational principles. This development generates new traditions all the while preserving the continuity of the civilizational process.

Greek mythology, clearly, was incapable of development in this sense. It lacked the generative potential for qualitative change and could grow, if at all, only quantitatively, producing more of the same (more gods or more stories about them). In Russian Formalist terms applicable to cultural change in general, it lacked the ability to evolve and could transform only by way of genesis, i.e., requiring an inspiration, a push from the outside.21
The monotheistic image of reality as consistently organized contrasts with the incoherent, chaotic vision of Greek polytheism (in which some see the former’s roots) primarily because ancient Greek culture was not a civilization: it lacked first principles that could translate themselves in systems of social institutions. (Of course, institutions there were, and they were organized in systems of poleis. But the relationship between the established ways of thinking and acting in the poleis and in different spheres within each of them, on the one hand, and their polytheistic religion with its shared myths, on the other, was not hierarchical. Myths did not exert causal influence on other institutions but, rather like the institution of literature or entertainment in modern society, coexisted with them in an unsystematic interaction.)
However, at least as striking a contrast to the inherently logical monotheistic image is presented by the Sinic and Indic civilizations, both very well ordered based on their respective first principles. A Buddhist maxim, commonly evoked in China and Japan, 事々無礙, translated verbatim as “thing, thing, no boundary,” declares that anything can at the same time be anything else. From the logical point of view, an explicit contradiction of the principle of no contradiction exemplifies the kind of thinking at the very least allowed, i.e., legitimate, in both Indian and Chinese cultural families. This is not to say that logical thinking within the Chinese or Indian civilizations — if such thinking is required by the nature of activity itself, like chess rules are required for playing chess, e.g., in science —is less rigorous than elsewhere. But there is no impetus within these two civilizations, as there is within monotheism, to equate logic of no contradiction with rules of human thought in general.
Analytically, one can distinguish in the foundation of any civilization several layers, the initial one being that of the basic idea itself that invokes all the others. These layers of the basic idea can be articulated as the first civilizational principles, and in the case of the monotheistic civilization include:

First civilizational principles call into being a characteristic (civilizational) mode of thought — in the case of the monotheistic civilization, logic.22 Together, the first principles and mode of thought lead to the cardinal values and ideas, or moral and cognitive concepts of a civilization. In the case of the monotheistic civilization, the first civilizational principles, starting with the fundamental idea of God the Creator of the universe consistent with His nature, and the logical mode of thought, ramify into core values and concepts such as good and evil, truth, justice, which we believe to be universal, but which are unique to the monotheistic civilization. ## Truth and Justice
Given the privileged position of logic in it, the central values of the monotheistic civilization are truth and justice. It is very telling that neither in Ancient Greece prior to the fifth century nor in the two great Asian civilizations (Sinic and Indic) are they given much importance. Both directly express the desire for consistency and mean consistency with fundamental (ordering) cultural expectations. Both suggest consistent order, making reality reliable and dependable, trustworthy. This meaning is implicit. It is very rare that people who insist on truth and justice and dedicate their lives to their pursuit are conscious of it. The substantial majority of the members of this civilization would feel deprived, uncomfortable to the point of sickness, if their lives lacked truth and justice. In fact, functional mental illness, such as depression, bipolar disorder, and schizophrenia, is almost invariably related to the experience of such a lack. The absence of truth and justice spells disorientation. This cannot be said of any other value.
But the fundamental expectations in the numerous coexisting and succeeding cultures of the monotheistic civilization are different. The concepts of truth and justice are intrinsically empty. And, while the exact meaning of every moral and/or cognitive concept (equality, freedom, individual) is affected by the context, the meanings of truth and justice are bound by nothing but consistency with the ordering expectations, within which bounds they can vary indefinitely.
Expressing the essential logical quality of the monotheistic world, truth and justice are unambivalently good. But they are problematic. Neither of them is simply given. They are ideals to be searched for and sought, pursued. All the actions in this pursuit are justified. The search for truth is always the search for organizing principles of the consistently ordered universe. Truth itself is these principles revealed. Justice is the implementation of these principles, i.e., truth in practice. Truth is God’s law — justice is its application.
The monotheistic ideal of truth is different from the Greek αλήθεια. The Greek word used as a translation of truth literally means “unforgetting,” and the concept is much narrower and much less important. It is the charge of a minor personage in the untrustworthy company of gods. In the unpredictable and uncontrollable world of human experience, resulting from their whims, forgetfulness is not a big deal. Aletheia and truth come near equivalence only in the scientific domain, where both can be interpreted as factual truth, specifically meaning the disclosure of material or natural reality.
Even in the scientific domain, a young, contemporary domain within the monotheistic civilization, existing in the secular society no longer holding God constantly in mind and often altogether forgetting Him, the search for truth within the monotheistic civilization presumes what aletheia in ancient Greek science did not presume: the consistent order in the universe created by God, the discovery of truth being the (however partial) discovery of this order.
Thus, the notion of scientific truth carries on the first principles of the monotheistic worldview and the characteristic mode of thought it calls into being and retains the ideal quality of truth in general.
Outside of science, truth, unlike aletheia, is closely related to meaning in the sense of “meaning of life,” “life’s purpose” (the words “truth” and “meaning” are often used as synonyms). One can say it is the essence of the meaningful creation. Indeed, it is arguable that this is the intended meaning of the Hebrew word for truth, emet (תֶמֱא), symbolized by the letters that compose it: the first, the middle, and the last of the alphabet, and referring to God in everything that exists. (The transition from mythos to logos of the fifth century BCE began the transformation of the meaning of aletheia, approximating it to that of truth in the framework of monotheism, and the integration of Greek culture in the monotheistic tradition through the Septuagint and the spread of Christianity completed it.)
In the original, undiluted and unadulterated monotheism, which entertained no doubts about the essential meaningfulness of the created world, justice was defined clearly. Two Hebrew words are translated as “justice”: tzedek and mishpat. Tzedek, which refers to an abstract notion — a categorical dimension of reality, experience (such as truth, falsehood, good, or evil) — can be perceived, understood, discussed, and analyzed outside of concrete interactions. It is, by far, the more important concept. It is an innate moral sense (i.e., an innate sense of right and wrong in social relations), only possible given the category of the individual as an autonomous agent and tightly related to the idea of individual responsibility, which requires that the apportionment of life’s (but especially social) rewards and punishments be based on and commensurate with one’s actions, merits, and demerits.28
Etymologically, it is related to tzdaka, usually translated as “charity,” helping the helpless, sharing whatever one has, including one’s roof, with widows and orphans, with wanderers coming to one’s door, in other words, active kindness. Exercising tzdaka as an integral part of one’s life, a natural inclination to kindness, even more than tsedek as fairness or recognition of individual responsibility, makes one a tzadik – a righteous, that is, good, person.

In this sense of kindness, justice is the quintessence of the good. Tzedek can also have the meaning of correct or accurate, factual or logical understanding, and, as such, be a characteristic of an argument, which connects the concept to the concept of truth.
Mishpat, which is also translated as “justice,” in distinction, refers to the judgment of specific actions of consequence for others. The word is related to “shofet” — judge, both as a noun, “a judge,” and as a verb, “pronouncing judgment.” This concept is also related to the idea of the individual as an autonomous agent, responsible for one’s actions; the judgment in question is the establishment of individual responsibility, the corresponding justice being understood in the sense of meting out in punishment or reward one’s “just deserts.” Punishments and rewards commensurate with one’s actions, what one deserves, that is, according to tzedek, is justice (mishpat tzodek); exaggerated or denied responsibility is injustice.
The translation of the two quite different concepts, tzedek and mishpat as “justice,” from Latin justitia, not only confounds these concepts (not doing justice to either of them) but projects onto the concept of justice in languages influenced by Latin the meaning the word justitia had in pagan Rome, thus doubly departing from the monotheistic message, intended in the Bible. This mistranslation at the basis of our notion of justice makes it that problematic, ever-pursued ideal whose realization, necessary for the completeness of the world, ever evades those who clamor for it.
In Rome, Justitia was the name of a minor deity borrowed as so many others from the Greek pantheon, and there named Themis. Themis was the goddess of unchanging order, of the way things are, and of divine law, which established, expressed, and was coterminous with these. Her daughter Dike was the goddess of human (laid down) law and order. Together, they patronized propriety, custom, and convention. Besides Dike, Themis was helped by Nemesis, who punished those paying Themis insufficient attention (i.e., behaved improperly or unconventionally). It may not be irrelevant that in addition to giving birth to Horai, of whom Dike was one, Themis was also the mother (and Dike a sister) of Moirai  the Fates, the whole family suggesting arrangements that are destined, cannot be changed, unrelated to human choice and, therefore, responsibility, and altogether outside the sphere of human agency.
Classicists understood that the Greco-Roman goddess Justitia had exceedingly little to do with our civilization’s idea of justice, even though, like other thinkers, they were confused and, as a result, did not stress this: she was called Justitia, after all. The name Themis is derived from the verb tithemi, to put (in place). Moses Finley, in The World of Odysseus, argued that modern Western languages had no equivalents of the noun themis. According to him, Homer used the word in descriptions of concrete events in a concrete time; thus, it is possible to surmise it alluded to the specific traditions and procedures of the 10th and 9th century Greece.
It was, in Finley's words, “untranslatable” in other historical contexts. In the context for which it was coined, it must have designated “custom, tradition, folk ways, mores, whatever we may call it, the enormous power of 'it is (or is not) done.'” Elsewhere Finley wrote: “A gift of the gods and a mark of civilized existence, sometimes it [themis] means right custom, proper procedure, social order, and sometimes merely the will of the gods (as revealed by an omen, for example) with little of the idea of right.”.”29 According to Eric Havelock,30 in the framework of the authentic Greek culture of the “mythos” period, there was no abstract notion of “justice,” “a principle with a priori foundations, either as a ‘rule of law’ or as a moral sense in man….” In Homer, per Havelock, it is absent not only as an abstraction, but even as a topic. The great bard’s use of “themis” and “dike” suggests that “justice is simply the conservation of existing mores, or a correction of a violation. It does not prescribe what in general the mores ‘ought’ to be” and could be addressed only within a concrete narrative with “agents acting or behaving in response to other agents.” Even among pre-Socratic philosophers, “justice does not exceed the logical limits of that propriety and regularity which was the fundamental form of Homeric society.” Only Plato was able “to abstract ‘justice’ from the particular situations in which [other discussants] found it.”31
Plato, of course, was already aided by logic, which made his thought categorical. Conceiving of “justice” as an abstract principle, he distinguished between its application on the collective and individual levels, but neither departed significantly from the meaning themis and dike were given by the concrete interactions described by Homer, though he did not equate them with remedial procedures deployed when rules were broken but only with the rules.
Thus, wrote Havelock, “Plato's justice, as writ large in the city, becomes the symbol of unchanging stability.” On the level of the individual, Plato’s justice was, like Tzedek, a moral sense, but it was the sense of “the same unshakeable, unchanging stability that the polis enjoys” and, presumably, the conduct corresponding to it.32 Again, there was no place for human autonomy or agency. The unchanging stability was the characteristic of the larger order of things; the individual either felt it or not, but had no choice — this “morality” was akin to intelligence.

This is not surprising. A person not suffering from pain does not need analgesics. Neither truth nor justice are important in worlds that are not sensitive to contradictions.
## Psychological Dynamics and Social Change
This leads us directly to the consideration of the psychological dynamics of the monotheistic civilization, its characteristic emotions and dispositions which define the existential experiences of its people, motivate their actions, and are reflected in the general outlines of historical trajectories of societies that constitute it. We begin this consideration with the effects of the logical mode of thought.
The emotional corollary of the privileging of the logic of no contradiction among us, codified as it is in the vocabularies, grammars, and syntaxes of our languages, and forcing us to think categorically, is the intolerance of contradiction.

1899. Also see Hajime Nakamura, Ways of Thinking of Eastern Peoples: India, China, Tibet, Japan, University of Hawaii Press, 1964.
Not all of us, obviously, are rigorous logical thinkers (the majority are hardly thinkers at all), and sensitivity to contradictions differs from person to person. But all of us are sensitive to gross contradictions, particularly those between our ideals and experiences, and especially when they touch upon our personal interests, and this sensitivity is highly unpleasant; we feel irritated, frustrated.
Contradictions offend us, take us aback, trigger within us aggressive, defensive emotions. There are always contradictions between ideals, what we believe should be and expect, on the one hand, and experiences, on the other, that underlie feelings of injustice or the sense of being lied to. Truth and justice (however defined) are expected to be honored; when they are contradicted, the reaction is often violent, as we can see almost daily and all around us at present.
More than a century ago, Durkheim, in his later work, Suicide, postulated that social contradictions constituted the most dangerous problem in modern society. Specifically, he meant contradictions between various collective representations, contradictory norms and values, i.e., a condition of systemic inconsistency, for which he coined the term anomie.41
The condition was, in effect, one of cultural insufficiency — the inability of a culture to provide individuals within it with consistent guidance. As Durkheim demonstrated in Suicide, anomie directly affected individual experience, creating profound psychological discomfort and, among other things, being a major cause of suicide. The concept, however, had a far wider applicability than modern personal problems or modern society, in general. Suggesting that contradictions as such were an irritant with disorienting, disturbing effects (something like a destabilizing cultural drug), capable of making life itself intolerable and requiring drastic resolution, it encompassed the most generally applicable theory of social change.
Among theories of its scope, this theory is the only one to lend itself easily to the probe of empirical evidence, because it points to the psychological mechanisms that connect the cause and effect in any case. Anomie has been successfully applied in microsociology as well as in macrosociology, explaining phenomena ranging from deviance, functional mental illness, to change of norms of personal and family relations, to the emergence of nationalism and revolutions. It would not be an exaggeration to characterize anomie as the most important concept in sociology, if not all social science, with unrivaled explanatory power, the greatest contribution thus far of this complete set of disciplines to human understanding.
41 Durkheim introduced anomie, defined as lack of regulation, already in his first book, Division of Labor in Society, where it specifically applied to modern economic relations. In Suicide, the concept became much broader, and its meaning changed.
But even a superficial comparison proves that contradictions as such, whether between collective representations or between representations and experiences, even if systemic, do not have the effect Durkheim and his followers (the present author included) have attributed to anomie. The obvious inconsistency between collective representations did not equate normative laxity and did not lead to disorientation in Ancient Greece: the guidance the culture provided its members there was firm, effective, and constant, despite not being logically consistent. Custom and propriety, knowledge of what is and what is not done, offered sufficient support for psychological equilibrium: Themis, Dike, and Nemesis, one must conclude, discharged their police duties successfully.
The disturbing psychological effects of cultural inconsistency, which provoke the desire to resolve it and thus lead to socio-cultural change, are predicated not on the fact of contradictions but on the intolerance of contradictions — psychological dynamics systematically cultivated by the monotheistic civilization. It is only within the monotheistic civilization, therefore, that the concept of anomie applies. Where deviations from the norm are not perceived as contradictions, the condition of anomie is not translated onto the psychological level with the same effect, and this most powerful explanatory concept in sociology loses its explanatory power.
Thus, anomie has no explanatory power in the Sinic and Indic civilizations. Among other things, this is proven by the virtual immunity of societies to functional mental illness — depression, bipolar disorder, and schizophrenia. As anomie increases everywhere with the increasing freedom of choice (and number of choices on offer), all over the monotheistic world, the rates of these diseases skyrocket. But epidemiologists
- at the World Health Organization watching over this increase consistently find extremely low rates — a “persistent puzzle” for them — in South-East-Asian countries.42
This specific kind of functional psychopathology is a civilizational phenomenon: it is an expression of the psychological dynamics of the monotheistic civilization, created in the first place by its mode of thinking, privileging logic and cultivating painful sensitivity to contradictions, and reinforced by the conceptual categories (contents of thought) related to its first principles, for instance the concept of individual as an autonomous agent. The central symptom of depression, bipolar disorder, and schizophrenia is a problematic sense of self; while not being impossible, these disorders cannot exist at high rates when autonomous agency is not central to the self.
42 WHO World Mental Health Survey Consortium, R. C. Kessler et al., JAMA 291 (2004):2581-2590. The connection of functional mental illness to anomie – so far, the only explanation of the increasing rates of all its varieties -- is the central dynamic in Mind, Modernity, Madness.
The powerful effects of anomie in the monotheistic civilization, among other things, are due to the activist disposition towards life’s challenges characteristic of it, also created by the intolerance of contradictions (i.e., mode of thought) and the concept of individual. This disposition finds its original expression in the prophetic tradition in the Hebrew Bible. A prophet is an individual who is personally bothered by contradictions between ideals and social reality and volunteers to commit to a mission to make reality consistent with the ideals. The essence of the prophetic role is in the personal dedication to the ideal (to begin with, Truth in the sense of God’s law, later truth in its secular meaning, whatever it is), doing the “right thing” regardless of the cost to the self.
The tradition continues in Christianity with apostles, martyrs, recognized and not, in Islam with Muhammad and his companions, and in the secular (monotheistic) world with those who sacrifice themselves in the service of social, political, and even scientific ideals.
Such self-sacrificial idealism, though characteristic, is rare. Much more commonly, the concept of individual agency leads to activism in orientation and behavior irrespective of any other considerations. The activist disposition then takes the form of attempts to control one’s own life in the sense of promoting one’s self-interest, whether this is defined as securing the salvation of one’s soul and eternal paradise, improving one’s status, or increasing pleasure and decreasing pain in life. What encourages such a disposition in either case is the conviction of one’s agency, stronger in the modern secular age but pervasive relative to the Sinic and Indic civilizations throughout the existence of the monotheistic civilization simply because of the idea of the human individual as a carrier of free will, created in God’s own image.
This contrasts with the quiescent disposition of Chinese and Indian societies, recognized by their members. 43 Whether idealistic or self-interested, the innate activism is both reinforced and modified by the historically added aggressive and intrusive proclivities of the monotheistic civilization.
A particularly important case of the psychological dynamics to which anomie gives rise in the monotheistic civilization is ressentiment — the persistent emotion of existential envy and hatred. The anomie in this case is the inconsistency between the desire for, and expectation of, the identity with a chosen model, on the one hand, and the actual inability to achieve it, on the other. Categorical thinking naturally encourages quantitative comparisons within the same category.

Thus, comparisons in terms of more or less, better or worse, when focused on the self, become invidious, provoking feelings of inferiority and superiority. This leads, on the part of those who develop feelings of inferiority, which, when durable, acquire the form of a psychological complex, to ressentiment. Ressentiment lies behind most of the modern political conflicts within as well as between nations, clearly demonstrated by the comparative-historical evidence on nationalism, but it is not a modern phenomenon. The same psychological dynamics lie behind the pervasive antisemitism central to the borrowed monotheism of Christianity and Islam, which has been a distinguishing mark of our civilization from the moment it transcended the limits of the original Jewish community which gave birth to it.44
It is of utmost significance in this context that there are no native antisemitic traditions in Sinic and Indic civilizations. In general, in a world which is not expected to be consistent, comparisons are likely to be qualitative, emphasizing differences in kind, rather than degree, and the relationally-defined self would not experience these differences in a deeply personal way the individual as an autonomous actor experiences reality — thus existential envy would be a relatively rare emotion, with few expressions on the collective level.
Indeed, there is no word for “envy” in either Chinese or Japanese; it is translated using a permutation of characters for sexual jealousy (嫉妒; 妒忌), with the sexual element easily traceable. The Hindi word serving as the translation for envy also translates jealousy, while Sanskrit Īrṣyā and Ghṛṇā translate as both envy and hatred because these are perceived as the same emotion, characterized in Vedic Scriptures and The Bhagavad Gita as manas rog, a (rare) mental illness.45
The privileging of logic within the monotheistic civilization, the intolerance of contradictions, and therefore the need to see the world as perfectly consistent, both necessitate deep commitments to dominant notions of truth and justice, whether religious or secular, adherence to which is likely to become central to one’s identity. This interpenetration of self-perception and ruling values makes for the internalization of these values as a result of acquiring existential significance; as such, they not only regulate behavior but also directly influence one’s sense of well-being. Personal investment in them is so great that it makes one uncompromising, intolerant of alternative points of view, ever ready to fight these alternative views (and their bearers) to the death — and this despite the dynamic nature of the historical process, in which ruling ideals constantly face contradictions and then change in attempts to escape them.

The change thus comes at a great psychological cost of confusion, disorientation, trauma on the individual level, and conflict, break in solidarity, division on the social level. Compare this to China and India, which do not privilege logic and, therefore, are neither bothered by contradictions nor much concerned with truth and justice. Their equanimity, for quite distinct reasons, in the face of deviations from any particular norm discourages deep ideological commitments and judgment of others’ conduct, instead encouraging acceptance of ideological pluralism and pragmatic, superficial (performative) conformity to norms of any social setting, replaced with changing circumstances without heavy psychological cost.
The characteristic psychological dynamics of the monotheistic civilization are reflected in the general outlines of the cultural process and historical trajectories of its constituent units.
The motor of history in it is a zero-sum struggle between competing worldviews, with a winner-take-all approach in any conflict. This means attempting to wipe every trace of the defeated worldview and superimposing the winning one, palimpsest-like, on the historical page, with layers of winning and ruling ideologies building up vertically, similarly to the build-up of layers in lacquer. Drastic changes are frequent, and most particular social systems are quite short-lived.
The remarkable longevity of social systems in the Sinic and Indic civilizations testifies to the fact that history works differently in them.
To begin to understand how exactly we would have to devote to each of them a discussion of similar length to the present one, following each down the modalities introduced here, from first civilizational principles to modes of thought, central concepts and values, psychological dynamics, and historical trajectories. This fascinating exercise must be left for another occasion.
