Sharyl Cross Distinguished Professor of Political Science and International Affairs St. Edward’s University, Austin, Texas sharylcross@stedwards.edu
Igor Okunev Professorial Research Fellow and Director at the Center for Spatial Analysis in International Relations, Moscow State Institute of International Relations, MGIMO University Abstract
This article sets forth the foundations for recent geopolitical civilizational narratives in Russia that are influencing Moscow’s contemporary foreign policy behavior and the future direction of the Russian “civilizational state” in the global multipolar world order. This article is not intended to provide justification for Russia’s foreign policy choices and actions, but rather the purpose is to explain Moscow’s official geopolitical narratives, and thus to provide a deeper and more comprehensive understanding of the considerations informing Russia’s foreign policy behavior.
Vladimir Putin has been at the helm of leadership in Russia exerting unparalleled influence as President and Prime Minister since 1999. Putin has offered straightforward, often extensive, statements and assessments. These provide a critical source for understanding the development of the material and ideational considerations operative in Russia’s foreign policy priorities and actions.
Primary sources for this article are Vladimir Putin’s speeches and writings, statements of his predecessors, official foreign policy documents and analysis and debate offered by leading experts of Russia’s foreign policy and the International Relations academic community. Writings of leading International Relations experts based in Russia and affiliated with the Valdai Club and annual Valdai international conference closely intertwine with Russia’s policy and defense community. These sources have been particularly helpful in defining factors shaping geopolitical civilizational narratives and potential future regional and global scenarios and vision for the Russian Federation.
The analysis aims to contribute to the literature establishing the ways that national interest considerations  NATO enlargement and other strains in Russia’s bilateral relationships with the West  and a distinctive cultural-historical experience now influence geopolitical and civilizational narratives which might yield insights for both the academic and policy communities.
The article contributes to elucidating challenges that nations motivated by unique civilizational experience present for the existing Western liberal international order.
Keywords: Russian foreign policy; geopolitical civilizational narratives; Putinism; major power conflict; world order
## Introduction: Tectonic Geopolitical Transformation, Russia’s Place as a ‘1,000Year Eurasian Civilization State’
While the last President of the U.S.S.R., Mikhail Gorbachev, has been credited with implementing transformational pro-democracy reforms, and although Russia’s successor leadership might have hoped that the collapse of the former Soviet empire and the former bipolar global power configuration would be followed by Russia and the United States emerging as co-hegemons in maintaining a cooperative world order, these aspirations were not to be realized. The United States, emboldened with triumphalism proclaiming to have “won the Cold War,” enjoyed a brief unipolar moment as an undisputed single hegemonic power in the global system, but this transitional period of unprecedented and unconstrained American global preeminence would be followed by tectonic geopolitical transition as global security, economic and cultural dynamics began a process of shifting away from the West.
International Relations scholars of the Western Realist tradition, George Modelski, Robert Gilpin, and others have offered explanations for the pattern of cyclical rise and decline of major powers in the international system.1 A hegemonic power dissipates resources attempting to maintain control in the global order; this creates vulnerability to challenges from rising powers. Nations ascending relative to the dominant nationstate will be prompted to unseat the hegemon, and the result will be a period of war and instability until a new era of equilibrium and stability can be established and sustained.
Although Russia is typically described by Western International Relations scholars and analysts as a diminished superpower, a great power in decline, or a disgruntled revisionist state, Russia is emerging from the turbulent early post-Soviet era characterized by unique geographic, spatial, material, and ideational features as the largest nation on earth. It is positioned in the heart of Eurasia, drawing on a “one thousand year historical/civilizational” experience anticipating resuming a place of “great power” consequence in the future evolution of the world system.2

International Relations scholars are increasingly questioning the adequacy of basing assumptions, theories, and frameworks for studying global politics and security on the experience of the Westphalian state system established in 1648 and an academic discipline developed principally in the West. Thus, Barry Buzan and Amitav Acharya have written extensively on non-Western traditions of governance and global interaction in the Chinese, Indian and Islamic empires, cultures, and traditions. They note that the “modern discipline of IR was formed during the last two centuries, exactly coinciding with the time when Western civilization became dominant and imposed itself on all others.”3
As International Relations professor at Erasmus University Rotterdam Mohamad Forough observes, “non-European actors such as China, India and Iran have histories of statehood for centuries or before the notion of the “West” or “Westphalia” were even conceived.” 4 These scholars join the chorus of International Relations experts throughout the world contending that the time of Western dominance in International Relations is “coming to an end” and that alternative approaches are sorely needed to adequately address the realities of cultural and historical diversity in contemporary global international politics.
Given an era of unprecedented interdependence among nations, the proliferation of global networks and a growing potential for influence on the part of nations of the nonWest in the contemporary world community, International Relations scholars recognize the importance of developing and deepening theories, approaches, and frameworks appropriate for the vast cultural diversity that composes the global system. Reliance on models that assume “rational” behavior on the part of nation-states that would yield explanations and predictions with universal application is simply not adequate for taking account of the unique histories, cultures, traditions, values, and visions that constitute the wide array of societies composing the global system.
Mohamad Forough observes that since the end of the Cold War global political and economic geographies have been undergoing processes of reordering based on shifts in global power and on the re-emergence of different civilizational influence in global politics (Global South, China, India, Russia and others).5

Forough has observed that actors employ strategic narratives as powerful tools for shaping the emerging orders in their regions and the world. Actors construct and employ geonarratives “to make sense of themselves and their place in their region and, more broadly, in the world.”6
Beginning in the period of the 1990’s following the collapse of the U.S.S.R., Russia’s leadership and society began a complicated process of grappling with re-defining Russia’s identity and place in the world system as successor to the Soviet communist empire. Drawing on Russia’s distinctive “one-thousand-year historical experience” situated at the center of the Eurasian heartland and its relationship with the West as “the other” oscillating from expectations of building partnership and cooperation to cultural and geopolitical clashes, Russia’s leadership and society set forth its role in a dynamic multipolar world order.
Russia, like other non-Western major global powers (China, India) and nations of the Global South, has defined strategic geopolitical civilizational narratives, based on both material and ideational considerations providing “shared meaning of the past, present and future.”7
## Limits of Traditional Realism: Geopolitical Civilizational Narratives and Contemporary World Geopolitics
A central assumption of the Realist paradigm in International Relations theory in the Western tradition suggests that nation-states will behave in accordance with rational calculation; it can be critical for explaining and predicting foreign policy choices. However, there are limitations to assuming that all nations would consider the same policy directions as “rational” regardless of unique historical or cultural experiences, ideas, or values. Scholars have recognized that foreign policy choices can vary and still be deemed “rational” because nations bring diverse perspectives to assessing the viability, feasibility, or wisdom of pursuing different actions.
Many Western policy officials have charged that Russia’s “special military operation” in Ukraine violates international law by crossing the borders of a sovereign nation-state and therefore is a “non-rational” act. Other leaders of the Western policy community, echoing former U.S. Secretary of State Hillary Clinton, Western defense experts and pundits cast Russia’s intervention in Ukraine as an “unprovoked” act of conquest. They compare Vladimir Putin to Adolf Hitler, claiming that the Russian leader has sought to re-constitute the Soviet empire, annex Ukraine, invade Eastern Europe and even Western Europe and more.

## Foundations of Russia’s Geopolitical Civilizational Narratives
Scholars have identified a wide range of factors comprising Russia’s international behavior narratives to include unique physical/material and ideational features such as location, physical size, resources, capabilities, neighboring actors, connection and association to the land and ancestral heritage and family, cultural traits and traditions, historical memory and experience, myth, belief in exceptionalism and mission, religious or spiritual ethos, sense of origin, destiny, dignity, or even soul of a population.

Russia’s features — including size, location, historical experience, and cultural identity — combined with Moscow’s relationship with the West have defined the geopolitical civilizational narratives shaping and legitimizing contemporary foreign policy directions and behavior.
In terms of physical or material characteristics, Russia, the largest country on earth spanning eleven time zones, stands at the center of the Eurasian land mass straddling two continents. Russia’s location as a continental land mass with huge borders has made the country a target for repeated invasion and border incursions, fueling a perpetual sense of heightened vulnerability to threats from outside. The perspective of a multiethnic society with a “border that never ends” because of Russia’s huge geographic space has led to a global-oriented population with an identity formed based on the expectation that the nation would always exert a significant or even decisive influence in international relations.
The origin of Russia’s geopolitical civilizational narrative in relation to the West might be dated to the middle of the 15th century, when Christian post-Roman identity (the boundaries of which included Ancient Rus) was supplemented by spatial characteristics forming the modern borders of “European” civilization.
This is due to the almost simultaneous success of the Reconquista in the Pyrenees and the fall of Constantinople in the Balkans, as a result of which the boundaries of Christendom began to coincide more closely with the geographical boundaries of the continent. At the same time, such a spatial image actualized the need to formalize the eastern border of European civilization as a mental rather than actual geographic construct.
The Horde Yoke in Rus and the attempt to create a national idea of a new Moscow state through positioning itself as the heir of Byzantium became the basis forming this mental demarcation. The dividing border was established between Catholic Western Europe and Eastern Orthodox Russia, creating a buffer gray zone on the territory of the Slavic, Baltic, and Finno-Ugric peoples.
After the downfall of the Byzantine Empire to the Ottomans in the mid-fifteenth century, Moscow assumed its place as the Third Rome or the center of Orthodox Christianity. Although Russia is predominantly an Eastern Orthodox nation claiming to be successor of Byzantium, the fact that the state is a richly diverse multi-faith nation supports the collective sense of a vast and expansive community.
Eastern Orthodox Christianity and multi-religio/ethnic origins would become a critical foundation for the development of the Russian state and post-Soviet identity.
The foundation for Czarist Russia “Orthodoxy, Autocracy and Nationality,” combining a strong state with distinctive civilizational features, would eventually come to be reflected in Russia’s contemporary identity, one following turmoil characterizing the initial period after the dismantling of the U.S.S.R.’s empire.
Contemporary Russian foreign policy behavior must be understood as taking account of the legacy of empire and “great power” status that has been of historic significance for both regional and world geopolitics. Russia’s historical experience has featured direct involvement with geopolitical turmoil accompanying the rise and decline of major powers transforming regional and global politics. Due to Russia’s size and geographic location, the history of Imperial Russia and the Soviet empire featured clashes over spheres of influence with the Chinese, British, Ottoman and AustroHungarian empires. More recently, Russia and the Soviet Union suffered mass casualties defeating invasions deep into Russian territory on the part of Napoleon 1812 and Hitler 1941.
The experience in the “Great Patriotic War,” the Second World War, defeating Nazi Germany and resulting in more than twenty-seven million casualties, continues to be a major defining feature in Russia’s historical identity and ideational narratives in the contemporary period. The Soviet Union emerged from the Second World War pitted against the United States in a bipolar global power configuration 1945-1990 involving a worldwide clash of geopolitical interests, ideology, and values.
Today Russia possesses the largest nuclear arsenal among nations and continues to modernize its nuclear force; it is developing new weaponry and enhanced missile defense and space, cyber and information assets. Moscow has significantly expanded its conventional military budget and provides advanced weaponry to China, India, and other nations. Russia possesses vast resources, especially gas and oil, and currently ranks as the 11th largest economy in terms of GDP in the world. It holds fourth place in GDP-PPP, recently overtaking Japan.
Moscow also has benefitted from inheriting the extensive diplomatic networks established throughout the world during the Soviet era. It holds the position of Permanent Member of the UN Security Council and plays leading roles in major security and economic organizations in Eurasia and throughout the world (Eurasian Economic Union/EEU, Collective Security Treaty Organization/CSTO, Shanghai Cooperation Organization/SCO, BRICS, and more). Russia’s combined military, economic, cyber/technological, and diplomatic assets provide a basis for assuming the role of a major world power. As in the 19th century and during the bipolar U.S.-Soviet era, there should be no question that Russia has emerged from the tumultuous early post-Soviet transition to regain capacity as a major consequential actor in regional and global geopolitics.
It is important to recognize that a key feature in the formation of Russia’s geopolitical civilizational narrative is the fact that unlike the United States and many other nations, Russia never experienced a struggle for independence or developed a post-colonial identity.
The central geographic narrative in Russia, which has a direct impact on domestic and foreign policy, is the debate about whether Russia is part of Europe or the West or a unique and separate space and civilization, Russian World or Eurasia.
Russia’s identity has been defined over time in competition or cooperation with the West. The question has been perpetually posed whether Russian civilization is equal to or superior to the West. Russia’s geopolitical civilizational narrative developed as a response to Moscow’s relationship with the West and is not static but can shift in dynamic patterns based on the ways Russia is viewed in terms of Western attitudes, threat perception and policy actions.
The discourse regarding the clash between Europe and Russia from the 18th century was clearly illustrated in the eternal dispute between Westernizers and Slavophiles; it became a fundamental driving dichotomy in Russian foreign policy carried forward to the contemporary period. Andrei Tsygankov, describing the history of the Russian science of International Relations, defines “Westernizers” and “Third Rome” academic schools, rather than the traditional schools of International Relations that developed in the West including realism, idealism/liberalism, and constructivism.27
Tsygankov and others have attributed Russia’s national ethos and international actions and responses to reflecting fluctuations with respect to the West.28 At one turn, Russia remembers that it is not Europe, and, perhaps, even anti-Europe, producing priorities and values challenging or antagonizing the West. However, since Russian spatial identity is dual, at some point Russia remembers that it is an essential part of Europe and should be together with the continent, and even a leader among nations of Europe. Then Russia pivots back toward Europe, though the turn West is never sustained as Europe resists embracing Russia as a fully integrated part of the European community, society, and culture, thus driving Russia to revert to a new cycle of isolation from the West.
Post-Soviet Russia’s relationship with the United States and its Western allies has been critical in establishing the foundations that would influence Russia’s post-Soviet narratives and role in the global community. Russia emerged from the collapse of empire during the decade of the 1990’s seeking to re-define its identity and place in the global order.

## Vladimir Putin’s Ideas, Putinism and the West
The formation of contemporary geopolitical civilizational narratives centering on Russia’s historical experience, unique culture, multiethnic sense of identity, spiritual tradition, and perception of place as an independent major Eurasian power with a significant role to play in the world community, is reflected in the ideas and worldview of Vladimir Putin.
Putin’s ideas, vision and actions developed in tandem with Moscow’s relationship with the West and Russia’s unique historical-cultural civilizational narratives. The two are employed to define perceptions of reality and interests and objectives vis-à-vis the United States, its Western partners, and the wider world.
As strains mounted in Russia’s relationships with the United States and Europe over NATO expansion, and perceived Western meddling in domestic political processes violating sovereignty in Eurasia, the Middle East and in Russian society, Putin would prioritize further strengthening and consolidating control of the Russian state, securing Russia’s peripheral “near abroad” in Eurasia, challenging the West and reasserting not only a regional but global role. Geopolitical clashes with the West would correspond with a strengthened understanding of Russia’s identity, values, and civilization as unique and separate from the West.
In his December 2012 Address to the Federal Assembly, Vladimir Putin spoke about a revival of “national consciousness” based on history.54 2012 had been declared the Year of Russian History, focusing on preserving historical memory, traditions shared over a thousand year history that Putin said provided the basis to “find inner strength and purpose in national development.”55
Putin stated: “For centuries, Russia developed as a multi-ethnic nation (from the very beginning), a civilization-state bonded by the Russian people, Russian language, and Russian culture native for all of us, uniting us and preventing us from dissolving in this diverse world. As far as the entire world is concerned, we are one people, we are Russians.”56
Putin continued that “Russia is characterized by a tradition of a strong state.”57

## Russia’s Geopolitical Civilizational Narratives, Contemporary and Future Foreign Policy Vision, the Ukraine Crisis and Beyond
Moscow’s leadership and foreign policy community not only describe circumstances in the present period, but also advance geopolitical civilizational narratives suggesting the future direction of Russia’s relationship with Europe and the United States. They position Russia as a formidable Eurasian power with a critical role to play in the development of the dynamic Asia/Pacific region. Projections regarding future scenarios also focus on contributions that Russia can offer to what they envision to be a more just world order fully suited to the historical-cultural-civilizational diversity of the contemporary and emerging world community.
Reflecting on the future of the global system, Vladimir Putin, addressing the Valdai Forum held in Sochi in 2018, stated that “typical of an empire making mistakes…America’s global dominance is coming to an end.” Putin stated: “Thank God, this situation of a unipolar world, of a monopoly, is coming to an end.”101
Putin cited a pattern of U.S. military interventions throughout the world and a series of other mistakes in the security and economic sphere that he attributed to U.S. hegemony.

Each day that the cycle of NATO-Russia kinetic escalation over Ukraine continues, the risks are heightened for miscalculation, misperception or accidents that could lead to unintended or unthinkable clashes involving nuclear powers.
The current trajectory suggests that Russia will increasingly move to engage nations of the non-West to counter Western interests, influence, and legitimacy throughout the world.
Taking account of geopolitical civilizational narratives provides a basis for acquiring a more comprehensive understanding encompassing the interplay between geographic material and ideational narrative factors that can have a direct impact on policy formation, choices, actions, and justifications.
There can be no higher priority than avoiding a confrontation among nations during tumultuous periods of change in the major global power configuration, especially in circumstances accompanied by rapid and extensive technological advances resulting in proliferation of such destructive weaponry throughout the world community.
Failing to accurately explain and predict choices of nation-states that cannot be assumed to uniformly view actions as “rational” can easily lead to misunderstanding and catastrophic consequences. Considering the identity, historical memory, values, vision, and objectives nations have defined as a manifestation of evolving unique experiences can be invaluable for avoiding foreclosing options to foster more constructive relationships and development of the world order.
Defining and analyzing geopolitical civilizational narratives allows for moving beyond traditional Western International Relations approaches to broaden and enhance analysis in ways that recognize the importance of any nation undertaking regional and global reimagining and diversity of civilizational identities and experiences, thus influencing perceptions and actions of nation-states in our global community.
