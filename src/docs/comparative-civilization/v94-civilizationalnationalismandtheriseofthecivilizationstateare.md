Nicholas Morieson Alfred Deakin Institute, Deakin University, Melbourne, Australia Introduction
The previous issue of this journal discussed the phenomenon of civilization-states. As several authors observed, over the past two decades leaders from Beijing to Moscow, from New Delhi to Ankara, have increasingly portrayed the nations they govern as civilization states, or heirs to and continuations of great civilizations (Greenfeld 2025; Cross and Okunev 2025; Alalykin-Izvekov 2025; Satkiewicz 2025). In doing this, they often summon deep time, speaking of thousands of years of cultural continuity, and present their states as guardians of cultural traditions in an age of immense social change.
For example, Sharyl Cross and Igor Okunev (2025) describe how Russia frames itself as a 1,000-year Eurasian civilization state at the heart of a multipolar order. They argue that Western realist models miss the cultural-historical dimension of Russian policy, rooted in memories of invasion, vast and vulnerable borders, and a multiethnic heritage. Most Western scholars and policymakers do not understand, then, that since the ascension of Vladimir Putin as Russian President in 1999, Moscow has cast foreign policy as a civilizational restoration project, defending the Russkiy Mir and prioritizing ties with Eurasia, the Asia-Pacific, and the Global South over relations with the West. The Ukraine war has deepened this view, recasting it as both a security necessity and a civilizational struggle against the liberal West.
Vlad Alalykin-Izvekov (2025) places Russia among states that “view themselves as bearers of civilizational missions,” challenging the Westphalian model with their own civilizational logics. For Russia, this draws on Orthodoxy, Eurasianism, and exceptionalism, providing a framework to resist liberal alien universalism and justify leadership claims in Eurasia. He argues that the Putin regime’s civilizational discourse strengthens its domestic legitimacy, legitimates revisionist policy, and aligns Russia with other non-Western powers, even as it risks essentialising civilizations into static blocs.
Most importantly, in his recent contribution to these pages, Stephen Satkiewicz (2025) rightly notes that this phenomenon is not entirely new. Nationalisms have always drawn upon myths of origin, heroic pasts, and sacred traditions. Yet the “civilizational turn” in contemporary politics is striking in its scope and intensity.
Today, when Putin speaks of the Russkiy Mir, when Xi Jinping promotes a “community of shared future for mankind,” when India’s Bharatiya Janata Party champions the renaming of India to Bharat, or when Erdoğan casts Turkey as heir to the Ottoman and Islamic worlds, these leaders are doing more than evoking history. They are positioning their states as the political expression of entire civilizations.
Satkiewicz poses the timely question: what, if anything, is distinctive about the civilization state? Or to put it another way, what can a civilization-state do that a nation-state cannot? In what follows, I offer a complementary perspective, drawing on my own concept of civilizational nationalism.
## Civilizational Nationalism
I will first introduce my own preferred term for the phenomenon: civilizational nationalism. I use this term to describe not only the rise of ‘civilization states’ such as Russia, China, India, and Turkey, but also to capture something beyond them: the increasing use of civilization as a tool of categorization, especially within nations. For example, right-wing populists across Europe and North America often describe their nations as being part of Western civilization and defined by the Judeo-Christian (or simply Christian) values of the West (Morieson 2022; Yilmaz and Morieson 2025). As a result, they claim that people who do not share Judeo-Christian values cannot be part of Western societies and should therefore not be permitted to migrate, in large numbers, to European nation-states or to the United States.
I have recently argued that ‘civilizational nationalism’ is not a discrete species of nationalism like civic or ethnic variants. Rather, it is better conceived as a framing strategy, or an ideological lens through which nationalist goals are pursued. Whether framing entire nations as civilization-states or merely using the concept of civilization to construct ingroups and outgroups within a nation-state, civilizational nationalism is useful to political actors because it gives regimes a normative alternative to the universal values of liberal modernity. It positions regimes, especially authoritarian and expansionist regimes, as legitimate expressions of a distinct civilizational path. As more states adopt this discourse, we may move toward a multipolar, multicultural, international realm that diverges from the liberal order.
It is not my intention to define terms such as ‘civilization’ or ‘civilization-state’ in a concrete manner. Instead, I prefer to examine how they are used, particularly by authoritarian regimes. Based on my own research and reading existing literature, I posit that civilizational nationalism is a form of political discourse with three main functions.
First, it redefines the concept of the nation. In this framing, the nation is not merely a contemporary political unit, but it is actually the living heir and custodian of a much older, sometimes ancient, civilization.
For example, in the Putin government’s rhetoric, Russia is depicted not simply as a nation-state but as a “state-civilization” (Blackburn 2021) — the culmination of the Russian people’s entire historical trajectory. This narrative draws a continuous line from Kievan Rus, through the Orthodox Church and its traditions, the Tsarist Empire, and the Soviet Union, to the present-day Russian Federation. Similarly, under Xi Jinping, official Chinese discourse casts China as the product of “5,000 years of Chinese civilization,” positioning the modern state as the inheritor of a millennia-long cultural legacy.
Second, civilizational nationalism is used to validate authoritarian governance. Leaders invoke the uniqueness of their civilization to argue that liberal-democratic norms are alien imports unsuited to their societies. In Russia, this appears in the notion of “sovereign democracy,” while in China it takes the form of “socialism with Chinese characteristics.” Comparable arguments can also emerge in Western contexts, where cosmopolitanism may be rejected on the grounds that it undermines “Western civilization” by admitting people from civilizations supposedly holding incompatible values, making assimilation impossible.
Third, it legitimates claims to territory and the shaping of borders. By treating certain lands or populations as integral to a civilization, political leaders justify both external territorial claims and preferential treatment of diasporas as civilizational kin. This framing also underwrites exclusionary immigration policies targeting those identified as outsiders to the dominant civilization. Russia’s justification for its invasion of Ukraine rests heavily on the assertion that Ukraine is inseparable from Russian civilization (Putin 2021; 2023). Similarly, Beijing asserts sovereignty over Taiwan on the basis that it has historically been part of Chinese territory and thus remains an inalienable component of Chinese civilization.
## Civilizational Nationalism in Russia, Turkey, and China
Vladimir Putin portrays Western liberal democracy as alien to Russia’s civilizational path, accusing the West of abandoning Christianity for a morally corrupt, anti-religious society. In contrast, he claims that Russia prioritizes unity, traditional morality, and spiritual values over pluralism and individual rights (Putin 2023; Blackburn 2021; Marten 2014). This framing positions liberal democracy as not only politically undesirable but civilizationally inappropriate for Russia’s multi-ethnic, multi-religious, and very ancient society.
In a 2023 speech, Putin declared that the nation-state had “outlived its usefulness” and that the future belongs to “state-civilizations.” He insisted that “each civilization is different, each is culturally self-sufficient,” rejecting universal liberal norms in favor of civilizational autonomy (Putin 2023).
He described Russia as “an original civilization-state,” a term that “reflects how [Russians] understand not only our own development, but also the main principles of international order” (Putin 2023). This framing legitimizes authoritarian rule, external aggression, and resistance to Western-led norms.
Civilizational nationalism also sharpens boundaries between Russia and its perceived enemies. The liberal West is depicted not merely as a geopolitical rival but as a civilizational adversary conducting a moral and spiritual assault on Russian identity (Teper 2015). NATO enlargement, Western NGOs, and media are framed as attempts to subvert Russia’s civilizational integrity. At home, liberal activists and opposition figures are denounced as agents of civilizational infiltration, justifying their repression.
This discourse also underpins territorial expansion and diasporic intervention. The annexation of Crimea in 2014 and the 2022 invasion of Ukraine were framed as civilizational reclamation projects. Putin repeatedly asserted that Ukraine is part of Russian civilization, united by common Slavic ancestry, Orthodox Christianity, and shared destiny (Putin 2021).
By presenting Ukraine as inseparable from Russian civilization, military action could be framed as defensive — protecting unity against Western manipulation. Beyond Ukraine, Russia claims moral leadership over Orthodox Slavic peoples and asserts a right to protect compatriots abroad. Civilizational nationalism thus legitimizes authoritarianism as culturally authentic, defines enemies through civilizational boundaries, and justifies expansionist policies as acts of restoration.
Under Recep Tayyip Erdoğan, civilizational framing has likewise become central to both domestic and foreign policy. Early in the Justice and Development Party (AKP) era, Turkey’s EU engagement was cast as a “reconciliation of civilizations” (Çınar 2018). After 2013, however, Erdoğan increasingly positioned Turkey as heir to an Ottoman civilization distinct from the secular West (Yilmaz & Morieson 2023).
At home, this narrative portrays Western liberalism as incompatible with Turkey’s Islamic civilizational identity. Democracy and minority rights are framed as Western tools to weaken the nation, while centralization of executive power, media crackdowns, and restrictions on civil society are justified as defending civilizational integrity (Moudouros 2022). Turkey is thus portrayed by Erdoğan and the AKP as both the continuation of Ottoman Civilization, and because of this, the leading or ‘core’ state of the Islamic world.
Civilizational discourse also shapes identity boundaries. Sunni Muslim identity is elevated as Turkey’s cultural core, marginalizing secularists, Alevis, and Kurds, while critics are accused of collaborating with hostile foreign powers (Yilmaz & Morieson 2023).
Erdoğan calls for the revival of Islamic civilization, depicting the Ottoman Empire as a model of justice and compassion contrasted with a corrupt, hostile West (Hazır 2022). Internationally, Turkey is presented as the leader of the global ummah, defending Islamic civilization from Western aggression. Post-Arab Spring interventions are cast as restoring Ottoman leadership and protecting Muslims from secular regimes and Western interference (Yilmaz & Morieson 2023). Turkish military operations in Syria, Libya, and the Eastern Mediterranean are similarly justified as defending Islamic civilization (Yilmaz & Morieson 2024).
The reconversion of Hagia Sophia into a mosque symbolizes the revival of Ottoman-Islamic identity. Domestically, mosque-centered urban planning, Islamic education, and an expanded Directorate of Religious Affairs embed civilizational identity within the state. Erdoğan’s civilizational nationalism consolidates domestic control and asserts Turkey’s leadership in the Muslim world, with the West serving as the civilizational “Other.”
In China, Xi Jinping has made civilizational discourse a cornerstone of political legitimacy, ethnic policy, and foreign strategy. He describes China as possessing “five thousand years of continuous civilization,” casting the Chinese Communist Party (CCP) as guardian of this heritage (Brown & Bērziņa-Čerenkova 2018). Liberal democracy is presented as a Western model unsuited to China’s character. Xi argues the CCP has created “socialism with Chinese characteristics” by merging Marxism with Confucianism, framing centralized rule as a civilizational imperative.
Civilizational rhetoric reinforces Han identity as the cultural core while positioning minorities such as Tibetans, Uyghurs, and Hui Muslims as peripheral groups requiring assimilation. Policies in Xinjiang and Tibet, including re-education camps and restrictions on religion, are justified as safeguarding civilizational unity. Separatism is portrayed as a threat to civilizational continuity, not an exercise in self-determination.
Externally, Xi frames competition with the United States as a civilizational struggle, warning that opposing China’s rise risks a “clash of civilizations” (The State Council Information Office 2021).
AI-driven censorship and surveillance are presented as necessary to shield Chinese civilization from foreign ideological influence. China promotes itself as a development model for the Global South, arguing that modernization need not mean Westernization (Yilmaz & Morieson 2024; 2025). Institutions like the AIIB and BRICS are offered as alternatives to Western-led governance, encouraging other nations to pursue their own culturally grounded modernities. Xi’s narrative of rejuvenation appeals to postcolonial states by framing China’s rise as the reversal of colonial humiliation.
The CCP’s diaspora policy further illustrates this worldview. Nathan and Zhang (2021) argue that Xi’s government constructs a transnational, blood-based identity that demands loyalty and aligns diaspora identity with regime objectives. Overseas Chinese are praised for advancing unification, defending Chinese interests, and contributing to “the Chinese ethnic group … and every son and daughter of Chinese descent” (Nathan & Zhang 2021).
This rhetoric merges regime survival with the preservation of Chinese civilizational identity. Compared to Russia and Turkey, China’s civilizational nationalism places greater emphasis on cultural continuity, presenting the CCP as inheritor and protector of an unbroken tradition.
This comparative view shows that in Russia, Turkey, and China, civilizational nationalism functions as more than symbolic rhetoric. It legitimizes authoritarian governance as culturally authentic, frames internal and external opponents as civilizational threats, and justifies territorial expansion or influence over diasporas as acts of civilizational renewal.
By grounding political authority in claims of civilizational uniqueness, leaders in these states reject liberal norms as alien impositions, recast domestic repression as cultural preservation, and present their international ambitions as the defence — or restoration — of a civilizational order.
## Why proclaim your nation a ‘civilization state’?
Undoubtedly, the most significant manifestation of the growing trend of civilizational nationalism takes the form of regimes proclaiming their nations to be ‘civilization states.’ Although the term ‘civilization’ is used, what we are seeing is not a return to the empires of old, but rather an accommodating of the civilization idea into a nationalist framework in which the nation-state is the primary actor in global affairs. It is worth considering, then, what specific advantages such a status might confer upon a nation and the regime that governs it.
First, the civilization state can mobilize people well beyond its nominal boundaries. It can draw the support not only of national diaspora communities but also of those who believe they belong to the civilization represented by the state. In this way, the civilization state positions itself as the guardian of a tradition older and broader than the state itself. Consequently, it can call upon scattered diasporas to join its cause or assert a special responsibility for communities living beyond its borders because they are “of the civilization,” whether defined by shared religion, language, or a combination of characteristics.
Second, the civilization state can project moral authority outward. Where a nation-state typically argues from the perspective of national interest, the civilization state appeals to a civilizational right to act in certain ways in the international sphere. This framing allows it to reject universal norms more easily, portraying them as expressions of the cultural imperialism of a rival civilization, most often Western civilization. Moreover, the civilization state can claim parity not just with other great powers but with other “civilization cores,” as Samuel Huntington might have put it, or with other recognized civilization states.
Third, the civilization state can engage in a kind of diplomacy that makes the nationstate appear narrow and transactional by comparison. Civilizational diplomacy draws on shared heritage, religious kinship, and epic history. It can organize summits of “civilizational leaders,” fund cultural revival projects, and send out emissaries of art, religion, and philosophy. These gestures may seem ornamental, but they help create a sense that foreign policy is not merely bargaining but the weaving together of a broader civilizational tapestry. President Erdoğan’s creation of the Museum of Islamic Civilizations is one such example.
Fourth, at home, the civilization state can frame its political project as a renewal not merely of the nation but of an entire civilizational tradition. Xi Jinping’s “great rejuvenation of the Chinese nation” integrates economic growth, moral discipline, and ideological conformity into a story stretching back five millennia. Similarly, President Erdoğan portrays himself not only as the leader of Türkiye but also as the heir to the Ottoman-Islamic order, holding up the Ottoman era as a model for contemporary governance. In India, Prime Minister Narendra Modi often invokes the Ramayana, a text set thousands of years ago, to link India’s modern identity to a grand Hindu civilizational narrative. For Modi, the idea of India as a mere 77-year-old nation-state is far less compelling than an image of India as the inheritor of an ancient and sacred tradition.
In China’s case, while the modern state has existed in its present form only since 1949, it claims an unbroken civilizational lineage stretching back 5,000 years. By looking to dynastic periods when China was powerful, such as the Qing Empire in the 18th century, the CCP encourages ethnic Chinese both at home and abroad to view themselves as part of a great civilizational continuum.
Finally, the civilization state can resist assimilation into any single global normative order. Whereas the nation-state may object to specific treaties or policies while still “playing the game” of the liberal international system, the civilization state insists that there are other games entirely, with different rules and different leagues to which it belongs. This stance grants it a distinctive role in a multipolar world: the champion not merely of a country’s sovereignty but of a civilization’s right to be different. This may be the most important reason for proclaiming one’s nation to be a civilization state.
Indeed, the more states that begin calling themselves civilization-states or heirs to ancient civilizations, the more we should expect resistance to liberal norms to grow.
Civilizational framing now extends into the digital realm. China, for instance, seeks to hard-code its civilizational values into AI systems while restricting U.S. platforms; Russia embeds Orthodox-traditionalist narratives into its digital governance. In liberal democracies, AI systems like ChatGPT, Claude, Gemini, and Grok inevitably reflect their creators’ cultural priorities, while the EU pushes “AI sovereignty” to enshrine its own normative commitments.
If Satkiewicz asks what a civilization-state can do that a nation-state cannot, one answer may be: it can claim not just territory, heritage, and moral authority, but also its own digital sphere, a space in which civilizational identity is enforced through code, algorithms, and information governance.
## Conclusion
In Russia, Turkey, India, and China, civilizational nationalism appears to serve several overlapping functions. It legitimizes authoritarian rule by portraying liberal democracy as a culturally alien system unsuited to the nation’s historical character. It defines political opponents, both domestic and foreign, as civilizational adversaries, enabling the suppression of dissent and the exclusion of perceived outsiders. It justifies territorial expansion or influence over diasporas as acts of civilizational restoration. It also creates the conditions for a broader, more value-driven diplomacy grounded in shared heritage rather than narrow transactional bargaining.
By proclaiming themselves civilization states, regimes gain new tools for mobilizing diasporas, resisting liberal norms, and asserting parity with other civilizational powers. They can wrap their policy agendas in the legitimacy of historical continuity, cultural guardianship, and moral authority. Such a stance also allows them to resist integration into a single global normative order, promoting instead a multipolar world in which multiple civilizational logics coexist and compete.
This resistance may easily spread into the digital realm, where the civilization state may demand its own territory; we see China doing this already. This imperative is already influencing the AI systems created by China, and the closing off of China to American AI systems.
Indeed, the governance of generative AI globally is increasingly a contest over values, with systems like ChatGPT, Claude, Gemini, DeepSeek, and Grok embedding political and cultural priorities that shape speech and thought.
In the United States, AI reflects liberal traditions of rights and free expression, framed as part of a geopolitical struggle, especially against China, while the EU seeks “AI sovereignty” to encode its own principles of dignity, privacy, and Human Rights.
China and Russia reject universal liberal norms, embedding “socialist core values” or Orthodox-traditionalist narratives into AI, tightly controlling content to defend their civilizational identities and justify censorship. Many non-Western states resist U.S. value dominance, but in authoritarian contexts this framing entrenches surveillance and state-aligned AI. The result is a fragmented digital order, with civilizational boundaries hard-coded into AI systems.
Civilizational nationalism is a discourse that shapes domestic governance, international strategy, and potentially the regulation of information in the digital age. It provides leaders with a powerful narrative to resist liberal norms and assert parity with other civilizational powers. But it also carries risks: exclusion, inflexibility, and suppression of dissent.
Understanding civilizational nationalism is essential, both for interpreting current geopolitical shifts and for anticipating the contours of an emerging multipolar order. Thus, if more states adopt civilizational frames, as opposed to the prevailing postWestphalian nation state frames, then the likelihood of a world structured around competing civilizational orders, in both the real and the digital realms, is sure to grow.
