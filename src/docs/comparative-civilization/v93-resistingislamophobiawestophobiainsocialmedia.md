Peter O’Brien Trinity University Jordan Nelson University of Chicago pobrien@trinity.edu Abstract
Both the practice and study of Islamophobia repose on the concept of comparative civilizations. Purveyors of Islamophobia use what Roger Westcott calls a “denotative” understanding to posit an objective conflict (or “clash” à la Samual Huntington) between Western and Islamic civilizations. Scholars who analyze Islamophobia tend to take what Westcott calls a “connotative” approach that views civilizations as subjective, social constructs (à la Edward Said’s “Orient”).
Despite many advantages, the latter approach tends to dismiss the agency of Muslims to resist Islamophobia. This article counters that trend by exploring Islamist resistance to Islamophobia, to which we give the shorthand label, “Westophobia.” To theorize resistance, we draw on the theory of central and peripheral domains articulated by Wael Hallaq in Restating Orientalism. To operationalize Hallaq’s theory for empirical analysis, we advance the hypothesis of reverse othering, whereby Westophobes reverse or invert negative Islamophobic stereotypes of Muslims. We document reverse othering through content analysis of selected social media posts, detailed in the section on methodology.
While generating significant opposition to Islamophobia, Westophobic discourse remains locked in the binary, hyperbolic, essentializing presuppositions and strategies of Islamophobic discourse, according to which all Muslims differ from all non-Muslims in existentially threatening ways. Through a series of in-person interviews conducted in 2024, we find more meaningful resistance among people with extensive intercultural experience.
Keywords: Clash of Civilizations, Islamophobia, Orientalism, Islamism
## Introduction
Islamophobia figures prominently in politics. It is, for example, a mainstay of far-right politicking and has helped to enhance the political fortunes of many far-right parties and personalities (Donald Trump, Georgia Meloni, Victor Orbán, and Geert Wilders, to name but the most successful).1 In particular, the field of Islamophobia Studies has demonstrated the reach and sway of anti-Muslim images and practices in international relations, domestic politics, and society at large.2
Both the practice and study of Islamophobia rely significantly on the concept of civilization, even if this concept is not explicitly acknowledged. Roger Westcott’s distinction (in this journal)3 between “denotative” and “connotative” meanings of civilization is instructive here.
For Westcott, the denotative understanding conceives civilization to be an objective reality— identifiable ideas, institutions, and peoples that really exist. By contrast, connotative comprehension views civilization as subjective, socially constructed perceptions of supposedly interconnected ideas, institutions, and peoples that need not have any direct correspondence with objective reality but still have an emotive or performative effect on believers.
Following Samuel Huntington’s notion of the “clash of civilizations,” purveyors of Islamophobia allege that Islam and Muslims — and Islamic civilization in general manifest objectively demonstrable characteristics that, because they threaten the real values and interests of Western civilization, should be feared and countered.

By contrast, scholars of Islamophobia, working in the shadow of Edward Said’s Orientalism, contend that the “Orient” represents a social construct created and maintained over centuries by Western Orientalists to depict and define Islam and Muslims in such a way as to legitimize dominating them. However, the latter camp of postcolonial constructivists tends to dismiss the agency of the dominated. We learn a great deal about how non-Muslim Westerners view (and treat) Muslims, but little to nothing about how Muslims receive and respond to Western ideas and actions aimed at them.4
This article begins to fill that void in the literature by exploring resistance to Islamophobia to which we give the shorthand label, “Westophobia.” We first introduce the theory of reactive radicalization but find it lacking because it treats Islamophobic and Westophobic discourses essentially as equals in terms of power and sway. For reasons outlined below, we prefer the theory of “central and peripheral domains” explicated by Wael Hallaq in Restating Orientalism.5
To operationalize Hallaq’s theory for empirical analysis, we devise the hypothesis of reverse othering. Many Muslims keen to resist Islamophobia simply reverse (or invert) its pejorative stereotypes, for example, by countering the allegation that Islam and Muslims are anti-democratic with the assertion that Western polities are themselves anti-democratic because they deny equal rights to Muslims. We conducted quantitative and qualitative content analysis of the postings (during 2022) of social media sites deemed Islamophobic (for example, Robert Spencer’s Jihad Watch) or Westophobic (for instance, Hizb ut-Tahrir) according to criteria laid out below.
We find that Westophobic sites reverse othering amounts to what Hallaq terms “dissident” resistance that opposes Islamophobia but fails to “question…the epistemological structures” of Islamophobic discourse. Rather, in essentially playing by the same rules of the central domain, Westophobes in the peripheral domain end up “reinforcing and strengthening” the very Islamophobic discourse that they wish to resist. However, through a small number of in-person interviews carried out in 2024 among students with extensive intercultural experience, we find signs of Hallaq’s “subversive” resistance that does question “the foundational assumptions and epistemological mainstays of the discursive formations…with a view to changing the underpinnings of the system, creating another one, altogether different.”6 The latter points in the direction of a new understanding of civilization that transcends the need/tendency to rely on the destructive binary of us vs. them.

## Islamophobia and Westophobia
The terms “Islamophobia” and “Westophobia” are employed throughout this essay as admittedly imperfect yet expedient shorthand labels. A brief explication of the choice is, therefore, in order at the outset. “Islamophobia” is a contested concept. Some investigators, like Halliday, reject it altogether because it purportedly excludes all criticisms of Islam as irrationally phobic. He also objects to the common claim that Islamophobia is a form of racism because neither Islam nor Muslims constitutes a “race.”7 Others who wish to retain the concept retort that the widely accepted theory of racialization solves this problem in that it views race not as an objective reality but as a social construct that is fluidly affixed to various groups.8 Islamophobia represents a form of “cultural racism.”9 By this reading, then,
Islamophobia is a contrived fear or prejudice fomented by the existing Eurocentric and Orientalist global power structure. It is directed at a perceived or real Muslim threat through the maintenance and extension of existing disparities in economic, political, social, and cultural relations…Islamophobia reintroduces and reaffirms a global racial structure through which resource distribution disparities are maintained and extended.10
As implied, Islamophobia represents more than a set of ideas (or merely a “discourse”): rather, it is ideas embedded in structures of power that enable those ideas to inflict reallife discriminatory consequences on persons perceived to be Muslim — precisely Said’s point in linking Orientalism to (neo)colonial practices. Similarly, The Islamophobic Studies Yearbook maintains that “Islamophobia is a form of institutionalized racism and has become more and more obvious despite European and North American factitious illusion of a post-racial order.”11 In other words, Islamophobia is a form of “structural racism.”12 For example, Massoumi, Mills, and Miller highlight the “Islamophobic state” fixated on counterterrorism.13
Because these structures are rooted in a fear of Islam as a threat to Western societies, the use of the term “Islamophobia” is appropriate.

Reiterating Westcott’s idea of the denotative understanding, purveyors of Islamophobia believe that Islamic civilization generates real ideas and actions of which Western societies must be wary.
In The Clash of Civilizations and the Remaking of World Order, Huntington, for example, discerns “the recommitment to Islam as the guide to life in the modern world" that amounts to a “rejection of Western culture.”14 Bernard Lewis is even more emphatic: “What we confront now is…a rejection and condemnation, at once angry and contemptuous, of all that America is seen to represent in the modern world.” 15 Islamophobes may differ on the exact nature of the threat, but all agree that Islam and Muslims are something to fear.
The appellation, “Westophobia,” is equally appropriate. As will become clear in the analysis below of Islamist groups like Hizb ut-Tahrir, resistance to Islamophobia often reposes on a fear of allegedly objective Western ideas, institutions, and people. This fear is perhaps best captured in the idea of “Westoxification,” commonly employed by Islamist critics of the West. Originally coined by Jalal al-e-Ahmad but popularized by the apologist of the Iranian revolution, Ali Shariati, the term operates as a warning to Muslims not to succumb to the perilous temptations of modern Western civilization (for example, moral laxity, secularism, materialism), lest they contaminate all that is right and good with Islam.16
The notion of fear also stands out in the analysis of the connotative dimension. It becomes known especially in the oft-applied concept in discourse analysis of the negative Other that is portrayed as radically different from the (Western) Self in ways thought to imperil that Self. According to Said, Orientalism identifies “‘us’ Europeans as against all ‘those’ non-Europeans…The ‘East’ has always signified danger and threat…in the history of popular anti-Arab and anti-Islamic prejudice in the West, which is immediately reflected in the history of Orientalism.”17 Similarly, for other scholars, “Islamophobia is the modern progeny of Orientalism…a worldview that casts Islam as the civilizational antithesis of the West and that is built upon the core stereotypes and baseline distortions of Islam and Muslims.”18

As intimated in the Introduction, discourse analysis of Islamophobia tends to underestimate or even fully dismiss the agency of Muslims to resist Islamophobic discourse. What critics call “overdetermination” — exaggerating the power of the discourse to define and thereby dominate its subjects — represents a flaw common to most structural discourse analysis of the kind inspired by Michel Foucault. After all, Foucault did assert that “In any given culture and at any given moment, there is always only one episteme that defines the conditions of possibility for all knowledge.”19 Similarly, Said, who openly acknowledged his debt to Foucault, averred that:
So authoritative a position did Orientalism have that I believe no one writing, thinking, or acting on the Orient could do so without taking account of the limitations on thought and action imposed by Orientalism. In brief, because of Orientalism, the Orient was not (and is not) a free subject of thought or action.20
The subjugated are presumed to be powerless in countering the dominant discourse. For example, Gayatri Spivak, when discussing theories of post-colonial discourse more broadly, noted that “the agency of the postcolonial is…obliterated in order to inscribe him and her as marginal.”21 Priyamvada Gopal has cleverly asserted that Postcolonial Studies
Has [sic] emphasized the post- over the anti-colonial…the focus of colonial discourse analysis…has largely been on the imperial centre—ironically, to the detriment of a consideration of those who were subject to these regimes, but not necessarily (indeed, hardly ever) silently so.22
While generally applauding postcolonial discourse analysis, friendly critics voice a need to incorporate resistance to the dominant paradigm. Thus, Stuart Hall insists that “the ideological sign is always multi-accentual…it can be discursively rearticulated to construct new meanings, connect with different social practices, and position social subjects differently.”23 Likewise, Homi Bhabha points to the “subversive strategy of subaltern agency that negotiates its own authority through a process of iterative ‘unpicking’ and incommensurable, insurgent relinking.”24

## Reactive Radicalization
One body of work that has specifically tried to analyze such resistance is reactive radicalization. The theory of “reactive radicalization”26 — also called “reciprocal radicalization,” 27 “co-radicalization,” 28 “mutual radicalization” 29 or “cumulative radicalization”30 — posits that radical Islamists and radical far-right groups copy one another’s propaganda tactics to attract more members and to enhance ingroup solidarity. ## Islamophobia as Central Domain
Hallaq’s concept of a “central domain” addresses and solves the problem of treating Islamophobia and Westophobia as symmetrical discourses. A central domain or dominant paradigm — Hallaq uses the terms interchangeably — has the power to construct reality and subjects. It does so via language transmitted through dominant structures of power. This fusion of linguistic and material power sets the terms (rules) of the opposition and resistance that the domain itself generates.

A central domain, by the very exercise of its power, inevitably generates opposition, or what Hallaq calls “peripheral domains,” where there “always exist subversive discourses and resisting strategies that constantly challenge the paradigmatic discourses, that is, the discourses that constitute, reflect, and advocate the central domain and its values.”57 However, peripheral domains typically do not succeed in replacing or overcoming the central domain because they are compelled to play by its rules:

## Reverse Othering
To operationalize Hallaq’s theory for empirical analysis, we devise the notion of reverse othering. We hypothesize that Westophobic Muslims keen to resist Islamophobia reverse its negative images of Muslims to project an adverse portrayal of non-Muslim Westerners.
We stress four examples of reverse othering. Westophobes reverse the Islamophobic claim that Islam is antiquated and pre-modern (I1) by contending that modern Western culture is materialistic and decadent (W1). Westophobes upend the claim that Islam is incompatible with democracy (I2) by contending that Western democracy is hypocritical when it comes to equal rights for Muslims (W2). Westophobes invert the charge that Islam is misogynistic (I3) by arguing that Western societies sexualize and thereby oppress women (W3). Westophobes reverse the assertion that Islam is inherently expansionist (I4) by insisting that the West continues to harbor (neo)imperialist aims and attitudes toward the Islamic(ate) world (W4).
In this way, reverse othering does produce opposition to Islamophobia, but the opponents play by the same rules of the central domain. These rules include primarily the sensationalistic, distorting use of binary, essentializing stereotypes according to which all (devout) Muslims differ from all non-Muslims in existentially threatening ways. This much the literature on reactive radicalization gets right, but it means that the presuppositions and tactics of the central domain (Islamophobia) “transform the structure, meaning, and even appearance of peripheral domains” (Westophobia). By simply reversing (rather than dismantling) Islamophobic stereotypes, reverse othering winds up reinforcing Westophobic and Islamophobic stereotypes alike (not to mention the presumption of clashing civilizations).
Furthermore, the four Islamophobic tropes are deeply embedded in and powerfully reinforced by material policy structures. The claim that Islam and Muslims are premodern takes many forms, but none so common and consequential as the allegation that neither the creed nor its followers respect secularism. ## Findings
Islamophobic and Westophobic tropes published in over 1800 posts during 2022 are categorized in Figures 1 and 2. Quantitative analysis revealed that out of 1,660 individual posts published on the five Islamophobic websites, 307 (18.49 percent) contained the hypothesized Islamophobic tropes. We coded 596 postings that correlated with one or more of the four hypothesized tropes: 32 percent fell into the category of pre-modern (I1), 8.1 percent into anti-democratic (I2), 19.5 percent into misogynistic (I3), and 40.4 percent into expansionist (I4). We found among 143 posts published on the five Westophobic websites a total of 96 (67.13 percent) containing the hypothesized Westophobic tropes. Of these, 18.8 percent correlated with the category of decadent (W1), 36.3 percent with hypocritical democracy (W2), 3.8 percent with objectifying women (W3), and 41.3 percent with (neo)imperialism (W4). Interestingly, 14.7 percent of all postings were images rather than words.
These numbers warrant several observations. First, the 14.7 percent images among the postings (presumably not captured by the keyword search) validates our methodological decision to supplement the quantitative with qualitative analysis. Second, although Islamophobic sites devoted only 18.49 percent of postings to Islamophobic themes, they nonetheless more than tripled the number of Westophobic postings (596 to 160 respectively).

## Dissenting Versus Subversive Resistance
The Westophobes represent what Hallaq labels “dissenting” authors and differentiates from “docile” and “subversive” authors. Docile authors “stand conscripted in the system of power, mostly not knowing that they are being conscripts.”93 These are the Islamophobes in this study. They “never transgress the boundaries of the commonly accepted assumptions of the ‘discursive’ tradition’.”94 Dissenting authors vigorously challenge the assertions of the tradition (in our case by reverse othering), but nevertheless fail to “question the mainstays of the system or the epistemological structures or forms of knowledge that define the worldview through the prism in which problems in the field are solved.”95
Those mainstays in this study are the act of reductive, essentializing (reverse) othering itself as well as the sensationalist tactics of distributing the “knowledge” through social media. “All these foundations and epistemic edifices are left in place, accepted and intact…It amounts, in other words, to a correction within the system or to the system, but the system nonetheless.”96 Indeed, Hallaq warns that dissenting opposition can even benefit the system by “rendering it stronger and more robust.”97 Westophobic reverse othering arguably does precisely that by adopting similar modes and strategies to those developed to demonize and dominate Muslims in the first place (even when they are directed at non-Muslims). Their followers “by virtue of their awareness of the need to critique the system, end up reinforcing and strengthening the system.”98
By contrast, subversive authors reject and (seek to) replace the central domain itself. They “interrogate the foundational assumptions and epistemological mainstays of the discursive formations as well as the larger system of power that sustains them…with a view to changing the underpinnings of the system, creating another one, altogether different.”99

In search of subversive resistance, we conducted in-person interviews. In September 2024, we conducted semi-structured, small-group interviews with twentyfive participants in the “Fulbright Diversity Initiative” (FDI). Sponsored by the German-American Fulbright Commission, FDI annually (from 2015 to 2024) selected twenty German university students with “migration background” (meaning at least one parent born outside Germany) and sent them to a university in the USA for four weeks (to motivate the grantees to apply for the conventional year-long grants to the USA after their return). We elected to employ a combination of symbolic interactionism and classic grounded theory approach rather than a purely constructivist grounded theory approach.103 We simply asked the interviewees to react to provided examples (words and images) of each of the eight Islamophobic (I1-4) and Westophobic (W1-4) tropes. ## Conclusion
Those whom we label Islamophobes or Westophobes do more on social media than engage in othering and reverse othering. Yet, we found sufficient evidence of each to confidently conclude that they represent an important aspect of Islamophobia and resistance to it. The overwhelming numeric advantage of Islamophobic discourse justifies designating it with Hallaq’s label of a central domain.
A study of wider and deeper scope than ours would be necessary to explore the myriad ways in which the dominance of Islamophobic discourse is reinforced, transmitted, and perpetuated by material structures of power and policies (but we did allude above to some studies that do so).110 Resistance to Islamophobia is hardly non-existent, as putatively implied by discourse analysts working in the tradition of Said’s Orientalism. Resistance is clearly discernible and vehement, as theorized in Hallaq’s notion of the peripheral domain.
However, our analysis demonstrated that Westophobes devote a large majority of their social media activity to directly countering the common stereotypes of Muslims produced by Islamophobic discourse. By doing so, they wind up inadvertently reinforcing the presuppositions and strategies (rules of the game) originally established by Islamophobes (othering).
In addition to this merely “dissenting” resistance (to use Hallaq’s terminology), we did find evidence of “subversive” resistance in our person-to-person interviews. Obviously, more such interviews are in order going forward. The same is true of analyses of organizations and social media sites (Muslim or non-Muslim) that directly or indirectly eschew (reverse) othering and offer an alternative to the binary of Islamophobia versus Westophobia.111 It does appear, however, that challenging and overcoming both are best pursued through direct and sustained personal contact among persons from differing cultural backgrounds.

Let us close with some thought-provoking questions framed to relate to Westcott’s distinction between the denotative and connotative comprehensions of civilization. Does the denotative reading itself represent an epistemic presupposition of the central domain, especially the claim (à la Huntington) that civilizations do (must) exist in contrast to and even in conflict with one another? Josephine Quinn, in How the World Made the West: A 4,000 Year History, writes: “Civilizations are such a familiar way to see the world today that they can seem like natural facts, a universal model for the organization of human society. They are in fact a recent European invention, part of a phenomenon that I call ‘civilizational thinking’.”112
Furthermore, is a perceptive appreciation (and analysis) of the connotative dimension of civilizations at least one prerequisite for a genuinely subversive resistance to the central domain? Would or should subversive resistance lead to the abandonment of the concept of civilization altogether or perhaps, alternatively, point in the direction of theorizing and realizing a global civilization that does not rely on the binary of us versus them?113 Our young interviewees seem already to be approaching the world in this more inclusive manner. As Huma remarked, “I don’t get us versus them because them is us.”
