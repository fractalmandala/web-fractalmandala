Reviewed by Stephen T. Satkiewicz
Pay any attention to current events and it seems that war and conflict are everywhere: from the Russian invasion of Ukraine in 2022, to the eruption of conflict in the Middle East in October 2023, to on-going geopolitical tensions in the Pacific over the status of Taiwan. It was only a decade or so ago that many were confidently predicting not only the decline but the outright end of war, as Steven Pinker boldly argued in his The Better Angels of Our Nature: Why Violence Has Declined (Viking Books, 2011).
Why is war still with us, after proclamations of its decline and end? To answer these and other related questions we have Christopher Coker’s book Why War? It seeks to answer the question of why war has been such a ubiquitous phenomenon within human history.
The late Christopher Coker (1953-2023) certainly was a scholar worthy of such an ambitious task. He was Director of the London School of Economics’ foreign policy think tank LSE Ideas. He was also Professor of International Relations at the London School of Economics, retiring in 2019.
In addition, Coker was a former twice-serving member of the Council of the Royal United Services Institute, a former NATO Fellow, and a regular lecturer at defense colleges in the United Kingdom, United States, Rome, Singapore, Tokyo, Norway, and Sweden. During the last year of his life, he was also a member of the International Society for the Comparative Study of Civilizations.
Why War? was Coker’s last book, and thus it represents a fitting finale to a long and distinguished academic career. It is also part of a growing body of scholarship addressing this question about war’s place within human history. Other prominent examples include Anzar Gat’s War in Human Civilization (Oxford University Press, 2008), Ian Morris’s War! What Is It Good For?: Conflict and the Progress of Civilization from Primates to Robots (Farrar, Straus and Giroux, 2014), Margaret MacMillan’s War: How Conflict Shaped Us (Random House, 2020), Siniša Malešević’s Why Humans Fight: The Social Dynamics of Close-Range Violence (Cambridge University Press, 2022), and most recently Richard Overy’s similarly titled Why War? (W. W. Norton & Company, 2024).
Within the pages of the book’s introduction, Christopher Coker provides the reader with his own backstory of how he got interested in this issue, including his training as a Junior Cadet (British equivalent to JROTC) in his late childhood.
This helps give the reader a more personal understanding of the author’s perspective and understanding of the subject.
Christopher Coker does give a comprehensive overview of this question, addressing the three main elements of the question: biological, social, and cultural. To help serve this purpose, Coker utilizes the model developed by biologist and ethologist Niko Tinbergen (1907-1988) in formulating four main elements to the study of war as a human behavior:
- • Biology
- • Culture
- • Ontogeny
- • Functions (pp.18-19)
The bibliography of sources the author consulted for such a wide scope is impressive and useful. The author does an excellent job maintaining a proper balance between biology, social, and cultural factors rather than trying to reduce it to merely one of them. Anzar Gat, for example, tends to do this with the biological component in many of his works. References are made to the Bhagavad Gita, the Bible, epic poetry, war films, and now video games, to demonstrate the ubiquitous nature of war in human cultures, but also to speak to a certain need for heroic virtue and other attributes.
Into the rich array of scholarship that Coker utilized for his project is injected the growing field of the complexity sciences. In this regard Coker provides the reader with a most concise summary of how complex systems analysis can be applied to human civilizations:
Human societies are complex adaptive systems. A society is made up of different ‘parts’ that interact with each other and produce complex and unpredictable behaviour. Growing complexity is the story of history. Which is not to say that it is not full of fits and starts and occasional reverses, but the direction is clear enough to most historians.
You can, if you wish, apply a concept of development without smuggling in normative assumptions, if by development we mean, not Progress, whatever that may be, but increasing complexity. Indeed, it is far more credible to claim that history is not progressive but directional, and what makes it so is our capacity to learn, to adapt to our environments and to learn increasingly quickly as the world becomes ever more complex. (pg. 125)
Into this complex mix, war is a major causal mechanism for these developments in greater social complexity, but it is also a reflection of that ever-growing complexity. Coker further argues that although war’s nature is unchanging, its character does change with this growing complexity.
This is a direct influence of the famous military theorist Carl von Clausewitz (17801831), which makes sense since Coker wrote his own analysis Rebooting Clausewitz: 'On War' in the Twenty-First Century (Oxford University Press, 2017).
Coker concludes the book with a chapter on the potential dangers of Artificial Intelligence (A.I.) and advanced autonomous machinery regarding the future of war. Coker was not afraid of Terminator-like advanced machines overpowering humans but rather that “[t]he real danger, in fact, may lie in out-sourcing more and more key decisions to machines that are not nearly as smart as we are in navigating through life.” (Pg. 212)
With all the current sensationalism concerning A.I., this may prove to be the more critical issue, since Coker also argues that humans are governed by age-old moral and ethical concerns and teachings that may not impact A.I. at the same level. This is not entirely a new issue, for even U.S. World War II General Omar Bradley (1893–1981) famously lamented how the modern world was governed by “nuclear giants and ethical infants” and by stressing how “[w]e have grasped the mystery of the atom and rejected the Sermon on the Mount.”
These ethical and moral traditions also forge the foundations for the Just War tradition, which at least in theory attempts to stipulate when and how wars can and should be fought. Can A.I. truly make that decision? Have humans lost the will to even ponder such deeper issues that they must allow computer algorithms to conduct such affairs? While there may be such a thing as Artificial Intelligence, there can never be anything like Artificial Wisdom. Wisdom and its traditions remain one of the critical foundations for civilization. Even the great Chinese strategist Sun Tzu (reflecting the Taoist tradition of wisdom) famously warned: “But a kingdom that has once been destroyed can never come again into being nor can the dead ever be brought back to life. Hence the enlightened ruler is heedful, and the good general full of caution. This is the way to keep a country at peace and an army intact.”1
Although Why War? is the last book written by Christopher Coker, it most certainly is not the last word on the topic of why the phenomenon of war has been so ubiquitous in human history. It is also worth noting that this book will not be the final word on the extraordinary intellectual legacy of Christopher Coker, whose works and insights will no doubt continue to influence and inspire future generations of scholars.
1 Tzu, Sun. “The Internet Classics Archive | the Art of War by Sun Tzu.” Classics.mit.edu, https://classics.mit.edu/Tzu/artwar.html. Accessed 7/21/2024.
Liberalism, Realism, or … Integralism? Perusing John J. Mearsheimer’s Book The Great Delusion Yale University Press, 2019
Reviewed by Vlad Alalykin-Izvekov
"The whole is greater than the sum of its parts."
—Aristotle Abstract
The paper presents an analysis of the scholarly views of prominent American political scholar John J. Mearsheimer as set forth in his book The Great Delusion (Yale University Press, 2019). It also offers analysis of other existing approaches, as well as elaborates on a new Integralistic Paradigm which has a potential to integrate the field of International Relations (IR) Theory.
## Brief Biography
John J. Mearsheimer is a renowned scholar in the field of International Relations (IR) Theory, known for his bold, thought-provoking analyses. Born on December 14, 1947, in New York City, he studied at the United States Military Academy at West Point and graduated in 1970. Following his military service, the future scholar started graduate school in political science at Cornell University in 1975, and he received his Ph.D. in 1980. Presently, John J. Mearsheimer is Professor of Political Science at the University of Chicago, where he has taught since 1982. The scholar has made significant contributions to the field of IR theory, particularly in the areas of offensive realism, the security dilemma, and the rationality of foreign policy. His work challenges liberal theories of international relations, arguing for a more realist perspective that emphasizes the role of power and self-interest in shaping global politics. (Mearsheimer et al, 2023; Mearsheimer, 2018; Mearsheimer, 2014).
## The Book
One of Mearsheimer's most intriguing works is his book The Great Delusion: Liberal Dreams and International Realities (Yale University Press, 2019). This thoughtprovoking piece challenges the prevailing liberal democratic norms. It argues that liberal states often overestimate the potential for cooperation and underestimate the role of geopolitical heft and ambitions in international relations. Mearsheimer postulates that liberal dreams of global peace and cooperation are often hindered by the realities of power struggles between states.
Mearsheimer's central thesis revolves around the idea that liberal democracies have a flawed understanding of international politics. He argues that their belief in the power of economic interdependence, democracy promotion, and international institutions is misguided. According to him, these liberal dreams fail to acknowledge the role of power struggles, security dilemmas, and the pursuit of national interests in shaping global affairs.
Mearsheimer's critique of liberal internationalism provides a valuable counterpoint to liberal assumptions prevailing in the West, prompting scholars to reassess their understanding of global politics. His extensive use of historical examples and case studies effectively illustrates his arguments, making a compelling case against liberal utopianism.
One of the book's strengths lies in its rigorous theoretical framework, drawing heavily from the realist school of thought. Mearsheimer effectively employs concepts such as offensive realism and the tragedy of great power politics, and he conducts highly enlightening excursions into political theory and philosophy to explain the perceived flaws in liberal ideology. Reflecting on his multifaceted scholarly approach, the author writes:
“To sum up, the discussion about human nature in chapter 2 focuses on the traits of individuals, the analysis of political liberalism in chapters 3 and 4 concentrates on how it relates to a country’s domestic politics, and the discussion in chapters 5-8 concerns how that ism relates to international politics. This basic template, of course, reflects the three levels of analysis – individual, unit, and system – that concern all students of international relations.” (Mearsheimer, 2019: 3)
By emphasizing the importance of power dynamics and the self-interest of states, Mearsheimer challenges the notion that cooperation and interdependence can lead to lasting peace and stability. The Great Delusion presents a compelling case for a more realist approach to international relations, highlighting the importance of power dynamics, aggression, and self-interest in shaping state behavior. By critiquing liberal ideals and emphasizing the role of power, Mearsheimer encourages more pragmatic understanding of international politics.
It is important to acknowledge, however, that Mearsheimer's critique of liberal internationalism may oversimplify the complexities of global politics. While he convincingly argues for the primacy of power politics, he may downplay the potential benefits of multilateralism and cooperation, as well as the existence of and perennial resistance to rapid change by socio-historic and socio-cultural traditions, values, and views. By primarily focusing on the behavior of “great powers,” his analysis may fail to capture the broader dynamics shaping international relations.
## Other Approaches
While Realists tend to focus on balance of power and political explanations of international relations, Liberalists emphasize democracy, human rights, international trade, and peace. Civilizationists concentrate on histories and cultures in the face of the survivalist instincts of nation states. Whilst Economic Structuralists stress economic factors, Constructivists tend to focus on the role of ideas in international relations.
Ideas by themselves are quite nebulous factors, and, if only based on them, sociocultural systems can play significant roles in changing fundamental social and cultural dynamics.
The English School approach blends some Realist and Liberalist perspectives. While this is a step in the right direction, its attempt at synthesis lacks historic and cultural depth. Critical Theory presents an attempt to challenge the assumptions of those approaches, however it falls short of offering a comprehensive conceptual framework.
Thus, current influential schools of thought in IR Theory appear to have several substantial flaws, most important of which are their one-sidedness and incompleteness.
## Integralistic Paradigm
A balanced solution may be found in the introduction of the Integralistic Paradigm in International Relations Theory. While incorporating crucial elements of Realism, Liberalism, as well as other schools in International Relations Theory, it readily offers an integrated framework for understanding and analyzing global politics.
The Integralistic Paradigm of International Relations Theory would incorporate the Realist focus on balance of power; the Liberalist interest in democracy, human rights, international trade and peace; the Civilizationalist tendency to concentrate on histories and cultures; the Economic Structuralist predisposition to stress economic factors; the Constructivist concern about the role of ideas in international relations; the attempts of the English School to achieve synthesis; and the Critical Theorist concerns about dogmatism, and more.
It would finally allow the building of an adequate architecture of global communication, cooperation, and collective security, as well as serve as a reliable prognostication tool. (Alalykin-Izvekov, 2023a; Alalykin-Izvekov; 2023b)
## Towards Integration of International Relations Theory
Thus, Integralistic Paradigm has a potential to integrate the International Relations Theory field. Several innovative ideas, methods, and technologies may prove to be helpful in the process.
For example, the Periodic Table of Elements may serve as a ready model for such integration. The Periodic Table of Elements is a fundamental tool in the field of chemistry, physics, and other sciences, providing a systematic organization of the building blocks of matter. By examining the structure and principles of the Periodic Table, one can draw intriguing parallels and envision new possibilities for understanding and analyzing the complexities of global politics.
The potential application of Artificial Intelligence (AI) systems in the development of the Integralistic Paradigm holds great promise as well. AI can enhance our understanding of complex data sets, improve predictive capabilities, and aid in identifying patterns and trends in international relations.
By leveraging AI technology, scholars can analyze vast amounts of information, identify potential conflicts, and develop more effective strategies for conflict resolution and cooperation. AI systems can also facilitate data processing, pattern recognition, and ethical assessments, enabling researchers and policymakers to navigate the complexities of global politics more effectively.
## Conclusions
1. John J. Mearsheimer's scholarly contributions to IR Theory have significantly influenced the field, challenging prevailing liberal assumptions and advocating for a more realist perspective. His book The Great Delusion: Liberal Dreams and International Realities offers a thought-provoking critique of liberal ideals and highlights the role of power in shaping international relations.
While the book provides a valuable contribution to the field of international relations, it is important to critically engage with Mearsheimer's arguments and to consider alternative perspectives. By doing so, we can foster a more fundamental and comprehensive, as well as more nuanced understanding of the complex dynamics that shape our world.
- 2. Most contemporary schools of thought in IR Theory offer one-sided, and therefore, at best, incomplete assumptions. The proposed Integralistic Paradigm of International Relations Theory would incorporate the focus of several of these schools of thought. It would finally allow scholars to build an adequate architecture of global communication, cooperation, and collective security, as well as serve as a reliable prognostication tool.
- 3. An Integralistic Paradigm offers the potential of integrating the International Relations Theory field. A number of innovative ideas, methods, and technologies may prove to be helpful in this endeavor. For example, the Periodic Table of Elements may serve as a ready, well-tested model for such integration. Modeling our thought on the Periodic Table, we can draw intriguing parallels and envision new possibilities for understanding and analyzing the complexities of global politics.
- 4. Artificial Intelligence systems have the potential to further revolutionize the study of international relations.

## CCR Style Guide for Submitted Manuscripts
Acceptance This is a peer-reviewed and peer-edited journal, issued twice per year: a spring volume and a fall volume. A manuscript will be accepted for publication only after it has been reviewed by our credentialed Peer Review Committee. Alternatively, if a paper is accepted by the Program Committee for delivery at the Annual Meeting of the International Society for the Comparative Study of Civilizations, it will qualify as peerreviewed, and acceptable, ab initio, assuming that it meets all other quality standards of the journal and has not been published previously elsewhere. Please note that the Comparative Civilizations Review holds the right to reject any submission with, or without, explanation. The manuscript becomes the property of the CCR once it is accepted by the journal for publication.
Submission Manuscripts must be written in English. Submit only ONE COMPLETE DOCUMENT— i.e., no attachments and no subsequent submissions, instructions, or corrections. Send your paper to Managing Editor Peter Hecht (peter.hecht@iscsc.org). If you belatedly discover an egregious error or omission in your submission, email Editor-in Chief Joseph Drew (joseph.drew@iscsc.org) and Managing Editor Peter Hecht (peter.hecht@iscsc.org). We will attempt to address your concerns, provided our printing schedule permits intervention. Once a manuscript is accepted, alterations are at the discretion of the Review staff.
Print Schedule The reviewing and editing process normally takes three to six months. Typically, we put the spring volume together in January or February and the fall volume together in July or August.
Format – Document Submit your manuscript, including tables, figures, and appendices, as a single Microsoft Word file. Page size should be 8.5 x 11 inches. All margins (left, right, top and bottom) should be 1-inch, including placement of your tables, graphs and illustrations. Single space your text. Use a single column layout with both left and right margins justified. Main body text font: 12 pt. Times New Roman. Use high-resolution images when possible. Maximum length of the article should be thirty pages including endnotes, bibliography, etc.
Please DO NOT include page numbers, headers, or footers. The editors will add these. DO NOT utilize automatic formatting to indent paragraphs. DO NOT utilize automatic formatting for the space before or following subheads and paragraphs. Do NOT utilize automatic formatting for numbering or bulleting paragraphs — use only for compact lists.
Format – First Page Begin the document with the title, author’s name, author’s academic department and institutional affiliation and its address (or write “Independent Scholar”), and author’s email address. All this information must be included.
All manuscripts must include an abstract containing a maximum of 150 words. A keyword section should be placed after the abstract, preceded and followed by a line space. Please supply up to five keywords. The word Keywords should be italicized and followed by a colon; the words themselves should be separated by a comma and should not be italicized; no period at the end of the list.
Format – Paragraphs, Subheads, Quotations Do NOT indent paragraphs. A line space should precede and follow each paragraph. Subheads are in bold, flush left, separated by a line space above and below. Long quotations should be placed in a separate paragraph with a .5-inch left indent, no quotation marks, and should be preceded and followed by one-line spaces.
Except for common non-standard English terminology, the use of non-standard English terms should be avoided. Authors should use proper, standard English grammar. Suggested guides include The Elements of Style by William Strunk, Jr. and E. B. White, and The Chicago Manual of Style, University of Chicago Press.
Underlining, using all capitalized letters in words, or employing bold-face type extensively in the text are all discouraged. Whenever possible use italics to indicate text that you wish to emphasize. Use italics for book titles, movie titles, and for foreign terms. Using colored text is prohibited. However, we encourage authors to take advantage of the ability to use color in the production of graphs, maps, and so forth.
Format – Tables, Graphs To the extent possible, tables and graphs should appear in the document near where they are referenced in the text. Large tables or graphs should be put on pages by themselves. Avoid the use of overly small type in tables. In no case should tables or graphs be in a separate document or file. All tables and graphs must fit within 1-inch margins on all sides, in both portrait and landscape view.
Format – Footnotes, Endnotes To create Footnotes and/or Endnotes, you must utilize the Footnote or Endnote option found under the References tab at the top of Word.
Footnotes and endnotes should be in 10 pt. Times New Roman, single spaced, and flush left, ragged right.
Use Arabic numerals for footnotes and Roman numerals for endnotes. Footnote and endnote numbers or symbols in the text must follow, rather than precede, punctuation. Exceedingly long footnotes are probably better handled as endnotes.
Footnotes, when used, should appear at the bottom of the page on which they are referenced. There should be a footnote separator rule (line) above the footnote. Endnotes should appear right at the end of the document and precede References.
References The subhead References (denoting Bibliography, Works Cited, etc.) should appear right after the end of the document, or right after the endnotes, if used. The references should be flush left, ragged right, with a .3-inch hanging indent. Use the format with which you are most comfortable, such as APA (American Psychological Association), MLA (Modern Language Association), or Chicago/Turabian.
|In October 1961, in Salzburg, Austria, an extraordinary group of scholars gathered to create the International Society for the Comparative Study of Civilizations. Among the 26 founding members from Austria, Germany, France, Switzerland, The Netherlands, Spain, Italy, England, Russia, the United States, China and Japan were such luminaries as Pitirim Sorokin and Arnold Toynbee.<br><br>For six days, the participants debated such topics as the definition of “civilization,” problems in the analysis of complex cultures, civilizational encounters in the past, the Orient versus the Occident, problems of universal history, theories of historiography, and the role of the “human sciences” in “globalization.” The meeting was funded by the Austrian government, in cooperation with UNESCO, and received considerable press coverage. Sorokin was elected the Society’s first president.<br><br>After several meetings in Europe, the advancing age of its founding members and the declining health of then president, Othmar F. Anderle, were important factors in the decision to transfer the Society to the United States.<br><br>Between 1968 and 1970 Roger Williams Wescott of Drew University facilitated that transition. In 1971, the first annual meeting of the ISCSC (US) was held in Philadelphia. Important participants in that meeting and in the Society’s activities during the next years included Benjamin Nelson (the Society’s first American president), Roger Wescott, Vytautas Kavolis, Matthew Melko, David Wilkinson, Rushton Coulborn and C.P. Wolf.<br><br>The presidents of the ISCSC are, in order: In Europe, Pitirim Sorokin and Othmar Anderle; in the United States, Benjamin Nelson, Vytautas Kavolis, Matthew Melko, Michael Palencia-Roth, Roger Wescott, Shuntaro Ito (from Japan), Wayne Bledsoe, Lee Daniel Snyder, Andrew Targowski, David Rosner, Toby Huff, and current president Lynn Rhodes. To date, the Society has held annual meetings, most of them in the United States but also in Salzburg, Austria; Santo Domingo, The Dominican Republic; Dublin, Ireland; Chiba, Japan; Frenchman’s Cove, Jamaica; St. Petersburg, Russia; Paris, France; New Brunswick, Canada; Rio de Janeiro, Brazil; Suzhou, China; Bucharest, Romania; Warsaw, Poland; and Alberta, Canada.<br><br>More than 30 countries are represented in the Society’s membership. Its intellectual dynamism and vibrancy over the years have been maintained and enhanced through its annual meetings, its publications, and the participation of such scholars as Talcott Parsons, Hayden White, Immanuel Wallerstein, Gordon Hewes, André Gunder Frank, Marshall Sahlins, Lynn White Jr., and Jeremy Sabloff.<br><br>The Society is committed to the idea that complex civilizational problems can best be approached through multidisciplinary analyses and debate by scholars from a variety of fields.
