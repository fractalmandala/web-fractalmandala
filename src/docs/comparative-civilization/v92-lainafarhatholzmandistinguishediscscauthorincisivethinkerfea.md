Joseph Drew Editor-in-Chief
It is a sad duty to report that a luminary who was long associated with this journal and with the leadership of the International Society for the Comparative Study of Civilizations, Dr. Laina Farhat-Holzman, has died.
She first appeared in the Comparative Civilizations Review in the Fall of 1997, with a paper entitled Zoroastrianism and Its Earthly Tenure. In this wide-ranging article, she covered the questions of the actual dates of Zoroaster, his origins, the adoption of his faith by the Persian nobility and the empire, the ultimate collapse of Zoroastrianism, and the effect of the religion on five other faiths. This was followed in the Spring of 2000, with a very thoughtful review lauding a recent book about Persian and Iranian history.
An expert on Iran’s history and culture, she had lived in that country first as the wife of an Iranian graduate student and then, fifteen years later, as a cross-cultural expert on a large government project.
Dr. Farhat-Holzman opined in the review that Iranians had the capability to explore ideas from everywhere and adapt them to their own substantial culture.
Nonetheless, she said:
They had one great monarch (Cyrus the Great), but the monarchs that followed him throughout 2500 years, until Reza Shah Pahlavi, were a succession of either disasters or monsters. Monarchy is a flawed system, but Iranians are still looking for a man on a white horse to rescue them from themselves.
This review was followed a year later by a peer-reviewed article entitled The Shahnameh of Ferdowsi: An Icon to National Identity. So impressive was her scholarship that she was elected Associate Editor of this journal that year, 2001, and just one year later she was named Editor.
Over the years she spoke frequently at annual meetings around the world and appeared as an author in the journal with forty-seven articles under her name. I count on the Digital Commons index (thank you, Brigham Young University) fully 164 listings of her role or work at the Comparative Civilizations Review.
Regretfully, Laina had to give up her post as Senior Editor and Publisher after the Spring 2014 edition, as her publication commitments to her bi-weekly article series in a range of Northern California newspapers, vivid and trenchant journalism anticipated and followed by many readers, and in other media, were overwhelming by that point.
Although she continued to author book reviews for the journal, she capped her administrative leadership role with an extensive appearance in the very next issue, as author of a study entitled The Great Literary Utopias Have a Nightmarish History. In that article, she covered fully 2500 years of the literature of utopias, and dystopias, from Plato onwards, revealing her knowledge of Comparative Literature and World Civilizations.
She began with a quote from the Rubaiyat of Omar Khayyam:
Ah Love! Could thou and I with Fate conspire To grasp this sorry Scheme of Things entire, Would not we shatter it to bits ---and then Re-mold it nearer to the Heart’s Desire!
That essay concluded with a review of dystopias that exist at the present time.
Other dystopias still with us are not the descendants of Plato or More; they are the products of another ideology, the fantasy of the perfect and just realms mirroring an imagined perfect world of early Islam.
She summarized this way:
Almost all these utopian experiments have morphed into dystopias, resulting in the deaths of millions of people and global war. But we must never overlook the seductiveness of utopian dreams and nightmares.
In the Spring of 2017, she reviewed two books for the journal. They were by Mary Elise Sarotte, The Collapse: The Accidental Opening of the Berlin Wall, and by Barry Rubin & Wolfgang G. Schwanitz, Nazis, Islamists, and the Making of the Modern Middle East. The two books, she wrote, both “treat one important subject that dominated 20th century history: the rise, fall, and influence of Germany.”
Among her observations:
The great irony here is that the Muslim world always managed to choose losers: first the Nazis, and then the Communists. Now they are following the latest Islamo-fascist cult, ISIS…. The connection between the Nazis and the Muslim World is alive and well but is doomed to the same end that the Nazis earned….This is a sad observation when one thinks of the wonderful contributions of Germany before that to science, music, literature, and philosophy.
For the next issue she reviewed two books. One was now-Vice President J. D. Vance’s book Hillbilly Elegy: A Memoir of a Family and Culture in Crisis. It was, she wrote, “about the culture of failure and violence that darkened his childhood.” Interestingly, she closed the review by observing that “Vance’s book makes it possible to see the virtues and failings of people who might otherwise be known to us only when they hoot and holler at a Trump rally.”
The other book was entitled The Shape of the New: Four Big Ideas and How They Made the Modern World, by Scott L. Montgomery and Daniel Chirot. It drew on main ideas from Adam Smith, Karl Marx, Charles Darwin, Alexander Hamilton, and Thomas Jefferson. She concluded the latter review with this observation:
As our authors point out in their chapter on the “Making of Democracy,” after the emergence of a number of new democratic states in the 20th century, the process stalled so that by 2014 only forty percent of the world’s population now live in fully democratic societies. Will there be a new, fourth wave of democratization? Present signs are not favorable.
She authored a final book review for the Spring, 2018, issue. It covered two historical novels that she recommended, Munich, by Robert Harris, and December 6, by Martin Cruz Smith. Both, as she wrote, explored “What Ifs” in history. Laina loved such challenges.
However, Laina was not done yet with the CCR. She made her last contribution to the journal via an “In Memoriam” article honoring her friend, the late Prof. Walter Benesch of the University of Alaska, Fairbanks. She attached to her kind words and memories of this stalwart member of the ISCSC an interview with Dr. Benesch by a local Alaska newspaper back in 2002. It was, she wrote, “such a charming description of this unusual man that we offer it here.”
The attached article did round out our memories of the man’s nature, but perhaps only Laina would have possessed the forethought to have us bid farewell in such a touching and meaningful way.
When Dr. Farhat-Holzman died, she was the subject of published obituaries that heaped praise upon her life and story. These editorials and reviews of her life were carried, notably, in the California newspapers that featured her writing for many years. They observed that she contributed to the community as a writer, a historian, and a columnist and that she had taught World History and Islamic Civilization, particularly at Golden Gate University in San Francisco. She also had served as Executive Director of the San Francisco United Nations Association and was a frequent speaker at the World Affairs Council and the Commonwealth Club.
The Santa Cruz Sentinel, one of the papers for which she was a regular columnist, commented that she had “amassed a large following of fans who looked forward to her op-eds, articles and movie reviews.”
Laina was born Marlena Chazen in Rochester, New York, but she concluded her high school years in California. She was an English major at the University of California at Los Angeles, and there she met and married an Iranian exchange student, Hormoz Farhat. In 1956 she spent two years living in Teheran, Iran, and this is where Laina explored her interest in ancient Persian and world religions, as well as Middle East studies.
She earned her Ph.D. in Comparative Literature with a minor in World Civilizations from the University of Southern California in 1971. In 1972, single once again, she relocated to the San Francisco Bay Area (Menlo Park) and began work as an editor for United Technologies Corporation.
Although she now had her two children to care for, she soon was teaching English to Persian military students (1975-1977). Then, in addition to the teaching position at Golden Gate University, she became active with the American Association of University Women, presenting programs on foreign affairs. Later, she was a member and officer of the Monterey Bay chapter of the Foreign Affairs Council.
Those of us lucky enough to collaborate with her had the good fortune to meet her husband, E.H. “Mickey” Holzman, an intelligent, kind, and supportive partner for Laina.
In her later years, while living in Aptos, California, she wrote op-eds and other pieces for the Santa Cruz Sentinel and the Register Pajaronian, and she taught monthly classes in world civilizations, current affairs, and understanding the current political landscape of the United States (called her "brown-bag series" as attendees were encouraged to bring their lunches).
When she died, many paid tribute to her, including her colleagues in the comparative study of civilizations community who were dedicated to her writing and her administrative work on behalf of this international body. Thus, the president of the International Society for the Comparative Study of Civilizations, Lynn Rhodes, wrote that Laina “was a dear friend and an esteemed, valuable, long-time, member of the ISCSC.” In a memo circulated widely, President Rhodes reviewed Laina’s biography and praised her for many meaningful presentations at ISCSC conferences and for continuing to advance civilizational study and academic research and writing. She also observed that her “contributions to scholarship and her many publications have helped to educate many and to inspire meaningful, critical thinking.”
Dr. Farhat-Holzman with Dr. Andrew Targowski
Two former presidents of the association also posted expressions of their sorrow when hearing the news.
Dr. Andrew Targowski said that “we mourn the loss of a venerable lady whose life’s work left an indelible mark on the field of civilizational development through comparative analysis. Her scholarly pursuits and her dedication to understanding the intricate tapestries of human society have contributed significantly to our grasp of various world cultures and their interconnections throughout history.” He continued:
Her approach, characterized by rigorous scholarship and a profound commitment to cross-cultural understanding, has paved new pathways for scholars and thinkers alike. She was not only a mentor to many but also a pioneering force who challenged conventional boundaries to explore the rich contours of human history and its impact on modern society. Her passing is a profound loss to our community and to all who have had the privilege of knowing her.
Citing her “unwavering integrity, compassion, and dedication to enlightenment and progress,” he concluded:
As we bid farewell to a truly remarkable individual, let us honor her memory by continuing to pursue knowledge with the same fervor and dedication she exemplified throughout her life. Her spirit, undiminished by her passing, remains a guiding light for all who seek to understand human civilization in its myriad forms.
Former President David Rosner wrote in a letter, “I am deeply sorry to hear this news. She was a force of nature.” He added that “this is a sad development for the ISCSC. She was a towering figure in our organization.”
Dr. Vlad Alalykin-Izvekov, a Senior Editor and Board Member of the Comparative Civilizations Review, wrote that this was “indeed, very sad news” and he observed that she, like him, had her family origins in Eastern Europe.
And Dr. Thomas Rienzo, the journal’s Digital Media Editor, wrote: “sorry to hear of Laina’s death. I always enjoyed her writing.”

Laina Farhat-Holzman, gave a “Keynote Address” in the Kirsh auditorium of the Massachusetts Institute of Technology. It was entitled “Will Religion Mitigate the Clash of Civilizations?”
Dr. Farhat-Holzman pointed out that religious or cultural conflict exists between Christians and Muslims in Nigeria, between Muslims and Hindus in the Indian subcontinent, between Muslims and Buddhists in Thailand, and, of course, between Muslims and Jews in the Middle East. She characterized religious militancy as a minority of believers becoming caught up in an idea.
Often religious orthodoxy masks greed and the desire for power. This leads to what Farhat-Holzman called “irrational history”, which was a main theme of her talk.
Can religion mitigate the clash of civilizations? Overall, Farhat-Holzman thought not. Irrational religion would eventually alienate its own supporters as people grew tired of the culture of death and Arab regimes lost the ability to finance religious wars when “green technology” replaced petrochemicals as a source of energy.

## Europe Rethinks Multiculturalism (2014)
Americans, unlike Europeans, have always made room for new citizens from other countries. Since the end of World War II, however, western European countries have been trying to counter their old patterns of bigotry by welcoming all immigrants fleeing horrors in their old countries. The governments of the UK, France, Germany, and Scandinavia have offered social services, welfare, housing, and public schooling for the newcomers.
What they have not done is to make demands on immigrants that they accept the values and behaviors of their new countries. Such demands, it was thought, assumed that their own cultures were in some way superior to that of the newcomers, a view not popular with multiculturalists. This well-intentioned policy is now facing revision  and many people hope it is not too late.
The American model has been different from that of Europe. We received hordes of refugees from the mid-19th century until World War I, refugees who were needed in the work force. The Americans who were already here certainly did not welcome people with disparate and often unpalatable cultures; every immigrant wave faced bigotry at first. However, within one generation, most of the children of these immigrants thrived and were as American as their neighbors. Immigrants took citizenship classes and worked hard to become Americans. They all learned English and few of them had any desire to return to their parents' countries. They were American.
Although some immigrant groups brought with them criminal organizations: the Italian Mafia, the Chinese Tongs, German Bunds, and Irish criminal-political networks, these were designed to evade, not replace American law. Moreover, American standards of tolerance required that people eventually adopt the values of the host country, and our immigrant populations have been integrated.
Europe's problem was that they did not have a tradition of integrating large numbers of immigrants into their age-old national identities. Even dissident Christian groups such as the Albigensians and Cathars were exterminated when they refused to accept Catholicism. Christianity throughout two millennia never considered the one small group with another religion, the Jews, as acceptable citizens. This only changed during the Reformation, when finally, some Jews were able to distinguish themselves as true German, French, or English citizens.
With the Reformation, however, came a two-century war between Catholic and Protestant states, also waged on the citizens with the wrong religion within those states.
The British barred English Catholics from the full rights of citizenship until the Pope stopped persecuting Protestants in Catholic countries. This should be the model of all tolerance: reciprocity.
European countries (and America) have welcomed Muslim immigrants, both the elites who were already educated in European schools (Persians and Afghans), and those perceived to be very downtrodden economic refugees: Turkish, Somali, Indonesian, Pakistani, Iraqi, Chechen, and Palestinian.
In Europe, these migrants were admitted unconditionally, with consequences of violence and lawlessness, particularly against women and children. Such groups have special family law, intolerance of the majority culture's mores, and excuses for religious-based violence. Why else would the British press try to call Pakistani Muslim rapists “South Asian?” Fear of being branded bigots motivates this.
Now, at last, the worm is turning. British Prime Minister David Cameron has proposed new laws that will permit seizing the passports of Britons who have traveled abroad to fight with terrorist Jihadis. They will not be readmitted and will lose their citizenship. Jihadis preaching violence in Britain will no longer be protected by British law. They will be deported.
The Dutch and Danes have finally scrapped their multicultural indulgence of Muslim migrants. They now demand that citizenship requires learning the language, mores, and values of their hosts. France was the first European country to ban the Islamic headscarf in government institutions and the total burqa in public (insulting to women and used as disguises by criminals and terrorists)
The US and Canada have been slower to adopt such measures, which may be changing now. Calling Hassan Nidal's Islam-inspired Camp Hood massacre “workplace violence” instead of Muslim terror is outrageous political correctness.

## The United States of Europe Is Obviously Premature (2015)
Europe is a geographic designation: the westernmost peninsula of the Eurasian continental landmass. For the past 7000 years, it has also been the home of a restless mass of human beings, always on the move, settling and moving on, replacing former residents and coming up with one invention after another, the most important of which, from today's discussion, came from ancient Greece: participatory government (Athens); Rome: written and progressively enlarging law; and England: that kings are not allpowerful (Magna Carta); and all of them: the religious, scientific, industrial, and political revolutions.
Restless, creative Europe (and its child, America) has given the world modern civilization: the good things being nation-states governed by their own people under rule of law; majority populations (middle class) wealthy and healthy beyond imagining; cities clean and vibrant with culture and convenience; and citizens living in safety and civility as in no cities before now. Europe's Scientific Revolution did much of this.
Now for the bad things. Europe's restlessness has also given the world endless warfare since the collapse of the Roman Empire in the 5th Century, culminating in two of the most frightening wars, the second of which, World War II, drew in the entire world and a third (the Cold War) which almost destroyed the planet. The dreadful things frightened Europe's elites so much that they determined to do something radical: follow the example of the United States of America and create a United States of Europe, one in which war would be impossible.
The American example is not easy to replicate, however. Laina Farhat-Holzman is a prolific author, the copious extent of whose production challenges the reader who is ambitious enough to go below any one work to grasp the larger issues and positions she presents. The four books that concern us now are: 1) Strange Birds from Zoroaster’s Nest (Revised Edition), 2) God’s Law or Man’s Law: The Fundamentalist Challenge to Secular Rule, 3) How Do You Know That: A Guide to Critical Thinking about Global Issues, and 4) Worldchangers: Ten Inventions That Changed Everything.
When read together, these works not only exemplify the value of taking a more expansive perspective of Farhat-Holzman’s work but also give rise to a hope that Holzman herself will continue her intellectual journey to its compelling conclusion.
Strange Birds from Zoroaster’s Nest (Revised Edition) was originally published in 2000. It is a gem of a work. There certainly are a handful of other books that can serve as more extensive introductions to Zoroaster and Zoroastrian thought, such as Zoroastrianism: An Introduction by Jenny Rose or In Search of Zarathustra by Paul Kriwaczek.
However, one of the enticing uniqueness’s of Strange Birds from Zoroaster’s Nest is precisely the pithy, clear, and sensitive way it goes directly to the heart of Zoroaster’s teaching. It focuses in on Zoroaster’s major foundational contribution to all the world’s later major religions: namely his transformation of the understanding of religion from that of “paying off evil forces with sacrifices,” what the writer calls “a protection racket,” to a process of deep self-awareness and deliberate ethical behavior.” (FarhatHolzman, 2003, 14)
It was Karl Jaspers who most prominently asserted the idea of a transformational age of religion: an age that ushered in both a new sense of moral understanding, and a new sense of the role of the individual in the search for that morality. Jaspers, who called the period between approximately 800 and 200 B.C.E. the “axial age”, saw this period as the age during which the spiritual understanding of humanity made a major rotational shift in a series of parallel but independent religious emergences. These included, in due course, the teachings of Zoroaster, Buddha, Confucius, Moses, Jesus, and Mohammed.
Farhat-Holzman, using more recent chronological evidence, makes the case that, in fact, it was Zoroaster who was the founding influence on all these religions. She asserts the need to give him a far greater recognition for being the “founding father” of moralitybased religion per se, through the direct influence of Zoroastrianism on the other faiths.
To support this position, Farhat-Holzman follows the train of Zoroastrian belief through Judaism, Christianity and Islam as it progressed, respectively, from its origins as a conceived battle between Ahura Mazda and Ahriman (Zoroastrianism) to becoming the battle between fulfilling or not fulfilling God’s word (Judaism), to becoming the battle between God and Satan (Christianity), then the battle between Believer and NonBeliever (Islam) and, eventually, arriving at the abstract philosophical choice between good and evil.
Farhat-Holzman’s advocacy for recognition of Zoroaster’s positive historic role, however, is counter-balanced by her less than positive portrayal of the life cycle of the religion as such. This includes her description of the establishment of Zoroastrianism as the official religion of the Sassanian Empire, and the increasing domination of its priests, rituals, and dogmas. Eventually Zoroastrianism came into conflict with rising Islam and was subsequently persecuted by it.
Today Zoroastrianism’s few remaining adherents are the prosperous Parsees of India. The ultimate irony is the accompanying survival of the misguided dogmatic thinking of its priests who today, though the community faces increasing diminution in numbers because of intermarriage, insist that only the offspring of both a Zoroastrian mother and father can be recognized as a member of the faith.
Farhat-Holzman’s God’s Law or Man’s Law, The Fundamentalist Challenge to Secular Rule was published between the original publication of Strange Birds from Zoroaster’s Nest and the present second edition. It is important in and of itself, but also to our understanding some of the additional material Farhat-Holzman has added to the second edition. That new material work goes beyond specifically discussing Zoroastrianism to bring in present day fundamentalist Islam and its increasing conflict with almost every expression of a globalizing world, religious and non-religious, that fundamentalist Islam sees as evil personified.
In God’s Law or Man’s Law, the author looks at “good” and “evil” today as basically a struggle between rationality and science on one side and fundamentalist religious doctrines and cults on the other. She recognizes that these fundamentalists do not take their cues from the nobler thoughts of the great religious thinkers of the past. I will refrain from outlining the specific material Farhat-Holzman presents because her book is an easy and quick read. It is a short but broad coverage of most of the major present day religious groups and doctrines in conflict with modern progressive thought and practices. Worldchangers: Ten Inventions That Changed Everything is the author’s succinct demonstration of how rational thought and empirically based decision-making made, on ten occasions, transformational charges to a world filled with superstition and dogma. This book and the fourth work under discussion, How Do You Know That?, are short works specifically designed to be resources for academic courses.
The first book identifies ten critical inventions: fire, agriculture, water technologies, printing press, telescope, communications, transport, energy, the computer, and future transformative science  i.e., space travel and brain science, as her candidates for the ten human inventions that have most qualitatively changed human life. How Do You Know That? focuses on the need for critical thinking, particularly on issues that Farhat-Holzman feels strongly about. Farhat-Holzman is at her core a scholar of civilization and therefore putting her works in the context of the phenomenon of civilization may be the best way to fully appreciate them. In contrast, Dr. Farhat-Holzman is passionately concerned and involved in today’s world, and she has a clarion message she wishes to give. On the other hand, she, herself, presents powerful evidence that today such critical thinking is becoming harder and rarer, given the nature of overwhelming and constantly changing information, and the domination of opinion by narrow disciplinary specialists.
In the end, I hope the time will come, the sooner the better, when Farhat-Holzman will write a synthesis of her independent works and meld her varied observations and conclusions into something greater than the sum of the parts. Personally, I suspect what is holding this back is her reluctance, like Thucydides and Djilas, to come to terms with the evidence that she is studying the unraveling of her own present civilization. It is a felicitous fact that the Table of Contents of two of Dr. Farhat-Holzman’s pathsetting books provide a quick reference — and important points — for the topics she covers.
Below is a transcript, with her annotations, of the Table of Contents of Strange Birds from Zoroaster’s Nest: An Overview of Revealed Religions.
## 1. The Mystery of Human Religion
In today’s world, we are accustomed to think of religion in terms of what the 12th century CE Omar Khayyam called “the two-and-seventy jarring Sects,” an understatement, certainly. However, a case can be made that visiting Martian anthropologists, if there are such, would see enough commonality in religions to consider them variants of one human religion. Today, more than ever, we could be called “Homo religious” rather than Homo sapiens.
## 2. The Common Stream of Human Religion
Religion is a human institution with more similarity than difference in its variety of expressions. Underlying all the world’s religions is the common need to attribute meaning to human experience and to attempt to find reasons for the frightening events that beset us. It comforts us to think that we are not helpless pawns of malevolent forces, that there are things we can do to avert the worst excesses of powers before whom we tremble. To know the rules, to be players in the game, gives us at least a more level playing field. Reasoning creatures, which we are, are not amenable to chaos theory. Religion is our attempt to reason through the unreasonable.
## 3. Beyond Priests and Human Sacrifice: Human Responsibility
At a certain point in human history, a profound thinker (Zoroaster) was struck by the idea that the inscrutable universe, at least for human beings, was governed by goodness and reason. In a flash of insight, he recognized that most things that we find evil are the fruit of human free will. At that moment, the process of transforming religion from a “protection racket” (paying off evil forces with sacrifices) to a process of self-awareness and deliberate ethical behavior had begun. The two concepts still live side by side in our world, each concept a part of all human religions.
## 4. Good and Evil: No Shades of Gray
Monotheism, unlike polytheism, has always had difficulty with accounting for evil. How can a god of creation, a god of goodness and mercy, create evil? Why would a good god put warfare, slavery, torture, disease, hunger, and death in the world to blight man’s life? It is fascinating to see how each revealed religion deals with this, and how Zoroaster’s take on this issue affected all religions that followed.
## 5. Unforeseen Consequences: The Transformed Message
What separates prophets or shamans from ordinary human experience is the intensity of their contact with the divine. Monotheistic prophets had unequivocal visions that were succinct, brief, and direct, and their followers believed them. But over the years, the messages were blunted by older voices, traditional practices that would not die. No religion has escaped this fate.
## 6. State Religion: The Kiss of Death
When a religion is embraced by state power, the state has a new moral authority, and the religion has a jump in recruitment. This benefit to both soon transforms government into hypocrite and religion into tyrant. Each of the monotheistic religions had unpleasant experience with political success, leading to grass roots revolts that both the state and church called “heresy.” When Zoroastrianism became the state faith of three successive Persian dynasties, it gave rise to some of the most potent heresies that have dogged every other religion to this day. The Islamic conquest of Persia nearly led to the demise of Zoroastrianism and the corruption of Persian Islam by underground Zoroastrian cults. Today’s Islamic Republic of Iran is an uncanny reincarnation of State Zoroastrianism.
## 7. The Role of Zoroastrian Heresies in Shaping Later Monotheistic Religions
As the first monotheistic universal religion, supported by three Persian empires, Zoroastrianism was able to affect the religious thought of the Greeks, Romans, Jews, Christians, Muslims, Hindus, and Buddhists. Even after its demise as a major religion, Zoroastrian heresies fed the streams of heresies of every other major religion, to this very day, with one special fantasy: the savior frenzy.
## 8. Zoroastrian Concepts in World Religions
While few people today even know who Zoroaster was, concepts that came either from him or from his later followers shape our psyches, dreams, and nightmares.
The concept of responsible, accountable humanity with free will; death and resurrection; the insistence that there is only one truth; the cosmic conflict between God and the Prince of Darkness; the irreconcilable difference between flesh and spirit and resulting misogyny; angels, and devils enmeshed in human affairs; and demonization of “the other” have become the hallmarks of all major human religion, including even Hinduism and Buddhism, which are not monotheistic.
## 9. Zoroaster’s Mark on the Secular World
The 16th century ushered in the printing press and soon thereafter the industrial revolution. Modern science won authority over religion as the source of truth and became a new faith, promising material perfectibility. Political democracy detached from state religion also promised the perfectibility of a just society. Did religion go into a decline, or did it flow into other arenas? What then were Naziism, Marxism, the Cold War, and what is religious fascism?
## 10. The Modern Dilemma: A World Religion?
A survey of today’s world will show us a thin elite of rational humanism floating on a sea of habitual religiosity at best and fanatical religious politics at worst. Both the conservative pieties and the fanatical fundamentalisms are almost indistinguishable by sect or cult; by their fruits rather than their names we would know them anywhere.
## Epilogue: The Future of Religion
Monotheistic religious values are now in the mainstream of global values of a responsible humanity with reverence for creation. Yet we live in a world that is increasingly superstitious, emotional, and desperate for rescue from misery.
The world of the 21st century has begun to globalize, although not homogenize, into discrete layers. Institutions such as governance resemble each other around the world more than they resemble the people they govern. The criminal underworld has become international, as has religious fundamentalism. There are far more differences within each culture than across cultures along these new fault lines. Religion of all sorts is stratifying into parallel universes rather than consolidating into any single faith. One religion cannot come out of such turmoil for some time to come, if ever.
