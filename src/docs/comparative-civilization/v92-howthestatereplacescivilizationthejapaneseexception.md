Robert Bedeski Professor Emeritus, University of Victoria, Victoria B.C. Canada reb1206ck@gmail.com Abstract
By conceptualizing civilization and state as integrated systems consisting of interacting subsystems, their similarities and differences can be analyzed. The central hypothesis is that civilization consists of three material subsystems — economy, government, and knowledge — and one non-material subsystem, transcendence. When the transcendent subsystem is diminished or destroyed by decree or overwhelming rational and scientific evidence, the emergence of the state occurs as in the cases of the Mongol Empire, the Soviet Union, and communist China.
The apparent universality of this observation is countered by an examination of the Japanese civilization and state, where imperial transcendence dominated and reinforced the militant state. After the defeat and an American occupation that abrogated the imperial mystique as state doctrine, collective transcendence metamorphized into individual immanence, thus maintaining a balance between civilization and state. PostCold War political and economic reforms provide evidence that the secular state is sovereign in the material realm with no intention to affect civilization-nourishing immanence.
Keywords: Japan, state, civilization, theory, transcendence A Theory Concerning Civilization and State
What is civilization? Two prerequisites are a written language and cities, which bestow order and continuity on large societies. Both accomplishments permit increased complexity, which enables control of extensive territory and peoples as well as generation of wealth and power. Pre-civilized tribal society is barbarism, where lives are spent raiding, hunting, and gathering in order to survive. The first stage of the medieval Mongol empire was not yet a civilization, in contrast to neighboring China or Russia, where the nomadic warriors conquered and ruled. Once a civilization is established, it takes on the characteristics of a system — an interacting pattern of mutually dependent parts or subsystems.
Considering a civilization as a system indicates boundaries and subsystems.
Japanese civilization evolved later than the Chinese and adopted some of its institutions, including city planning and the writing system, adapting them to local conditions and creating a unique culture in the archipelago. Thalassic and oceanic coasts form boundaries within which Japanese civilization developed cities and a written language.
Four subsystems can be identified in a civilization system: Economic Subsystem, Government Subsystem, Knowledge Subsystem, and Transcendent Subsystem. Removal or damage to any of them results in erasing or diminishing that civilization.
The first three contribute to the material survival of individuals. The Economic Subsystem includes trade and commerce of services, raw materials, and agricultural and manufactured goods. It provides necessities for life as well as luxuries. The Government Subsystem provides coordination and currency, internal and external security, communications, and laws to guide behavior and ensure justice. The Knowledge Subsystem includes the written and spoken record of what has transpired, lessons learned, and future plans, as well as techniques and discoveries.
These three subsystems are based on material rationality — the cumulative observations and conclusions about cause and effect that form the basis of education, making intergenerational continuity possible.
The fourth component of civilization is the Transcendent Subsystem. Its origin is in the minds of individual men and takes the form of religion, philosophy, art and literature. Unlike the above material subsystems, the Transcendent Subsystem mirrors the imagination and thoughts originating in the minds of civilization’s inhabitants. It addresses unseen and unverifiable phenomena which may or may not exist.
Transcendent ideas cannot be validated by scientific methods nor established to obey the laws of cause and effect. Transcendence exists in the minds of men and motivates individuals and groups to act and to assign meaning to life and death. An example of material and transcendent statements: If a person says, “the moon is green,” other observers can see for themselves whether the statement is true or false. But if the same person says, “I think the moon is red,” others have no way of ascertaining what his thoughts actually are. He may be lying, joking, inebriated, or hallucinating, but there is no objective method to determine the status of that thought at that moment.
Transcendence operates in the same way — Greek myths were stories of unprovable gods and events but were powerful moral lessons and were intrinsic to their civilization, which included the other three subsystems.
Can this systemic and sub-systemic approach be applied to contemporary civilization in its post-modern iteration? My hypothesis is that material rationality is led by and increasingly subject to science; it has coalesced the three material subsystems and has diminished the fourth non-substantial transcendence —“‘the infinitude of the Comprehensive,’ the ultimate encompassing reality that exists beyond the world of time and space.”1
This tectonic movement of subsystems has created the modern state, where myth and religion occupy less and less space and are sometimes tolerated when helpful to the state, and where art and literature are dependent upon the state in the absence or weakness of transcendence. (See Figure 2)
The Mongol Empire, as Pax Mongolica, was a precursor to the modern state in that its rulers were exclusively concerned with material power, and while bowing to the worship of Tengri and consulting shamans, they were tolerant of other religions to which various khans converted in later years. Their tolerant attitude conveyed recognition that domination of multiple societies was easier when prevailing beliefs were not threatened. The French, Russian, and Chinese revolutions saw traditional religion as enemies of their new order. The American Revolution was fought in part against the official Church of England and restored freedom of religion while creating a wall between secular government and religious faith.

Of the above four subsystems, the Transcendent Subsystem is the most vulnerable, largely because its source is spontaneous, non-replicable, and not dependent on the material world. The appearance of transcendence in history occurred in what German philosopher Karl Jaspers termed the "Axial Age." It was preceded by millennia of Neolithic progress in the accumulation of material security and the refinement of the three material subsystems, with cities and written language as platforms where transcendence could emerge and blossom to create civilizations.
Transcendence is a mental construct influencing further thought, insight, emotions, creativity, and belief. It might be prompted by intuition or insight. It is a source of human energy leading to purposive action. It is what makes us human, enabling progress and collective solidarity. Transcendence produces art, religion, martyrdom, morals, and philosophy. But as the latest accretion to the human condition, it is also the most exposed to denial and dismissal. Modern civilization is notable for accelerated progress in economy, government, and scientific knowledge as well as their mutual reinforcement. This results in the modern sovereign nation-state, which makes and enforces laws. Transcendence, in contrast, has seen slight improvement or progress, even though its expressions have attempted to keep up with material transformations. Notions that produce transcendence include emotions such as courage, love, hope, fear, beauty, and faith, and have not modified much since the Axial Age.
Along with civilizations metamorphosing into states, globalization is another of several processes occurring in the post-modern age. The present global pattern is one of sovereign nation-states, each with vital interests, demarcated territory, distinctive culture, and government structure. Particularism and sovereignty are declining in the presence of a global interlinked economy and electronic communications.
Science and technology are increasingly common global possessions, accessible to users and innovators. International migrations are breaking down demographic homogeneity in the Euro-American democracies. While global problems beg for global solutions, nation-states persist in reserving the right to make final decisions.
As the English poet William Wordsworth wrote, “The world is too much with us.” I would translate this phrase as a lament about the material world crowding out the higher things in life — including the legacy of transcendent ideas and sentiments that gave life meaning and purpose. The referred "world" is the sum of material concerns that make life possible and enhance life security. It is the world of necessary things, laws, economics, science, and customs. As this world is progressively benefiting humankind’s survival and growth, it is also diminishing transcendence, which has few visible or sensible payoffs. Growth of the state also diminishes human freedom, which emerged in the Axial Age.
## Japan as Civilization and State
Japan may have escaped — or at least postponed — the secularization trap, which nullifies civilization and empowers the state. Japanese civilization and state present a unique mix of coexistence marked by historical adaptation and periodic violence. Its archipelagic geography contributes a large part of its character, one that allows leaders to adopt or reject models or fashions from abroad. Two major top-down transformations occurred within the space of less than a century (1868-1945). The first, the Meiji Restoration, elevated Shinto to state doctrine and partnered it with the dogma of the emperor’s semi-divinity. The second was defeat in war and abrogation of the imperial myth; one result was driving transcendence underground to become a national piety expressed in multiple forms, including festivals, art, literature, and amulets. Even the exchange of gifts, with elaborate wrapping, or buying souvenirs to give as gifts (omiyagi), takes on a ritualistic aroma.
This post-war transformation shattered previous collective transcendence into individual immanence 2 and had the effect of restraining the state, which might otherwise have moved to secularize society when state Shinto was drained of its imperial mystique. A common Japanese culture, rooted in language and custom, stubbornly retains piety towards nature, even in cities where apartments bloom like bamboo.

Adaptation of Chinese civilization in the ninth century established the state with new levels of material subsystems. Adoption of Buddhism did not replace, but supplemented, existing paganism and Shinto, setting a precedent of religious accommodation. With the introduction of Christianity in the sixteenth century, Japanese toleration of foreign religion was tested. Perceived as an external threat and a faith that could challenge the central shogunate, the new religion was persecuted and prohibited.
Selective importation of aspects of the Western state — particularly Prussian — in the Meiji Restoration transformed the material foundations of Japan, while traditional civilization in its transcendent expression was not only retained but also employed by the state to reinforce imperial centralization. Further adaptation of Western government structures proceeded during the Taisho Era, and signs pointed to the emergence of a democratic state.
The creation of a modern, patriotic, and centralized army had imperialist consequences. The army and navy proved their mettle against two empires in the First Sino-Japanese War and in the Russo-Japanese War. By 1930, the combination of global economic depression and tightened Western colonialism unleashed ultra-nationalism, with the army claiming the mantle of Imperial Will and the spirit of Bushido. Less extreme but following the same path, the navy led the war against the Western colonial powers. In the ignominious defeat of 1945, the Japanese state was reconstructed based on the capitalist Economic Subsystem, a democratic Government Subsystem, and a Knowledge Subsystem oriented to the West.
Where the pre-1945 Transcendent Subsystem had incorporated Shinto and the cult of the emperor, occupation reforms replaced it with a constitutional monarchy.
Shinto reduced its nationalist spirit, and a new national identity emerged with a focus on improving well-being and equality. Foreign and defense policy aligned with the United States under the “Treaty of Mutual Cooperation and Security between the United States and Japan “(1960).
The nearest Japan came to secularization of its civilization was in the occupation reforms when the removal of the emperor from the state system was considered. Without the monarchy and in a Japanese "republic," a compliant population might have become rudderless, vulnerable to diverse ideologies from abroad and class warfare. The American occupation set the course for the preservation of a modified civilization and formation of a conservative, non-threatening state, thanks to Article Nine of the constitution.
## Japan’s Chapter II: Renunciation of War, Article Nine
Aspiring sincerely to an international peace based on justice and order, the Japanese people forever renounce war as a sovereign right of the nation and the threat or use of force as a means of settling international disputes. To accomplish the aim of the preceding paragraph, land, sea, and air forces, as well as other war potential, will never be maintained. The right of belligerency of the state will not be recognized. National humiliation blamed wartime militarism and authoritarianism, easing the transition to a democratic government.
Modern demonization of monarchies stems from the prevalence of democracy which abhors absolute power. Some of the most successful states have been the European constitutional monarchies — Great Britain, Sweden, the Netherlands, Belgium, and Norway. The royal capstone of the State contributed to stability and honoring history to enhance identity while preserving a key component of civilization.
Elsewhere, the Bolshevik regicide of Nicholas II and the Chinese Communist humiliation of China’s last emperor made the return to monarchy impossible and facilitated the transformation of their civilizations into states. The war leadership of Wilhelm II led to German renunciation of any future king and facilitated the invention of the ill-fated Weimar Republic — terminated by the Nazi state. No such renunciation of the Showa emperor occurred, although numerous scholars have documented his personal involvement in wartime decisions.
A monarchy is more visible as a symbol of civilization than are aristocracies or churches, and its elimination rarely produces the desired results. The American renunciation of the British Crown was an exception — desirable at the time and it proved to be a rational move to transform a collection of colonies into a sovereign state while preserving much of the civilization inherited from the mother country.
Japan has been an incomplete state with its weak defense force, and though the economic boom nurtured sentiments that greater sovereignty could be attained, the collapse of the roaring economy in the 1990s punctured that dream. The rise of China and continued friction further narrows available choices.
## State-Building in Japan
Before the state, there was civilization, although the Mongol empire was an important exception. Prior to civilization, there was some form of society.
The earliest migrants to the Japanese archipelago constructed boats to cross the various straits and had to have more than primitive technology to accomplish this. Hunting and gathering was their main form of activity until the Neolithic introduction of agriculture, with rice as the main staple grain. Family, clan, and tribe formed primitive society, followed by local kingdoms. As the population grew, conflicts occurred more frequently. A legal code and sovereign emperor brought cultural and political unity as the ruling aristocracy took control of the government.
With population growth, the Economic Subsystem expanded trade networks not only on the main islands but also with outlying isles, Korea, and China. The division of labor became more complex with more specialization and technical skills. Japanese statebuilding since medieval times has followed a unique pattern, in large part because of its detachment from, yet nearness enough to, the mainland. This propinquity enabled borrowing and adaptation of Chinese culture and institutions and still allowed Japan to remain separate, unlike Korea, which remained closer and vulnerable to China. An island society never occupied by a foreign power until 1945, its formidable pelagic frontiers enabled the evolution of a unique civilization.
State evolution was contained within island frontiers. Links with the continent remained continuous, with trade, cultural borrowing, and occasional military involvement. Civilization changes generally originated from above. The emperor personified continuity, while an aristocracy and military leadership exercised discretion and control over society. No social revolution occurred to change the direction or character of the state as in Russia, China, or France. In the late nineteenth century, the Japanese aristocracy, aware of China’s decline and subordination to Western pressures, undertook a revolution from above and transformed the state and society into an efficient and modern form.
The Japanese possessed and practiced a religion that was not transferable to other societies. Although Buddhist monks and priests had periodically exercised economic and political influence, the native Shinto faith was rarely attacked and persecuted. The effect was to preserve the transcendent subsystem of Japanese civilization while altering the economic, government, and knowledge subsystems.
In other words, Meiji modernization did not demand secularization to succeed; rather, it created an imperial cult with religious characteristics.
Militarization of the state in the prewar period was a form of limited rational secularization within the Meiji-constructed Transcendent Subsystem. The application of military force against Russia, China, Korea, and Southeast Asia from 1894 through 1945 was overt rationality in the sense that it sought and resulted in the achievement of territorial goals against weaker antagonists. The 1941 attack on America in the Pacific was intended to buy time to consolidate the empire. The goal of territorial and economic expansion created the “Japanese Co-Prosperity Sphere”— a variation of “Manifest Destiny” on the other side of the Pacific.
Aside from the rational aspect of military expansion, an element of bushido, the warrior code, was present. It was spiritual and demanded courage, loyalty, and death for one’s lord or emperor. The notion of a “divine mission” was explicit in wartime ideology. After the devastating war, Japan was fortunate to escape the fate of Germany, divided among four powers. Also, proximity to Korea was a significant economic stimulus during the UN war against North Korea.
State evolution occurred in the modern period by fusing government, economy, and knowledge into a corporate unity, resulting in the isolation of transcendence. Japan’s modernization was an outlier in that the emperor cult was linked to Shinto and maintained a vital link between state and transcendence that remains today, albeit in more subliminal than explicit forms. Social cohesion is reinforced by festivals, rituals, language, family, education, and language as well as the emperor as a continuous and implicit reference point of Japanese identity and common vulnerability to natural disasters.
Japan is a case study of how civilization and its subsystems interact to meet the challenges of global and domestic change. The formula of “adopt, adapt, adept” applies with fair accuracy to Japan’s derivative civilization while recognizing its separate character. The same process occurred with late nineteenth-century modernization; Japan adopted Western forms, to avoid the spectacle of Chinese civilization collapsing, while retaining the emperor as the embodiment of the state.
Japanese social cohesion has a distinctly Confucian quality, with a powerful family loyalty element distinctive not only in bloodline but also extended to political party factions (oyabun-kobun = parent-child) and an emperor as parent-figure. Prior to his defeat in World War Two, the emperor was more of a strict and distant father figure, transformed into more of a softer mother figure after the war.
School classmates and entrants of a corporate or bureaucratic cohort exhibit similar mutual allegiance.
The expression ganbatte (頑張って) links the speaker and the target individual into the same team. The same loyalties also result in fierce competition among groups and the motivation to do one’s best for the group. Since the Meiji period, the imperial system provided a national umbrella to contain these groups into a cohesive unity.
Post-war reforms diminished the imperial mystique and released previously repressed class conflicts. American occupation and reforms created an updated state that emphasized democracy, a multi-party election system, and an economy linked to and dependent on access to U.S. markets. There was also the normalization of the monarchy by denying links to divinity.
A recent book by Professor Satoshi Machidori3 summarized how the state has adapted to global and domestic challenges. The Japanese "Miracle" was once touted as the wave of the future with high economic growth and export-driven re-industrialization. Despite limited natural resources and massive destruction in World War Two, industrial policy with MITI (the Ministry of International Trade and Industry) in command delivered prosperity under the leadership of the Liberal Democratic Party. Chalmers Johnson’s MITI and the Japanese Miracle: The Growth of Industrial Policy, 1925-19754 analyzed the major sources of economic policy, while other scholars found cultural and psychological themes contributing to Japanese economic success. A few even considered a coming second war with America over global hegemony.5
## Speculative Bubble Burst
During the 1990s, because of overconfidence and arrogance, Japan allowed a speculative bubble to develop in its domestic economy. The spiral upward in prices was based primarily on overinvestment in productive capacity and unrestricted bank lending using inflated real estate prices as collateral. When the inevitable collapse came, instead of reforming its banking practices and holding its companies responsible for bad investment decisions, Japan exported the bubble to South Korea and Southeast Asia.6
Events drove a stake through the heart of Japan’s climb to global leadership. First was the bursting of the economic bubble, in which the trade surplus of the high-growth period led to trade friction with the U.S. and other industrial countries. Yen appreciation and dollar depreciation led to recession, but not before causing a heady cultural confidence that undermined fiscal restraint. Many Japanese became intoxicated by its booming economy.

Major changes were occurring in the Communist world with their failure to reform and these affected Japanese stock prices, which began a descent at the start of 1990. Prices had soared and no longer reflected real values as non-performing loans dragged the economy down.
The high-growth period collapsed around the same time as the Soviet Union dissolved. Both the Japanese and Communist models of development showed fatal flaws. Japan’s policy of picking winners and Communist dogmatic central planning proved inferior to market rationality and were modified or abandoned. Over the next quarter century Japan sought to restore economic viability through long-postponed reforms.
Restructuring the central banking system was a priority. A central bank’s role is to oversee the system of banking and maintain the financial system’s overall stability. As a lender of last resort, it maintains the credit system. The Bank of Japan was established in 1882 to function as the government bank. During the “Bubble,” low interest rates and monetary easing combined with deregulation and investment in new products and construction. A stronger relationship emerged between the Ministry of Finance and the financial system. More subordinate to the ministry, the Bank of Japan was unable to operate independently and set interest rates to regulate the money supply. Other industrial countries faced similar challenges, and a Central Bank Study Group proposed a closer relationship between the elected government and the Ministry of Finance. The Bank of Japan Act (in effect April 1, 1998) strengthened the bank’s independence in monetary policy.
Professor Machidori provides a detailed description and analysis of how the Japanese establishment responded to domestic and external changes and challenges. The end of Liberal Democratic Party dominance was a key factor in breaking the political logjam. The postwar election system, with its multi-member districts, created situations where members of the same party ran against each other. This created incentives for individual candidates to build up organizations, placing greater emphasis on the individual over the party. Koenkai (後援会, lit. "local support groups") became personal fiefdoms requiring financial support for activities and often depended on interest groups.
Under Liberal Democratic Party dominance, personal factions also flourished, and cabinet positions were doled out as rewards. Small reforms of the electoral system were overshadowed by strengthening cabinet functions and a reorganization of ministries. Machidori refers to the changes as a form of constitutional revision, noting that the actual amendment of the American-written constitution has proven to be difficult.
Article 96 requires that any revision must gain the approval of two-thirds of legislators from both houses of the Diet and then approval by a majority in a national referendum.
The prospect of revision has seemed so distant that a national law specifying how to hold a referendum was not considered until the early twenty-first century. The first such law passed the Diet in 2007 and was revised in 2014.7
Another reform was to reduce the size of government by capping the number of civil servants, even as the proportion of government workers (8.6%) was below that of other industrialized states. A 1969 law capped the number of civil servants, despite the expansion of the state’s role in society.
Neoliberalism called for autonomy of the economy from the government, and major public corporations were privatized, including Japan National Railways, Nippon Telegraph and Telephone, and the Japan Tobacco and Salt Public Corporation. Personnel cuts and organizational contraction were prominent in Japan’s administrative reform.
Japan undertook legal and administrative reforms in the face of economic and political challenges. Moves to greater central control and the strengthening of the chief executive at the expense of the legislature and bureaucracy had parallels in other industrial countries. Some reforms came about through opposition party demands, internal studies, and criticisms by Japanese intellectuals. In large part, these accommodations to new realities were evolutionary and did not lead to the polarization more characteristic of the U.S. for example.
One can admire how consensus and recognition of national limitations enabled gradual reforms without creating a host of further problems.8
On national defense, perceptions have changed since the demise of the Soviet Union. A new national security strategy has emerged, recognizing growing threats in the region. The former Self-Defense Agency was elevated to the Ministry of Defense in 2006 and former restrictions on military budget and activities were modified. China’s rise and her closer relations with Russia are new dangers that force Japan into a reconsideration of the previous minimalist approach to security, as America faces numerous new challenges on a global front.
## Conclusion
The Japanese state has jettisoned or modified institutions that were successful during the Cold War but were inadequate when facing global transformation.

No social revolution has occurred to shake the foundations of the state or civilization in Japan, and an attitude of permanent pragmatism combined with adherence to cultural values and life security has enabled the nation to weather the crises of the present century.
Regarding the Civilization/State Theory, Japanese culture has retained some measure of traditional transcendence, with modified Confucian attitudes retained in family and social values. The unique imperial cult of the prewar period, as a vital transcendent component of the state, was eliminated by the American occupation and was replaced by a new spiritual immanence that preserves the native and continuous piety towards nature inherent to the Japanese while simultaneously cultivating subtle individualism.
Thus, the state, as expressed in government, economy, and knowledge, retains a recognizable noninterference approach to the postwar shattering of transcendence as expressed in religious persuasion, cultural continuity, and hierarchy. While the emperor no longer claims semi-divinity, the monarchy retains some charisma to preserve Japanese civilization and serve as a source of common identity.
Secularization is the removal of religious transcendence from a society or civilization. It shifts man’s focus from the sacred to himself. It places man at the center of the universe and leads to individual self-worship.
The irony of modern civilization is that the Transcendent Subsystem has been a source of energy, producing material discoveries and human solidarity. Its energizing success in building modern knowledge-based, democratic, and prosperous civilization may have rendered its future role superfluous. It provided the basis for fusing the material subsystems of government, economy, and rational knowledge into the post-modern state where transcendent creativity is diminished. But as the Japanese phenomenon suggests, the transformation of transcendence into individual immanence can preserve human spirituality without inflating egos into solipsistic units craving anarchy.
What will be the nature of the post-modern state? With diminished or absent transcendence, it will lack a sense of purpose except to improve material benefits in the form of longer and happier lives for the global population. This is a noble goal, to be sure, but it will not be conducive to human freedom, which is the source of the creative spirit. Societies will be ruled by the post-modern state, and the only remaining standard will be a “Permanent Pragmatism.”
This contains no vision and no higher meaning than global utilitarianism. It places problem-solving at the center of human activity. Whether the future of humanity is to be the closed society, as described in Plato’s Republic or Huxley’s Brave New World, or an open society as aspired to by the American founders, it certainly will be defined by the system and subsystem interactions of civilization and state.
