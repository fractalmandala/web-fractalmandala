The name Ethiopia is associated in the Book of Genesis with the creation of the world, indicating a sacred and ancient heritage. We find Ethiopia mentioned as having relations with the ancient Biblical nations such as Israel, Egypt, Assyria, and Mesopotamia. Ethiopians get uplifting gratification in reading specific scriptural references from the Bible and applying them to the country. These Biblical allusions include discussion of the Queen of Sheba and her son, King Menelik the First, born from her relationship with King Solomon, who was himself the son of King David, the founder from whom came the lineal parentage of Jesus Christ. Ethiopians also refer to Moses, whose wife is mentioned in the Bible as an Ethiopian lady.
If we accept the Bible as undisputed authority, and its historical facts as correct, we cannot dispute the references mentioning Ethiopia and Ethiopian personalities in both the Old and New Testament. In addition to Moses’s wife, we find another Ethiopian in the Bible. This was Ebed Melech (Gebre Nigus, a common Ethiopic proper noun meaning King’s Servant); yet another was the Ethiopian Eunuch mentioned in the New Testament. In this case, what is Biblically correct, and accepted in reference to Egypt, Israel, and other Middle Eastern nations, is also right, correct, and accepted in reference to Ethiopia.
Outside the Bible, whose theme provides more a religious than a secular foundation for examining objective facts, Ethiopia has its own political and economic capacities and capabilities; it has been an interlocuter with other major powerful civilizations. The contents of its own civilization have inspired classical historians such as Homer, Herodotus, Plutarch, and modern historians and discoverers such as Edward Gibbons, Bruce James, Arnold Toynbee, and Edward Ullendorff to write of how Ethiopia was engaged over lengthy periods of time in diplomatic and trade relations with such mighty and far-flung civilizations as Egypt, Persia, Assyria, India, Greece, and Rome.
The name Ethiopia in geographical terminology refers to the region south of Egypt extending south up to Lake Victoria, the entire Horn of Africa, and Western Arabia. Historical epochs such as the rise of the Roman Empire, the expanse of the Dark Ages, and the revolutionary flame of Islam, each saw the complete and violent destruction of previous civilizations. Similarly, each saw the rearranging of the region known as Ethiopia.
However, cultural, religious, political, and historical ideations that prevailed in their ancient and Biblical characteristics are still alive in today’s Ethiopia. Some of these ancient values from the Biblical, classical, and modern epochs remain obvious today: language, religious rites, alphabet scripts, and manners of worship and iconoclastic remains. It is possible to state that Ethiopia, as the origin of man, may be the only country in the world that has stayed unchanged in its historical, religious, cultural, and linguistic traditions.
One European scholar gives support to the above statement:
In Abyssinia, the Mosaic law, the feudal system, and the most modern ideas jostle each other throughout, and the introduction of the new does not appear to displace, but to exist side by side with the old in the life of the nation, which, to quote a recent writer is ‘young today, though it was powerful when the Book of Genesis was written, and we Christians when our own ancestors still worshiped Thor and Odin’1
In Ethiopian studies, scholars emphasize modern Ethiopia as viewed from the vantage points of the 19th century to today. Events that occurred after the Second World War occupy great space in Ethiopian affairs.
This article redefines Ethiopia by taking into consideration the term, Ethiopia, its geographical extent, and limits. Ethiopia is here defined wholistically in its ancient historical, biblical, classical, and modern attributes. Ethiopia’s geographic span, its history, and its ethno-linguistic features are all evidenced in the common thread of linguistic, religious, and cultural experiences of Ethiopians, Nubians, and Eritreans today; they form the body of analytical explanations of Ethiopia’s ancient glory and rekindle the urgency for its recovery.
## Classic Ethiopia
The trajectories of global affairs and the march of history are like sunrise and sunset. Nations rise as the morning sun at their birth; and they make their unique journey enroute to their terminal sunset of decay or regeneration. Throughout their journey, they exhibit variations of temperance and resiliency of character and reach or fail to reach contemporaneous standards of achievements. Ethiopia provides an example of ascendant greatness in ancient times and inglorious decay today. It awaits recovery and resurrection.
Ethiopia is ancient in time, and rich in mythical and factual history.

## Ethiopian Christianity in Wholistic Perspective
A cogent argument can be made that Ethiopian Christianity is inseparably linked with the governing system of Ethiopian monarchism. The Orthodox Monophysite belief was defined by the Ethiopian Diocese, a junior partner to the Alexandrian Papacy since King Ezana declared Christianity in 330 C. E. The coronation of Emperor Ezana by Abuna Anastasios, a prelate sent by Pope Cyril of Alexanderia, created a permanent close link between the king and the state. The king was identified as being enthroned by the Heavenly Ruler of the country, meaning God. God is the ruler of the country, and the king is his delegate and representative on earth.
The concept of a divine ruler, common in European kingdoms, prevailed also in Ethiopia. The Ethiopian version was a bit different since it fused the throne and the state in a theocratic blend of equal measurements — superiority in the sacred and the secular realms of authority.
The concept was also one of a balance of power, with the religious and state authorities operating on their demarcated authority, plus the equivalent of a third branch, holding the mandate of judicial review, paralleling the federalist version of government arising in the West from Montesquieu.
State and church occupying equal positions in the political and spiritual affairs of Ethiopia made the Ethiopian state a quasi-theocratic one. A symbiotic relationship of mutual and harmonious application of administrative governance and spiritual presence permeated Ethiopian society. Edward Ullendorff writes how this blend of church and state was an integral aspect of Ethiopian civilization and heritage. He stated:
One has to consider such deeply rooted traditions — many of them reflected in the legend of the Queen of Sheba in all its manifold ramifications — as the Aaronite origin of the Aksumite clergy, the references to Abyssinians as the dekika Esrael (Children of Israel), and the consciousness of having inherited from Israel the legitimate claim to being regarded as the chosen people of God.
It is clear that these and other traditions, in particularly that of the Ark of the Covenant at Aksum, must have been an integral part of the Abyssinian national heritage long before the introduction of Christianity; for it would be inconceivable that a people recently converted to Christianity should thereafter have begun to boast of Jewish descent and to insist on Israelite connection, customs and institutions (Ullendorff, E. 1976:96).
The Ethiopian state as an empire has categorical phases beginning with the Queen of Sheba as ruler after her father’s death. The book, Tradition of Hazega and Zazega, also seen as the Book of Axum, narrates Ethiopia as being ruled by an allegorically typecast gargantuan bone-crushing boa constrictor snake. As was common in the Middle Ages, and typically practiced around ecclesiastical readings, the oppressive boa constrictor allusion probably implies a tyrant who rules as dreadfully and wicked as a snake.
The possible meaning here is that there was an age of the tyrannical ruler, one symbolized as a boa constrictor, and that this age may have been contemporary with the era of the ancient Egyptian pharaohs. Clearly, the vicinity of Asmara and the Barka Lowlands of Eritrea exhibit traces of an ancient civilization, one now awaiting further exploration and the academic corroboration of its links to regional and cross border civilizations.
Carlo Conti Rossini states that the history of Ethiopia was contemporary to Pharaonic Egypt. The reign of the Queen of Sheba is a category in the vast history of the country when a seamless unity of the Semitic tribes prevailed. It crisscrossed the frontiers between the Red Sea and the eastern coast of Africa. The region thrived in trade, cultural contents and international affairs, and Ethiopia was the celebrated empire of vast wealth to export to Asia and, later, to Byzantium Europe.
At its highest level of civilization, Ethiopia was deeply grounded in the Bible. The Ethiopian Orthodox Tewahdo Church was a partner of the Ethiopian state. For many centuries before 1270 C. E., the Ethiopian Emperor occupied a special office in the highest organizational structure of the church. Ethiopian emperors were ordained royal priests. They were scholarly authors and theologians, and they participated in conducting sacrament services. The overthrow of the Zaguwe Dynasty in 1270 by Yukuno Amlak, a head of an insurgent tribe, led to his crowning himself as an emperor; thus ended the prince-priest role until it was resurrected by Yukuno Amlak’s great-great grandson, Emperor ZeraYaqob (1434-68).

## The First Modern Revolution of Ethiopian History
When Emperor Ezana conquered Arabia, and Nubia, the Ethiopian Empire was robust and mighty. The Kingdom of D’AmaEt / D ’mt, from its location in southcentral and central Eritrea, ruled as far as the coasts of Somalia and farther, as far as Ceylon and India. The renowned king of the time was Zoscales. He was known for his diplomatic skills, military valor, and craftiness in amassing wealth. He institutionalized a wellestablished system of justice and governance for subsequent rulers, such as Emperor Ezana. The port of Adulis served as the entrepot for rich trade coming from the bounties of hinterlands in Eritrea, Western Tigray, Begemidir and as far as the Gojam borders with Sudan.
The revolution of 1270 was masterminded by Yukuno Amlak, who sought to overthrow the Zaguwe Dynasty. Yukuno Amlak repudiated the ethnic origin of the Zaguwe Dynasty due to their Jewish ancestry. It did not matter to Yukuno Amlak that they were genuine upholders of the Monophysite Orthodox faith.
The reasons given by those who justify the usurpation and those who condemn it as a serious transgression of ecclesiastical and royal edicts each hold obvious merits.
The northern region of Ethiopia was a well-trodden path for ancient evangelists and hermits; some of these hailed from North Africa and were deeply attached to the Zaguwe Dynasty. They resisted the overthrow of the Zaguwe Dynasty king. They based their resistance on the Biblical scripture found in the First Book of Samuel, Chapter 26, Verse 9. They protested, arguing that the killing of a king was a breach of the long-held scriptural tradition stating: “Who can put forth his hand against Yahweh’s anointed and be guiltless?”
Kings in Ethiopia who were unable to fulfill their royal duties were permitted to retreat into monasteries or holy places of their choice. Yukuno Amlak violated this tradition when he murdered Emperor Ytbarek at the Saint Chiriqos temple and inside the holy of holies of the temple, where only ordained priests or ordained kings were permitted to enter.
The overthrow of the Zaguwe Dynasty kindled a new revolution, establishing what came to be known as the Solomonic Dynasty. The overthrow of the last Zaguwe ruler, King Ytbarek, was followed by tumultuous years. Yukuno Amlak, who had crowned himself as the first of the Solomonic line of kings, was confronted by massive opposition in the modern region known as Eritrea and Tigray. His rule ended in violence and unrest, but his son, King AgbaA Zion (Redeemer of Vanquished Zion) was able to restore stability by heavy-handed subjugation of the northern region.
Ethiopian studies have tended to be heavily influenced by pressures not to offend Ethiopian rulers. It is not uncommon to read how Ethiopian and international scholars, eager not to antagonize rulers, downplayed the violence and massive human rights abuses that had occurred under their reigns.
Moreover, the Ethiopian state was ethnicized and reduced to a provincial entity under the Amhara domination. Since then, ethnic identity has superseded Ethiopian identity.
## The Menace of Islam and the Death of Ethiopic Civilization
The Islamic invasion of Egypt in 641 C. E. transformed Egyptian Christianity. The destruction of Egypt — one of the preeminent Christian nations, with its ancient Biblical history — at the hands of Islamic conquerors was catastrophic.
Spencer Trimingham, lamenting the calamity visited by the Muslims on Egyptian Christianity and its ripple effects to Ethiopia and Africa, stated:
Christian Africa was abandoned to its fate. Egypt remained a Christian country for a long time with the Muslims as the dominant ruling minority, but through the machinery of the state the church was slowly strangled and more and more of her peoples were absorbed into the new religion, until in the course of time Egypt became a Muslim state with the Christians the subject minority, as they remain to this day.
Strangled by the isolation caused through the rise of the Islamic Empire, Axum [the Ethiopian Empire] relaxed its grip upon the physical environment and allowed itself to be cut off economically and culturally from its connections with the outside world by the pressure of the Beja tribes; and the ruins of great temples and public buildings point the contrast between its splendid past and the sordid future which it was to experience.11
The Beja tribes occupying northeastern Sudan — extending to the Red Sea coasts of northern Ethiopia, today’s Eritrea — were converted to Islam through the ardent teachings of Muslim clerics from Egypt. The Caliph Omar, iron-willed and oppressive in all manners of military conquest, secured control of captured lands, and he extracted extortionist resources from lands he conquered, unleashing dread and death against Christian Egypt.
Thus, the erstwhile Christian Beja in southeastern Egypt were Islamized. They served as wolfpack warriors exterminating all societies on their path who refused to convert and to accept the faith of Islam. These were descendants of pharaonic Egypt. Their lives had been upended by foreign invaders such as the Greeks, Persians, Romans, and the Arab conquerors of Egypt.
So, they abandoned Christianity and converted to Islam. The fervency of their Islamic faith and their intuitive skills as warriors enabled them to occupy the heartland of the ancient Axumite Empire in northern Ethiopia, modern Eritrea. They served as proxy warriors to the successive rulers of Egypt, and they caused the utter destruction of the ancient Ethiopian Empire of Axum.
As a sister diocese to the Ethiopian Orthodox Tewahdo Church, Ethiopian emperors took responsibility to defend their fellow Christians in Egypt. Emperor AmdeZion (Pillar of Zion) redressed Yukuno Amlak’s flagrant violence against his adversaries who did not approve of the way he violated Ethiopian tradition forbidding the killing or assassination of the monarch.

## Islam in a Wholistic Global and Regional Perspective
The era of exploration, the avaricious and temperamentally violent Arab slave trade, resulting in Europeans joining the slave trade, brought devastation and irreparable ruin to Ethiopia’s civilization, religion, system of government and social fabric. After the 1270 revolution overthrowing the Zaguwe Dynasty, and the putative “restoring” of the Solomonic Dynasty, Ethiopian rulers were not able to re-establish Ethiopian heritage or to bring its territorial integrity to its ancient glory. The first reason for this was the Islamic advances, which accelerated at the heights of the Ottoman Empire in the 1520s as the Ottoman Turks were pushing to advance Islam in Africa.
Since the eighth century C. Entrepreneurial jihad was the method by which Muslim merchants from North Africa scattered throughout Africa. The practice of combining religious proselyting, trade in commodities, and slavery continued to embattle Ethiopian rulers whose territory was specifically targeted for the combined campaigns of advancing Islamic teaching and Arab slave trading.
The second struggle for Ethiopian rulers to re-engage in national and state building, enough to restore Ethiopia to its ancient glories, was the jihad climax exploding in the heart of the Ethiopia Empire. A warrior by the name Ahmad Ibn Ibrahim Gragn, originating from the Somali borders, and armed and equipped by Turkey with superior weapons, waged a campaign of jihad in 1529 to expand Islam in Ethiopia and Africa.
The war was violent and almost conquered the entire country, converting many people living in eastern and central Ethiopia. Ethiopian society felt the brunt of the war as many Ethiopians were forcefully converted to Islam. Eventually, the Portuguese sent a contingent of volunteers to defend Ethiopia. Portuguese soldiers, numbering four hundred, and under the leadership of Christopher Da Gama, landed in the port of Massawa, Eritrea in 1541.
In subsequent battles, they defeated the Islamic armies, killed Mohammed Gragn, and liberated Ethiopia from the brink of complete Islamization. Referring to the onslaught of the Islamic campaign, Edward Ullendorff stated:
The Holocaust enveloped most parts of Ethiopia and brought in its train misery and murder, ruin, and devastation. Much of the literary and intellectual heritage of Abyssinia was irretrievably lost, and barbarism and brutality had an effect far transcending that age.
For the Ethiopians, a good deal of their hard-won civilization was destroyed, while to the historian and ethiopisant precious documentation and irreplaceable evidence perished forever (Ullendorff, p. 70).
The Islamic wars’ lasting impact was the complete destruction of the ancient Axumite Empire. The Ethiopian state was crippled as its sea access fell under Turkish agents controlling the Red Sea littoral coast. The region was under Ethiopian rule after Emperor Madezion chased out Arab aggressors in 1284 C. E, but monasteries, educational institutions, churches, and their treasures were destroyed and looted. The invaders had relished massacring and razing villages as they pushed their advances throughout the entire country. They targeted the killings of priests, monks, nuns, and lay believers. Before he was killed, Mohammed Gragn was reminded by his own followers and warned of the covenant of the Prophet Mohammed advising his followers never to wage jihad against Ethiopia.
Ethiopia’s ancient religious and political institutions had become isolated. In similar patterns of uprooting cultural and historical treasures that were destroyed forever when the Beja invasion of northern Ethiopia and Eritrea had occurred in the seventh and the eighth century, equally damaging cataclysmic destruction took place because of the Gragn invasion of 1529.

They were unstoppable in their kinetic outbursts of conquest and expropriation of settled societies in the southern part of Ethiopia. They set the patterns of the current tendencies for the disintegration of the Ethiopian state system. The current wars of Tigray vs. Amhara, Amhara vs. Oromo and other regions of the country can all be traced to the post-Mohammed Gragn invasion by the Oromos.
To defend the country from complete disintegration, several war lords from the provincial regions of the country competed to crown a prince from their respective regions. This led to the Era of the Judges, starting in 1769, and ending with the reign of Emperor Tewodros in 1855. The term the Era of the Judges was coined by Ethiopian clergy recollecting Israel’s anarchic situation during the period narrated in the Book of the Judges.
The Era of the Judges brought further ruin and disarray upon all sectors of society and national institutions. In 1855, a war lord from northwestern Ethiopia by the name of Kasa Hailu, whose throne name he chose as Tewodros (Theodore), rose up inspired by fervent zeal for the restoration of Ethiopia’s faith and state unity. He succeeded in subduing the competing princes and crowned himself Emperor Tewodros II of a united Ethiopia.
Emperor Tewodros was enthusiastic, inspired by a conviction of Ethiopia’s mandate to hoist the banners of Christianity and liberate Jerusalem from the domination of Islam. His attempt at modernization, his reckless diplomatic relations, and his cruel manners overshadowed his fervent intentions and undercut his visionary grandiose idea of himself as a global leader in the war against Islam. Emperor Tewodros succeeded in uniting Ethiopia by subduing regional governors. However, he failed to make the unity of the country a platform by which to institutionalize a lasting state system, one with prospects for permanently restoring Ethiopia’s glory to the pre-1270 golden ages.

## European Colonialism in Ethiopia
The last decade of the 19th century was the climax of the contentious bargaining of European powers attempting to grab colonial lands left unclaimed in previous decades. Ethiopia was targeted by Italy and France, with Great Britain acting as a balancing power. Emperor Yohannes sought to establish diplomatic relations with these European powers, with domestic adversaries and with Ethiopia’s neighbors, Sudan, and Egypt.
In Sudan, the Mahdiya forces under the leadership of Muhammed Ahmed Abdullah, mounted repeated acts of aggression against Ethiopia’s western and northern fronts. Emperor Yohannes IV was determined not to surrender an inch of Ethiopian territory to any of these countries. He saw himself as an inheritor of Ethiopia’s ancient rulers whose vision of Ethiopia’s glory would not permit breaching and transgressing its “sacred” borders by outsiders (Ghebre Selassie, Z. 2006: 84).

## Diplomatic Betrayals and Domestic Sellouts
Domestic discord proved even more detrimental to Emperor Yohannes’s struggle to defend Ethiopia’s territorial integrity from external aggressors. His tolerant, inclusive, and genuine ambitions for a united Ethiopia faced roadblocks from a rival claimant of the throne, Prince Shale Mariam, who later, after the martyrdom of Emperor Yohannes, was crowned as Menelik II; he was one whose ambition for power overcame his patriotic and trustworthy sensibilities. As Emperor Yohannes was battling against domestic, regional, and international aggressions against Ethiopia, Sahle Mariam (Menelik II) was secretly negotiating with Italy. He promised Italy to refrain from cooperating with Yohannes as they battled Ethiopia’s defense forces on the Red Sea coast and northern Ethiopia (Marcus, H. 1994: 125).
Menelik’s covert collaboration with Italy to secure modern weapons, which he used to fight against Emperor Yohannes IV, in cooperation with Italy, Egypt and Sudan, was uncharacteristic; thus, only a few would-be national leaders would subscribe. Ethiopia’s adversaries were determined to attack Ethiopia from all corners of its borders, making it difficult for Emperor Yohannes to sustain Ethiopian unity and border security. Harold Marcus states that the entire Ethiopian population, including church authorities and the vast clergy from King Menelik II’s dominion, had profound respect for Emperor Yohannes. They even tried to persuade King Menelik to support the emperor. Menelik II was sold on the Italian plan to attack Ethiopia expecting that once Emperor Yohannes was defeated, he would then occupy the Ethiopian throne.

## World Wars, Decolonization and Transition to Post-Colonial Sovereignty of Africa
The Post-Industrial Revolution accelerated and intensified colonial adventures. As the race for colonies increased, there were conflicts on diplomatic protocols and agreements among European powers that were reflected in the Napoleonic and other wars.
Civil war in the United States brought to the forefront antislavery champions in Great Britain and the United States. The diplomatic trajectories of the time highlighted labor abuses by industrial capitalists, whose interests blended with the exploitative networks of global imperialism.
These developments became catalysts for advocacy for workers’ rights in Europe and liberation from colonial rule in the colonial territories. Human rights abuses in European colonies, and massive exploitation of colonial territories climaxed in the First and Second World Wars. What Europeans cultivated and sowed in their home turf, when they conspired and waged wars to outdo each other in the colonial scramble, came to fruition in bloodshed and destruction of European material and industrial civilizations.
Europeans waged bitter wars against each other mercilessly; and in the mayhem, they involved their colonies in the drama of killing and pillaging. Italy’s capacity to contribute to the war objectives of fascism as a member of the Axis powers was tried, but it failed with the defeat of the Axis powers at the hands of the Allies. Eritreans felt estranged, unwelcomed into the Ethiopian fold of the post Second World War years. They gradually oriented themselves towards searching for self-determination, independence, and sovereignty.
Despite all the diplomatic capital that Emperor Haile Selassie had gained globally, his betrayal of Eritrea became reinforcement of Menelik II’s betrayal of Eritrea when he abandoned it to Italy.
Emperor Haile Selassie gained global attention after the fascist invasion of Ethiopia in 1935. His diplomatic successes were moderate, but even more dismal was his lack of a uniting vision for Ethiopia’s future. His diplomatic insights were better than the dull and circumvented vision of his imagination. A popular uprising in 1974 overthrew Emperor Haile Selassie. Then, junior military officers, under the leadership of Major Mengistu Haile Mariam, hijacked the 1974 Ethiopian popular revolution. They imprisoned the emperor and his family, and he was killed by the Dergue regime in 1975.
The overthrow of the Ethiopian monarch and the introduction of Marxism occurred together. The Dergue rule introduced a communist ideology totally alien to Ethiopia’s ancient heritage of faith, culture, and political tradition.
Marxism as an ideology, looked at from the perspectives of Ethiopia’s ancient Biblical civilization, would be seen as the last ideology possible to find access in the Ethiopian political ethos. It is, thus, not surprising that Marxism in Ethiopia degenerated into an ideological virus with deadly consequences. The Red Terror conducted by the Dergue Communist regime brought violence where hundreds of thousands, mostly the educated and professionals, were killed.
To prolong their rule, the Dergue regime used ethnic identity as a means of divide and rule. Finally, the Dergue was overthrown in 1991. The Ethiopian Peoples’ Revolutionary Democratic Front (EPRDF) replaced the Dergue. The PRDF under the leadership of Meles Zenawi, introduced a federal system like that of India and Canada.
Meles Zenawi’s government wrote a transitional Charter, conducted snap elections for a constituent assembly, and scheduled a national election in 1994 to inaugurate a Federal Democratic Republic of Ethiopia. EPRDF then introduced “developmental democracy,” a blend of strong-arm dictatorship and liberal economic policy pursuits. They introduced federalism by drawing regional borders based on ethnic identities. Post-World War Ethiopia can be said to have experienced modernization endeavors that have had the effect of distancing the country away from its ancient heritage.
A protracted guerrilla warfare for Eritrean independence started in 1961 and materialized in liberating Eritrea from Ethiopia in 1991. A referendum for independence was held in 1993; the outcome was a decision for complete separation from Ethiopia, with 98 percent favoring full sovereignty for Eritrea.
## Conclusion
For nations to recover their glorious history, they need leadership. Leadership vision and the willingness to undertake the task of nation-building are critical. Ethiopia’s long history and vast territories have regions where many ethnic, linguistic, and religious groups can be found. The task of creating an inspirational and uniting political theme to inspire all Ethiopians to accept Ethiopian citizenship, instead of prioritizing ethnic and religious identities, is the task of leadership willing to carry the burden of nationbuilding.
In the 1960s, Cold War conflicts led to competition for global influence between Marxism and Liberalism as distinct and contrasting ideologies. Ethiopia suffered serious riots and violent demonstrations on ideological grounds.
After months of demonstrations, those who saw Ethiopia as an ancient and feudal imperial state called for the overthrow of Emperor Haile Selassie’s rule. A military junta known as the Dergue (Committee) took control of the government. Mass slaughter followed.
The task of creating a healthy, sober, tolerant, and democratic nationalist feeling is a tall order for Ethiopian leaders today. Their temperaments, particularly as found among the Oromo and Amhara ethnic leaders, reeks of division, suspicion, and open hostility towards one another. The proverbial African saying “When two elephants fight, it is the grass that gets trampled” has been proven by how smaller ethnic groups, such as the Tigrayans and the Guragues, are used as scapegoats by the Amhara and the Oromo leaders.
The opportunity to embark on nation- and state-building exists. There exist also challenges that persistently expose the inability of Ethiopian national leaders to cast aside their provincial and ethnic approaches to governance. Events reflected in the last five years raise concern about the intractable ethnic division of the country.
There is a tradition of political culture in Ethiopia that can facilitate the task of nationand state-building. Ethiopian society long has approved of the rule of law, and it embraces conflict resolution by legal and peaceful methods. Scholars support such conclusions.
Referring to the Ethiopian legal system, Perham emphasized the following:
The system, for it well merits that name, showed two contrasting features. It was at once very democratic and highly centralized. It was democratic in that most Ethiopians, who are deeply interested in litigation, acted a vigorous part in it. Indeed, it was said that to play at litigation was a favorite amusement, while D’Abbadie credited the Ethiopians with remarkable critical facilities and a great knowledge of law and said that they regarded injustice as the worst of enemies.
It was liberal also in that natives and strangers had some choice as to their court and full right of appeal. These rights were, indeed, excessive, since it has been estimated that in some parts of the empire it was possible to take appeals through fifteen courts and much weight was given to whether the litigant received a favorable verdict from most of these courts.16
The above analysis highlights the rich history of ancient Ethiopia and how it evolved through the tempests of ancient events, playing a role in global relations on trade and diplomatic interests.

However, the rise of Islam in the seventh century C.E., the subsequent global turmoil and industrialization, colonialism, and post-colonial challenges in refocusing and reconstituting ancient instruments of governance, modified, shaped, and oriented ancient capacities. Ethiopia’s survival as the successor of ancient glory has dimmed.
Is the loss of Ethiopia’s ancient glory retrievable, or is it lost forever? Ethiopia and Eritrea entering the 21st century as separate entities, both sovereign and independent, would appear to indicate recovering Ethiopia’s ancient glory is unrealistic. Indeed, this will be unrealistic if the intellectual mindset of Ethiopian and Eritrean political elites is devoid of creative political thinking. Should the building of conciliatory institutions rooted and governed by the dictates of Natural Rights, and the establishment of standards of administration that are inspired by justice, equality and personal and public freedoms prevail, perhaps Ethiopia’s ancient glory will revive, like the mythical phoenix from the ashes. Possibly the cultural, religious, intellectual, and institutional values exist that will enable it to arise once again. Ethiopia’s predominant values have long been human-friendly and oriented to justice and rule of law.
Nations can regenerate and resurrect if they are in the hands, hearts, and minds of “nation-regarding”17 good leaders, with an awakened and aware citizenry. Throughout history, evidence abounds where nations passed through trials and tribulations to rise, recover, and progress. China, India, Brazil, Singapore, Korea, Taiwan, and Turkey once were members of the less developed countries; they are now approaching the standards of progress seen in the developed industrial states. Ethiopia’s glory awaits a glorious rise to shine once again.
