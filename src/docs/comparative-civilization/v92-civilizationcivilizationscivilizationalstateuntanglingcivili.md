“The truth is rarely pure and never simple.” —Oscar Wilde
Recently, a herd of terminological hybrids have trampled onto the field of International Relations Theory. These include civilizational state, civilization-state, statecivilization, civilizational nation-state, civilizational privilege, and geopolitical civilizational narrative. What do those notions tell us about global tendencies and trends today?
This paper explores the origin, evolution, and meaning of these terms; it also proposes solutions for problems of a nascent, multi-polar, and multi-civilizational world within the framework of an Integralistic Paradigm in IR Theory.
## 1. International Relations Theory and Its Discontents
Despite its distant roots in history, the academic topic of International Relations Theory is relatively new. Initially, it was dominated by two paradigms — Realism and Liberalism. Realists tend to focus on balance of power and political explanations of international relations, while Liberalists emphasize democracy, human rights, international trade, and peace.
Later, other perspectives emerged. These have included Economic Structuralism, Critical Theory, The English School, Constructivism, and Civilizationism. Economic Structuralism concentrates on economic issues, while Critical Theory challenges the status quo, but lacks a comprehensive conceptual framework. The English School is sometimes referred to as Liberal Realism since it attempts to combine those two approaches. Constructivists emphasize the power of reality-changing ideas while Civilizationists focus on histories and cultures in the face of nation-state survivalist instincts.
Each school of thought offers valuable insights, yet each falls short because of their one-sidedness. This results in a “different playbooks” dilemma, which current events in Eastern Europe, in the Middle East, and in the Far East clearly demonstrate.
## 2. The Civilizational Paradigm
The protean notion of civilization has been advanced by various political actors and their agendas for ages. As British political scholar Andrew Linklater notes, “Western and non-Western political forces have exploited the language of civilization to promote nationalist or statist objectives.”
For example, the Mission Civilisatrice (Civilizing Mission) was a justification used by the French, as well as other major powers of the time, as a political rationale for military intervention and colonization in the period between the fifteenth and the twentieth centuries.
The rapid rise of the Civilizational Paradigm within International Relations Theory consummated with the arrival of seminal works by the late American social scholar Samuel Huntington. He developed scientific fundamentals of the paradigm, including its principles and essential terminology. He wrote: “In the Cold War, countries related to the superpowers as allies, satellites, clients, neutrals, and nonaligned. In the postCold War world, countries relate to civilizations as member states, core states, lone countries, cleft countries, and torn countries.” (Huntington, 1996: 135).
According to Huntington, even though civilizations are essentially “cultures writ large,” they possess their own political structures. He elaborates:
… civilizations are cultural, not political entities. They do not, as such, maintain order, establish justice, collect taxes, fight wars, negotiate treaties, or do any of the other things which governments do.
The political composition of civilizations varies between civilizations and varies over time within a civilization. A civilization may thus contain one or many political units. Those units may be city states, empires, federations, confederations, nation states, multinational states, all of which may have varying forms of government.
As a civilization evolves, changes normally occur in the number and nature of its constituent political units. At one extreme, a civilization and a political entity may coincide. China, Lucian Pye has commented, is a “civilization pretending to be a state.” Japan is a civilization that is a state. Most civilizations, however, contain more than one state or other political entity. In the modern world, most civilizations contain two or more states. (Huntington, 1996: 44)
- 2.1. Civilizational State and International Relations Theory
- 2.1.1 The Civilizational State and Its Derivatives
Recently, a horde of odd “trojan horses” have swarmed onto the field of IR Theory. These have included: civilizational state, civilization-state, state-civilization, civilizational nation-state, civilizational privilege, and geopolitical civilizational narrative.
The archetypal prototype for those portmanteau terms is the notion of Civilizational State. Positioned at the intersection of political science, history, and cultural studies, the concept challenges the Westphalian state model which has dominated global politics since the Treaty of Westphalia in 1648.
The central idea in the concept of civilizational state is that a nation's governance, policies, and international interactions reflect its cultural and historical ethos. This idea underscores the difference between nation-states and those geopolitical entities that view themselves as bearers of civilizational missions. Thus, civilizational states perceive their role in global affairs as a manifestation of their historical legacies. The civilizational state notion is helping the elites of China, India, Russia, Turkey, Iran, Israel and others to position themselves in a nascent multipolar world. (Maçães, 2023). Self-declared civilizational states often resist Western-supplied norms of international law and governance, advocating for systems that reflect their own civilizational principles. The British political philosopher Christopher Coker warns that while civilizations (understood as cultures) themselves do not clash, civilizational states may do just that:
…while civilizations themselves may not clash, civilizational states appear to be set on challenging the rules of the international order that the West takes for granted. China seems anxious to revise them, Russia to break them, while Islamists would like to throw away the rule book altogether. … When seen in the round, these challenges could be enough to give birth to a new post-liberal international order. (Coker, 2019)
- 2.1.2 Theoretical Underpinnings
The civilizational state concept is rooted in the idea that states are not merely political entities but are also expressions of distinct civilizational heritages. This paradigm gains traction in contexts where some states assert their legitimacy by invoking historical, cultural, and civilizational continuities. Scholars have pointed to cases such as China, India, and Russia as exemplars, each claiming to embody millennia-old civilizational values.
The theoretical validity of the civilizational state concept lies in its capacity to offer an alternative lens for understanding global power dynamics. Unlike the universalist aspirations of liberal democracy or the ideological hegemony of Cold War-era paradigms, the civilizational state notion underscores the plurality of modernities. It insists that civilizations, not ideologies, are the primary actors in shaping world order. Proponents argue that this framework rectifies Eurocentric biases embedded in traditional IR theories, such as Realism and Liberal Institutionalism.
Critics, however, contend that the concept suffers from essentialism and risks reifying civilizations as monolithic and static entities. By framing states as civilizational actors, there is a tendency to overlook internal diversities, contestations, and the dynamic processes of cultural hybridization. Furthermore, critics question whether the civilizational state notion is more than a rhetorical device wielded for political purposes, particularly in regimes seeking to legitimize authoritarian governance or resist Western norms.
## 2.1.3 Practical Applications
The survivability of the civilizational state as a model is intricately tied to its ability to adapt to the demands of modern governance and international politics. On the domestic front, civilizational states often deploy a narrative of historical exceptionalism to consolidate national unity. For instance, China’s assertion of itself as a Confucian civilizational state underpins its domestic policies and foreign engagements, particularly through initiatives like the Belt and Road Initiative. Similarly, Russia’s emphasis on a Eurasian civilizational identity serves as a counterpoint to Western liberalism and NATO’s geopolitical expansion.
However, the operationalization of the concept of the civilizational state faces significant challenges.
Internally, the invocation of a singular civilizational identity often marginalizes minority groups and dissenting voices, thereby exacerbating social and political tensions. Externally, the civilizational state framework can lead to heightened geopolitical rivalry, as states claim exclusive spheres of influence based on historical or cultural affinity.
## 2.1.4 Global Implications
The global context in which the civilizational state concept operates is marked by an intensifying multipolarity. As power shifts away from a Western-dominated order, civilizational states find fertile ground to assert themselves. Yet, their survivability hinges on their ability to engage constructively with global norms while maintaining their unique identities.
Emerging global challenges, such as climate change, technological disruptions, and pandemics, require collaborative solutions that transcend civilizational divides. The rigid adherence to a civilizational state model risks fostering insularity and conflict, undermining efforts to address these shared threats. Conversely, a flexible interpretation of the model, which acknowledges inter-civilizational dialogue and cooperation, may enhance its legitimacy and resilience.
## 2.1.5 Validity and Survivability
The validity and survivability of the notion of Civilizational State depend on its ability to balance historical-cultural authenticity with the imperatives of modern governance and global interconnectedness. While the concept provides a provocative critique of the universality of Western-centric International Relations theories, its practical applications reveal significant limitations and risks.
For the Civilizational State to endure as a meaningful construct, it must evolve to accommodate pluralism within and beyond its borders, demonstrating both adaptability and relevance in a rapidly changing world order. ## 4. Hypothetical (Future) Typology of States
Rapidly shifting geopolitical dynamics, emergence of new technologies, and multiple other global factors may drastically change future state typologies. If we try to imagine a typology of state entities in some distant future, it may very well look like a mélange of nation-states, supranational entities, corporatist world orders, civilizational paradigms, technological and AI-driven orders, environmental and sustainability models, conflict-driven and fragmented models, religious or ideological paradigms, as well as of various hybrid frameworks.
- I. Nation-State Models (Existing and Evolving). Traditional sovereign entities may adapt or fragment in response to technological and cultural shifts. Examples include neo-isolationist states ("digital hermits") and federal superstates like a fully integrated European Union.

-  Federal Superstates (e.g., European Union, potentially an African Union)
-  Neo-Isolationist States (e.g., North Korea, hypothetical future "digital hermits")
- II. Supranational Entities. Scientific and technological advancements (including AI) and other developments may facilitate deeper integration among unions like ASEAN or enable global governance models such as a reformed United Nations.
-  Existing Unions with Greater Integration (e.g., ASEAN evolving into an EU-like entity)
-  Global Governance Models (e.g., a reformed United Nations, new Bretton Woods System)
-  Oceanic/Polar Regional Governance (e.g., Arctic Council as a full governing body)
- III. Corporatist World Orders. The dominance of tech giants could give rise to corporate-driven states or blockchain-based digital autonomous zones.
-  Corporate-Driven States (e.g., regions dominated by corporations like Amazon or SpaceX hypothetical "Marsgov")
-  Blockchain-based Digital Autonomous Zones (e.g., "Bitcoin Nations")
- IV. Civilizational Paradigms. Renewed emphasis on cultural identities may lead to Huntington-like civilizational blocs or reawakened indigenous systems.
-  Huntington’s Civilizational Blocks with modern twists (e.g., Islamic Caliphates 2.0, Confucian Renaissance)
-  Reawakened Indigenous Confederal Systems (e.g., pan-Amazonian networks or Arctic Indigenous leadership)
- V. Technological and AI-Driven Orders. AI-guided nations and virtual states could redefine governance, while space colonization introduces entirely new frameworks.

-  Ecological Alliance Nations (e.g., nations uniting under a "Green Belt and Road")
-  Oceanic/Underwater Settlements as Sovereign Entities
- VII. Conflict-Driven and Fragmented Models. Rising inequalities and regional conflicts may result in city-state resurgence or stateless zones.
-  City-State Resurgence (e.g., Singapore, future Hong Kong-like entities)

## 5. Integralistic Paradigm in International Relations Theory
In earlier works, the author of this article assessed influential IR theories, including Realist, Liberalist, and Civilizationist Economic Structuralist, Constructivist, English School, and Critical Theory approaches. The existing paradigms in IR Theory are increasingly inadequate for addressing the challenges of a globalized world. Issues such as geopolitical multipolarity, civilizational clashes, resource depletion, terrorism, and climate change create an urgent need for new theories and paradigms.
## 5.1. Premises of the Integralistic Paradigm
As demonstrated in the author's analysis and previous publications, many contemporary schools of thought in IR Theory suffer from one-sidedness and incompleteness. The core idea of the Integralistic Paradigm is that IR Theory must pivot to accommodate multi-actor systems.
The cross-pollination of ideas, for example, between Economic Structuralism and Civilizationism (e.g., Gramsci and Toynbee; Wallerstein and Braudel), Civilizationism and Constructivism (e.g., Sorokin and Wendt), and Realism and Civilizationism (e.g., Mearsheimer and Huntington) clearly illustrates Joseph Campbell’s observation that “Truth is one, the sages speak of it by many names.”
The Integralistic Paradigm seeks to incorporate Realism’s focus on the balance of power, Liberalism’s interest in democracy, human rights, international trade, and peace, Civilizationism’s attention to historical and cultural factors, Economic Structuralism’s emphasis on economic forces, Constructivism’s focus on the role of ideas, the English School’s efforts at synthesis, and Critical Theory’s concerns about dogmatism. The Integralistic Paradigm would allow for a more complete, comprehensive, and adequate analysis of the geopolitical, socioeconomic, and sociocultural intricacies of the rapidly globalizing world. The paradigm would integrate relevant and valid elements from various theoretical traditions into a coherent, holistic framework, offering both theoretical insights and practical applications for IR.
Tested models such as the Periodic Table of Elements and innovative applications such as AI systems could prove crucial in helping to integrate the IR Theory field and transform the study of international relations. Introducing the Integralistic Paradigm in International Relations Theory could enhance, synthesize, as well as ultimately integrate the field of International Relations. (Alalykin-Izvekov, 2023; AlalykinIzvekov, 2024).
5.2. Principles of Integralistic Paradigm The following principles define and buttress the Integralistic Paradigm.
Interdisciplinarity: Drawing on insights across theoretical traditions. Valid insights, theories, and perspectives from other paradigms are incorporated into the Integralistic Framework and applied to specific problems.
Systemic Integration: Viewing global dynamics as interconnected and value based. The paradigm’s systemic nature is represented as a set of interconnected elements, forming a value-based, integrated whole.
Scientific Rigor: Grounding the framework in empirical analysis. The paradigm is grounded in rational concepts, theories, and insights derived from social sciences.
Compatibility: Ensuring cohesion among diverse theoretical elements. The principle ensures compatibility of various ideas, concepts, and theories within the Integralistic framework, allowing for cohesion and balance among diverse elements.
Adaptability: Evolving in response to new global challenges. Incorporates mechanisms for self-assessment and adaptability, allowing the framework to evolve in response to new global challenges, as well as drawing from historical realism while anticipating and shaping future ideas and developments.
Predictability: Providing actionable insights into policy and forecasting. Ensures the paradigm is not only theoretically sound but also capable of practical, predictive insights that aid in policymaking and forecasting.

## Case Study One: Russo-Ukrainian War (2014–Present) Through a Prism of Realism
The Russo-Ukrainian War, spanning the period from the annexation of Crimea in 2014 to the ongoing large-scale conflict, presents a compelling case study for Realism in International Relations. Realism’s emphasis on state-centric power struggles, survival in an anarchic international system, and the pursuit of national interest offers a foundational framework for analyzing the war. However, as this case demonstrates, relying solely on Realism overlooks critical dimensions of the conflict, thereby limiting its explanatory and prescriptive capacity.
## Realist Interpretation
Realism posits that the international system is inherently anarchic, compelling states to prioritize security and power accumulation. From this perspective, Russia's actions in Ukraine can be understood as an attempt to counterbalance perceived Western encroachments, especially NATO's expansion.
Moscow’s annexation of Crimea and its support for separatists in Donetsk and Luhansk align with Realist predictions: Russia seeks to safeguard its strategic interests, including access to the Black Sea and maintenance of a buffer against Western influence.
Similarly, Ukraine's responses — whether through closer alignment with Western institutions like the European Union or military resistance — reflect its pursuit of sovereignty and national survival. The competing power dynamics between the West (NATO and EU) and Russia thus epitomize the Realist notion of a zero-sum game in international politics.
## One-Sidedness of Realism
While Realism highlights critical aspects of war, such as power struggles and security dilemmas, it fails to account for the multidimensional nature of the conflict. For instance, Realism largely neglects the role of ideology and identity, including the cultural, historical, and linguistic divides that have fueled tensions between Ukraine and Russia. The war is as much about competing visions of national identity — Ukraine’s European orientation versus Russia’s civilizational mission to protect the so-called Russian World (Русский мир) – as it is about geopolitical calculations.
Moreover, Realism underplays the agency of non-state actors and the influence of global norms.
The international community’s reaction, from sanctions to UN resolutions, reflects a normative framework that transcends the Realist focus on material power. Similarly, the role of civil society, from grassroots resistance in Ukraine to global advocacy networks, challenges Realism’s state-centric worldview.
Finally, Realism struggles to incorporate the economic and technological dimensions of the war. The interplay of energy politics, cyber warfare, and global economic interdependence significantly shapes the conflict’s trajectory, yet these factors are secondary in traditional Realist analysis.
## Case Study Two: Russo-Ukrainian War (2014–Present) Through a Prism of Liberalism
The Liberal School of International Relations emphasizes the role of institutions, cooperation, and democratic values in mitigating conflict and fostering stability. From a Liberal perspective, the Russo-Ukrainian War emerges as a stark challenge to the global liberal order, underpinned by norms of sovereignty, self-determination, and international law. However, while Liberalism sheds light on critical aspects of the conflict, its application alone is insufficient to grasp the war's full complexity.
## Liberalist Interpretation
At its core, Liberalism attributes the origins of the conflict to the clash between democratic and authoritarian governance models. Ukraine's post-2014 alignment with Western liberal democracies, following the Euromaidan protests, reflects its aspiration to join the European Union and NATO — bodies that Liberal Theory views as cornerstones of peace and cooperation. Conversely, Russia’s opposition is seen as a direct challenge to these liberal institutions and the values they represent.
From the Liberal perspective, the war underscores the importance of robust international institutions and norms. The Western response, characterized by sanctions, military aid to Ukraine, and diplomatic pressure on Russia, exemplifies the role of multilateral mechanisms in upholding the liberal order. Additionally, the rallying of democracies in support of Ukraine highlights the liberal expectation that shared values drive collective action.
## One-Sidedness of Liberalism
Despite its strengths, Liberalism offers a narrow interpretation of the Russo-Ukrainian War by overemphasizing institutional and normative dimensions while underestimating the power-centric realities and cultural underpinnings of the conflict.
-  Overreliance on Institutions and Norms. Liberalism assumes that adherence to international norms and the strengthening of institutions will deter aggression. However, Russia’s actions illustrate the limits of these mechanisms in the face of power politics. The United Nations, for example, has been largely ineffective in resolving the conflict, hamstrung by Russia’s veto power as a permanent Security Council member. This highlights the inadequacy of institutional responses when confronted with a determined great power.
-  Neglect of Power and Security Concerns. Liberalism underestimates the Realist motivations driving Russia’s behavior. Moscow’s actions are not solely a rejection of liberal norms but also a strategic effort to maintain its sphere of influence and its security buffer. Similarly, Ukraine’s turn to the West is partly driven by its existential need for security guarantees, reflecting priorities better explained by Realism than Liberalism.
-  Simplistic View of Identity and Ideology. Liberalism often views conflicts through the binary lens of democracy versus autocracy, overlooking the deeper cultural and historical factors at play. The war is also rooted in Russia’s civilizational narrative, which seeks to preserve its historical and cultural ties with Ukraine. By focusing on governance models, Liberalism misses these critical ideational drivers of the conflict.
-  Assumption of Universalistic Liberal Aspirations. Liberalism presumes that all states and societies inherently aspire to liberal democratic ideals. This perspective struggles to account for segments of Ukrainian society with ambivalent or oppositional views toward Western liberalization, as well as Russia’s domestic support for its anti-Western stance.
## Case Study Three: Russo-Ukrainian War (2014–Present) Through a Prism of the Integralistic Paradigm
In this Case Study, the Russo-Ukrainian War (2014-Present) is analyzed through a prism of the Integralistic Paradigm. The following analysis clearly demonstrates advantages of applying the Integralistic Paradigm in this analysis. Since the Integralistic Paradigm creatively employs most relevant aspects, angles, and methodology of all paradigms, it demonstrates clear advantages.
The Russo-Ukrainian War represents a multidimensional conflict that defies simplistic explanations offered by any single IR paradigm. By creatively synthesizing relevant elements from various theoretical approaches, the Integralistic Paradigm offers a robust framework for understanding the complexities of this war.
## Advantages of the Integralistic Paradigm
The Integralistic Paradigm excels in capturing the multifaceted nature of the RussoUkrainian War by incorporating insights from Realism, Liberalism, Constructivism, and the Civilizational Paradigm. Its integrative approach highlights the interplay of power, norms, identity, and culture in the conflict, offering a comprehensive analysis that single paradigms fail to achieve.
## Huntington’s Civilizational Paradigm in Context
Huntington’s clash of civilizations thesis provides a critical lens for understanding the broader civilizational dimensions of the Russo-Ukrainian War. Ukraine’s struggle is emblematic of its status as a “cleft country”, straddling the cultural and historical divides between the Western and Orthodox worlds (Huntington, 1996: 42). The war reflects a contest not only for territory but also for Ukraine’s civilizational identity whether it aligns with the liberal democratic values of the West, or with the Orthodox, autocratic model championed by Russia.
Huntington’s concept of the “core state” further elucidates Russia’s role in the conflict. As the core state of the Eastern-Orthodox civilization, Russia sees itself as the natural protector of Orthodox values and its associated sphere of influence. This geopolitical ambition drives its opposition to Ukraine’s westward trajectory, viewed as a threat to the structure of the Eastern-Orthodox civilization, and the stability of its civilizational core (Huntington, 1996: 135-139).
The Integralistic Paradigm also accounts for Ukraine’s condition as a torn country, divided by competing civilizational allegiances. This dynamic underscores the war’s ideological dimension, which transcends traditional power politics and requires a nuanced understanding of civilizational identity.
## Critique of the Civilizational State Notion and Its Derivatives
The Civilizational State concept and its derivatives — such as civilization-state, statecivilization, civilizational nation-state, civilizational privilege, geopolitical civilizational narrative – prove inadequate in explaining the Russo-Ukrainian War’s complexities. These notions often oversimplify civilizational dynamics by conflating state and civilization as static, monolithic entities. Those inadequacies may be listed as follows:
 Reductionism. The Civilizational State concept reduces civilizations to mere extensions of state power, ignoring the fluid and contested nature of civilizational identities.
Ukraine’s contested identity, with elements of both Western and Orthodox civilizations, demonstrates the fallacy of such reductionism.
-  Geopolitical Manipulation. The geopolitical civilizational narrative instrumentalizes civilizational rhetoric to mask and justify the pragmatic power politics driving Moscow’s strategy, as seen in its selective invocation of civilizational ties to legitimize territorial aggression. Contrary to the geopolitical civilizational narrative, Russia represents the core state of the Eastern-Orthodox civilization, not an autonomous civilization in and of itself (Huntington, 155-157, 162).
-  Failure to Address Internal Diversity. Civilizational State frameworks overlook internal pluralism within civilizations, assuming homogeneity where fragmentation often exists. The Russo-Ukrainian War highlights this flaw, as diverse voices within Ukraine contest both Western and Orthodox alignments.
## Integralistic Paradigm as a Scientific Framework
By combining Huntington’s civilizational insights with elements of other paradigms, the Integralistic Paradigm transcends these limitations.
-  From Realism, it incorporates the importance of power and security concerns.
-  From Liberalism, it recognizes the role of international norms and institutions.
-  From Constructivism, it validates the importance of identity, culture, and historical narratives.
## Conclusions

Initially, it was dominated by two paradigms — Realism and Liberalism. Later, other perspectives emerged, including but not limited to Economic Structuralism, The Critical Theory, The English School, Constructivism, and Civilizationism. Each school of thought offers valuable insights, however, each falls short due to one-sidedness. This results in a “different playbooks” dilemma, which current events in Eastern Europe, in the Middle East, and in the Far East clearly demonstrate.
 The protean notion of civilization has long been referenced by various political actors and shaped their agendas. The rise of the scientific Civilizational Paradigm within IR Theory reached its apogee with the arrival of seminal ideas by the American social scholar Samuel Huntington. That thinker developed scientific fundamentals of the paradigm, including its principles and essential terminology.
 Recently, many terminological hybrids have been presented to the field of IR Theory. These have included the civilizational state, civilization-state, statecivilization, civilizational nation-state, civilizational privilege, and geopolitical civilizational narratives. The archetypal prototype for these portmanteau terms is the notion of the Civilizational State. Positioned at the intersection of political science, history and cultural studies, the concept challenges the Westphalian state model, which has dominated global politics since the Treaty of Westphalia in 1648.
 If we look at an emerging (present and near future) typology of states, it is easy to see that it already represents a diversified matrix, with new entities rapidly coming forth from layers of the past systems. The European Union (EU) represents a compound of nation-states, ancient empires, and economic blocs, and China, Russia, Iran, Japan, Israel and some others are rapidly positioning themselves as civilizational states.
 Rapidly shifting geopolitical dynamics, the emergence of new technologies, and multiple other global factors may drastically change future state typologies. A future typology of state entities may look like a mélange of nation-states, supranational entities, corporatist world orders, civilizational paradigms, technological and AI-driven orders, environmental and sustainability models, conflict-driven and fragmented models, religious or ideological paradigms, as well as of various additional hybrid frameworks.
 The Integralistic Paradigm allows for a more complete, comprehensive, and adequate analysis of the geopolitical, socioeconomic, and sociocultural intricacies of the rapidly globalizing multi-polar and multi-civilizational world. The paradigm can integrate relevant and valid elements from various theoretical traditions into a coherent, holistic framework, offering both theoretical insights and practical applications for IR. Introducing the Integralistic Paradigm in International Relations Theory could enhance, synthesize, as well as, ultimately, integrate the field of International Relations.
