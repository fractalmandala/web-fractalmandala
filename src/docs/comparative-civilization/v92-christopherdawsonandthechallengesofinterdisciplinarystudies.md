Prof. Joseph T. Stuart, Professor of History Mary University, Bismarck, North Dakota
The following interview was conducted by Ms. Bibi Pelic, the Director of Communications for the International Society for the Comparative Study of Civilizations. The recording is available on the ISCSC YouTube channel:
https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=so-hS2SdoZc Pelic: Hi, Joseph. It is a pleasure to welcome you. Stuart: Thank you very much for having me.
Pelic: Today I would like to discuss with you several topics, including your book on Christopher Dawson. However, before we get into that, please tell us a bit about yourself.
Stuart: Sure. So, I grew up in the state of Michigan and studied history and religion and philosophy before heading off to graduate school in Scotland. I had the great joy of studying at the University of St. Andrews and also the University of Edinburgh in Scotland for the master’s and PhD. Then, I came back to United States in 2009. I started working at the University of Mary, here in Bismarck. I met my wife here and now we have four kids.
I am the head of History at this institution, which is such an honor. I enjoy and get to teach in classes on all kinds of different subjects, so it has been a wonderful journey this far in my career.
Pelic: Okay that is great. It is very impressive. Also, you are now actually a member of the International Society for the Comparative Study of Civilizations. Why did you join and how did you join?
Stuart: I am so pleased that this is such an unusual and unique society. I was very excited to discover it through one of your members, and its interdisciplinary nature, in particular. So, I am noticing in a recent issue an article by an anthropologist, another by a philosopher, another by a historian, obviously. So that is a wide breadth of fields. It is unusual to see this kind of situation in a peer-reviewed professional journal.
I love interdisciplinary work. The man I have studied and written about for twenty years, Christopher Dawson, was a very inter-disciplinary figure and so I am excited not only for that but also for the reason of the study of civilizations.
You know you have a lot of specialists who focus on distinct aspects of civilizations, but you do not have a lot of organizations looking at sort of some of the bigger questions, which bring innovative research — yes, specialized but also focused on big questions.
Pelic: Also, what made you turn to Christopher Dawson in the first place? Why is he interesting? Why should he be interesting for us, too?
Stuart: Let me show you his picture. So, this is him in 1930, as a young man. He was an English historian of culture. He is outstanding for developing the idea that religion and culture are the driving forces of history.
So, one of his most famous books is this one, called the Dynamics of World History. There are wonderful essays in this book, and they are presented in a very interdisciplinary way, about the importance of a society or a culture's religion — or its vision, whatever that might be.
It is significant in the way that it plays out in the various aspects of culture, from economics to society, and so forth. Then, it builds on that interaction between different cultures, the engines behind historical change, and the exchange of ideas. Dawson is interested in world history and civilization and culture in a broad way, and I was interested in history and the relationship between religion and culture, too.
So, he became a model for me to study and say — okay — here is a way to do it, not the only or even the best way but a way. So, how can I then implement this with my students and in my own research?
I have just found it to be tremendously fruitful and so this book is partly coming out of that fruitfulness of Dawson's approach.
Pelic: I was reading your brief review of it, and I was listening to your podcast with our member, Steven Satkiewicz. You mentioned that an important aspect of Christopher Dawson was that he experienced the First World War and that his thoughts and philosophies come from that moment.
Stuart: Yes. That is so. He was born in 1889, so he was around twenty-five when it started. You know he was a little older, but he you know he still could have been drafted, of course. But he was a little bit too sickly, so he was not part of the First World War in a physical or military sense, but he certainly was in an emotional and spiritual and intellectual sense, like everybody else was at that time in the early 20th century.
I guess one way that it really shaped him and others, including Arnold Toynbee, one of the founders of the International Society for the Comparative Study of Civilizations, is that it was the First World War that gave the lie to the nationalist interpretation of history.
| | |
|---|---|
Arnold Toynbee (left) and Christpher Dawson Both were influenced by the experience of World War I
Dawson wrote at one point: look, historians were partly to blame for the First World War because they had created the narratives that formed in everybody's minds about how history works: It was a story of nations in conflict with each other. That story influenced the minds of the diplomats and the politicians and the military leaders in 1914 to create an unbelievable civilizational tragedy.
Toynbee comes out of that thinking, okay, we need to focus on world history and civilizations. Dawson does, too, but just in a slightly different direction. We can get into this more in detail, but this different direction was — yes, civilizations, but to look at civilizations we need to go a step below and we need to engage with the anthropologists in the study of culture. We need to see various cultures that incorporate distinct cultural traditions, which combine with each other to make civilizations. That way we would see a slightly more appropriate empirical approach.
He thought Toynbee was a little bit too philosophical at some points. Dawson wanted to engage a little bit more with the anthropologists before touching the philosophers.
Pelic: This now makes me think of one basic question: What is civilization, according to you or maybe Christopher Dawson?
Stuart: This is great. Dawson wrote a book called The Age of the Gods. It is an ancient history, and it is his first book, published in 1928. It was widely reviewed internationally and was highly respected by archaeologists and historians.
He is writing about the beginnings of civilization in Mesopotamia, in Egypt and in Europe, and the coalescence of distinct factors ranging from engineering to agricultural techniques, to language, to the alphabet, and to a higher religion.
So, Dawson really sees civilization as a comprehensive kind of reality that includes multiple different cultural traditions, a certain self-awareness, an ability in food production, specialized kinds of positions like the priesthood and the educated classes, and the scribes who can preserve knowledge.
Civilization is different from culture. Every human society in the world has a culture; not every human society has a civilization.
One of the societies that I have studied is the Mandan Indians of my part of the world on the Missouri river in North Dakota.
They have a beautiful, rich, incredible culture but they did not quite rise to the level of civilization. Almost. They had settled existence; they even had some specialization, but they did not really have a written language, with an alphabet, nor with monumental architecture. So, it is more of a proto-civilization. That is by no means any kind of judgment on them. It is just a question of looking at the several stratifications that different human societies reach.
I think that would do be Dawson's kind of approach.
Pelic: Talking now about the Mandans, you mention in your review that they are an example of a river civilization.
Stuart: Yes. It was a sort of proto-civilization because the Missouri River here in North Dakota provided similar environmental benefits as did the Nile in Egypt or the Tigris and Euphrates or the Yellow River. That is in a sense that there was good soil along the river, and several kinds of irrigation were possible.
Here, particularly important on the Plains, is protection from the wind. So, you have a river valley, and you farm and live in the valley, while the wind blows over the top of you; we get a lot of wind here in North Dakota. And there is transportation, plus of course, defense against the Sioux further east. There are lots of reasons that this amazing, longest river in North America, the Missouri River, played a role in the life of this people that is analogous to that of other river civilizations in the world.
Pelic: What you also write in your review is how today even though the Mandan are, well, not exactly extinct, indeed there are those who carry the blood of the Mandan, there are, however, values that have been continued, passed on, to people who now live there. You mentioned stability and generosity. How does that happen that values from one culture pass on to another?
Stuart: That is an amazing question. I'm interested in the Mandan culture. It is like a case study of Christopher Dawson's wider understanding of culture. It is one of the ways that I am trying to illustrate some of Dawson's basic points.
So, there are two ways to look at this. First is the specific observation that you brought out about the Mandan and their values of stability, of agriculture. These are values very much of my state of North Dakota, among the wider Euro-American population that has encountered this Native American population.
It is a harsh environment here. To survive you must be a community. You must work together; you cannot leave the person who is stranded on the road, who has broken down along the freeway. You must pick them up, you must help them. When there's flooding on the Red River or the Missouri River, you are forced to come together as a community to help people. It is agriculture and stability.
I'm at a Benedictine university. The Benedictines are known for their stability. This is an interesting sort of cross-cultural kind of resonance that has happened here in this part of the world with the Native American and the Euro-American cultures.
Now to your other point: how does this happen? Well, the way Dawson looks at culture is as something interdisciplinary.
Every culture has a vision at the top of ideas, beliefs and assumptions, and that vision is worked out in the ways of life of the people. There are folkways, in particular, there are workways and there are place-ways.
Thus, Dawson's vision of culture is four-fold. You have an “I” at the top for ideas and then you have an “F” for folk, a “W” for work and a “P” for place. For Dawson it is a paradigm of culture. Every culture has it. The Mandan have an IFWP, the Euro Americans have an IFWP, and the Aboriginal Australians have an IFWP.
There is a local reality in history, but what is interesting about our humanity is that a culture is not simply something that is local. It has an ability to transcend its local environment, even at a material level. So, if you
take horses or tools or guns and you take them out of the Spanish culture in Mexico and you move them up the Missouri River and you begin trading with the Mandans, these physical artifacts, that is transcultural. It changes their way of life. They adapt to the new reality of guns and horses about 1750.
This is even more true when it comes to ideas of a people that can spread through books, through general contact, through missionaries, through whoever, through schools. You see this all the way back to the ancient Greeks and the texts of Plato that are still widely read, at least were read widely in not just the West but also the Islamic East. You can see the power of ideas to transcend culture.
This is why Dawson would critique a figure like Oswald Spengler, one of the other great world historians, who was a little bit older, part of a previous generation. Spengler had this idea that all cultures are their own independent entities that rise and fall, like the flowers. You know, they are beautiful and then they die out, and then the next one comes along.
Dawson would say yes to a point; cultures do rise and fall. There is an organic natural rhythm in human life, but there is more than the organic. There is also the transcultural; there is also this intellectual and spiritual element that can transcend culture. This means that a culture is not simply relative to itself but rather, cultures come into contact, engage with each other, on higher questions of truth and meaning.
That means that Spengler’s vision was a little bit narrow. Ultimately, the cultural relativism of Spengler was not only wrong, but it was also dangerous. It fed directly into the kind of Nietzschean superman mentality of the 1920s in Germany.
Pelic: That is very interesting. Christopher Dawson mentions about this set of four, ideas and so forth, IFWP; as I understand it, if one of these four is changed, then a whole new process starts happening. How could we then apply it? Once I read this I was thinking, okay, how can I mirror it to what is happening where I live, and then apply it to what is happening on a global level?
Stuart: Yes. Let me see if I can share a screen here. These are the four elements of culture: here is a diagram of what Dawson is talking about. Culture is on the left side and then ideas, folk, work, and place.
So, these are his four elements of any culture. His claim is that these are universal categories that we can use for comparative study between cultures or even civilizations. There is an aspect of community that is resident in each of these four: thought and belief, people, and their folkways, workways, technology and then the location or the natural environment.
So, what is interesting about this is that this is an immediate recipe for interdisciplinary study. Because if you are going to understand the culture as a whole, immediately we say okay, what are the disciplines that would deal with the ideas aspect of culture? It is theology, philosophy, intellectual history, psychology, language study, all those kinds of fields would be relevant to that study.
What about folkways? Well, sociology, social history, anthropology, economics, geography, and even geology and climate studies and Environmental Studies. If you are going to understand a culture, you do not have to be an expert on all of those, but you need to have the humility to be interested in those other fields, talk to people who are experts. Then, in your own writing and thinking and speaking, figure out ways to weave together those different insights to give a sense of the culture as a whole. That is hard because we want to stick in our safe places, in our disciplines.
Dawson's vision is particularly challenging here: Go beyond and make friends with other people in societies like this one, where you can profit from each other's expertise.
Then in the third column are historical examples of each of these. We look at how these can work in culture; we can look at education, folkways, marriage rites, feudalism, and guilds of workers.
Place — one of the most fascinating examples I have recently learned about are the Ethiopian Church forests which are about a thousand years old. There are churches dotted across Northern Ethiopia that are surrounded by trees that have not been cut down because they are considered sacred with the church. It is just beautiful. We can see YouTube videos on these; they are just amazing. So that is a relationship between the vision of that culture and its geography, its environmental location.
So, if I were instructing the students on my whiteboard in classroom, I would draw an arrow from “I” down to the “P” to show the influence between those two things and what you are asking about, that is, change. This dynamic element of culture — what happens is one of these elements, something will happen in history that will change it, and then that shapes the rest of the culture.
The Mandan example I used of the horses and the guns would shape the “F” and the “W” elements of the culture. Then that shapes the way they relate to place through hunting buffalo and even interestingly shape their world view in the sacredness of the horse, in their gratitude to God for the horses. So, that is an example of material change affecting the whole culture.
You can also see an even more powerful kind of change from the spiritual, intellectual level, so the ideas of a culture or even of a civilization can be changed. For example, Muhammad rises in Arabia and his new vision unites all the different warring and violent tribes of the Arabian Peninsula into this new culture. That then forms a new civilization, one that happens through the “I” shaping the folkways of life, the workways of life, and even the interaction with place and architecture, aspects of Islamic civilization as a unified whole.
Not only does this model of culture describe culture at any one point in history, like a synchronic view, but it also helps us understand change through time or a diachronic view of culture.
Pelic: Very interesting. This suggests a thought by Hendrik Willem van Loon. He was a journalist, but he was also a historian. He said that more than the facts, it is more important to feel history. What you were now talking about recalls that sometimes it is even more important to feel history then it is to know the facts. It is important to know, but feeling then really helps us understand culture and ultimately civilization.
Stuart: I think that is right. One of my favorite quotations about history comes from the book The Historian's Craft, by Marc Bloch. He was walking and talking with one of his friends at a conference. The quotation was from Henri Pirenne, a friend of Bloch. And Pirenne said, “If I were an antiquarian, I would only love old things. But I am a historian, and therefore, I love life.”
I think he was trying to indicate exactly what you said about a feeling, an emotion, and allowing one's ability to empathize with people in the past to be nourished by one's empathy and participation in life in the present.
For life in the present is what alerts you to the different human conditions and factors which, yes, were different in the past; nonetheless, if you do not have an emotional connection to them, it is difficult to enter in a meaningful way into the past.
Pelic: True, and here comes now the key word — interdisciplinary. I gather that you cannot understand Dawson if you do not understand what interdisciplinary thinking is. And, further, perhaps, one of the prerequisites to understanding Dawson is also, maybe not exactly empathy, but perhaps understanding emotion and feeling. Somehow I do feel that they are correlated a bit. What do you think?
Stuart: Let me first say that, yes, the interdisciplinary is essential to Dawson. Again, this is related to his post-World War I environment. What happened in the Great War, as with a lot of major events, is that you must survive it, you must get through it, so you focus all your efforts on one thing.
If you were French, that one thing was killing Germans; if you were German, it was killing the French. You had to focus on that, since your whole society was geared toward that one thing.
It is a vast simplification.
Dawson saw this as a feature of modern thought. Often this reductionism, this kind of simplification, whether in Marx a simplification to simply the “W” factor in culture or economics, or in various nationalist historians who simplified everything to place, to the nation as the explanation of the whole.
No. Dawson would say, no, you have IFWP. You cannot just simplify it to one of those elements. Even the “I” — Hegel did that. That is not human life. That is not history. It is not just about ideas, either. Practically, when people begin simplifying like this, Dawson called it the lust for simplification, it affects politics. You have the Treaty of Versailles, for example, which blamed everything on Germany. That was a simplification that was disastrous. It set us up for World War II.
So, this problem of simplification is not only academic; it is very practical. The way around it is to teach people what civilization is, what the cultures are that make up a civilization, and how to really understand that object of knowledge is to do it through different disciplines.
You do that through friendships and alliances between different people who have diverse backgrounds and expertise, so that you can learn from each other and understand that whole better. That is a good model, it seems to me, for any kind of organization, let alone politics.
Pelic: Do you think that could be one of our main suggestions for today's world? Stuart: I do, yes, I do. I think that and that is one reason I love studying Dawson, because he is a constant challenge and reminder of this importance.
But while Dawson was widely reviewed and respected, other academic historians often rejected him precisely because he was interdisciplinary. To them, history is history. So, Dawson did not have a normal academic career. He taught at the University of Essex in Southwest England for a while in his twenties and then he ended his career at Harvard for a number of years, but in between he was largely excluded from the British academic world. I think that is why there is a lack of sympathy for the interdisciplinary approach.
If you look at it, and you look at the past, you can conclude that a lot of those academic historians, their books are no longer read. They are forgotten. Almost all of Dawson's books are still in print, which is a remarkable fact for a historian who has been dead more than 50 years.
Pelic: Do you think interdisciplinary thinking is on the rise today or is it still struggling as in the case of Christopher Dawson?
Stuart: We reached a low point in interdisciplinary thinking in the mid- and late 20th century when people were deeply invested in a kind of scientific specialization. I think the postmodern age has helped us a little bit by loosening the grip that the empiricist mentality has had on us; this is allowing us to bring in the importance of imagination once again, creativity, even mythology, in shaping the narratives that then shape society.
People are realizing, I think, that unless we understand those narratives and these bigger pictures that people are interested in, we are going to really struggle to make sense of our world. So, I am hopeful. I think there is a lot of work to do yet. There needs to be a lot more members of the International Society for the Comparative Study of Civilizations. There needs to be new societies in different fields and various places.
But, yes, I have seen signs since the 1990s of increased interest in interdisciplinary work, and that is one reason that Dawson's books have been reprinted starting in 2001. They are being reprinted, republished with nice introductions, and in wonderful scholarly editions.
I think that is one reason they are being reprinted and are selling internationally; in Brazil, Spain, other parts of Europe, as well as the United States, Dawson is being widely read today.
Pelic: Well, that is great. Perhaps that means that there is still hope for our world and for understanding. Recently I was reading how interdisciplinary thinking is important for creativity. You reminded me of this when you mentioned imagination, because if there is no imagination, if you cannot think in more directions, how can society, our cultures and civilizations worldwide move forward?
Stuart: Absolutely, that is well put. Dawson is a beautiful writer. He grew up on the border between England and Wales in a twelfth-century castle where he was born, which is now a used bookstore. His mother was of Welsh ancestry, and she was deeply interested in Welsh mythology, literature, and the saints. He talks in his memoirs about the significant role that imagination played in his upbringing and his thinking about history.
One reason I appreciate Dawson is because he did have an almost postmodern sense about him in literary imagination, in the construction of history, being really important. Still, he also went to Oxford and was trained in the methods of historical study and research, and he brought those two worlds together. He made history both an art and a science and that is really one of the keys here to his longevity.
Pelic: Oh, that is great — Art and Science together. I have never thought of that. What a great takeaway. Well, we have now had in this talk a number of takeaways for our audience. So where is your book available?
Stuart: It is available on amazon.com and at the Catholic University of America Press. The title is Christopher Dawson. It is an attempt to look not so much at his biography but at his intellectual trajectory. I call it his cultural mind in that it was shaped not just by the study of history but also of anthropology, sociology, and comparative religion.
The first part of the book is looking at how those disciplines shaped his cultural mind and then the second part of the book is how he applied that cultural mind to the fields of politics and education. So, I would call it more a biographical study, I suppose, than a biography.
It is also a look at the wider cultural history of the early 20th century as well. Pelic: Are you planning some further book on Christopher Dawson, or on some part of his work, or writing something completely different?
Stuart: I am not sure. I am working on a few projects on Dawson, little ones right now. There was an essay I discovered that was unpublished, that he wrote in manuscript form, so I have transcribed that with some student help. That is being published shortly in the Chesterton Review.
But you know, Bibi, what I have often thought is that we do not necessarily need more studies of Dawson. We need people doing what Dawson did.
So, I have been experimenting in another book with the following: what would a Dawsonian cultural kind of approach be, one that takes religion and the material factors of life seriously, what would that look like if we addressed, say, the 18th century and the Age of Enlightenment? What if we did that in a way which was taking advantage of recent scholarship, but also this big civilizational view?
I did that in a book entitled Re-thinking the Enlightenment: Faith in the Age of Reason, as an attempt to try to show what it could look like to do Dawson in a different field, in an area that he did not necessarily write about directly.
What particularly interests me going forward is undertaking a kind of cultural and civilizational scholarship inspired by Dawson but doing it in a new way, oriented toward the future of scholarship.
Pelic: Essentially applying the process. That is what you did in the 360 Review, where you wrote At the Heart of the World: Reflections on Mandan History and Culture, basically applying the Dawsonian method from ancient civilizations to today.
